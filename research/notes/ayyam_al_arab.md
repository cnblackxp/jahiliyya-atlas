# Ayyām al-ʿArab (أيام العرب) — Chronology, Geography, and Narrative Detail

**Scope note / epistemic warning for the report-writer:** Several high-value sources I was asked to consult were not reachable in this session: Brill's *Encyclopaedia of Islam* (2nd/3rd eds.) entries sit behind `referenceworks.brill.com` paywalls, `iranicaonline.org/articles/dhu-qar` returned a client error, and the Arabic Wikipedia articles `أيام العرب`, `أيام العرب في الجاهلية` and `حرب داحس والغبراء` all returned URL-not-found via my fetch tool (the Arabic-script URLs may need different encoding). I therefore could **not** independently verify the day-by-day lists inside the Basūs and Dāḥis wars (Yawm al-Nahy, al-Dhanāʾib, Wāridāt, Qiḍḍa, Taḥlāq al-Limam, etc.) from a citable source. Those are flagged as Gaps rather than reported from memory. Where I report a date or a name below, it carries a URL.

A second, structural warning: **almost every date in this field is a back-calculation, not a dated record.** The one near-exception is Yawm Ḥalīma, which can be tied to a Greek-source battle of June 554 CE. Everything else rests on Arabic genealogical reckoning (counting generations back from the Hijra or from the Year of the Elephant) and on 19th–20th-century European attempts to turn that reckoning into absolute years. Treat the CE dates as *scholarly conventions*, not evidence.

## Key Question 1: War of al-Basūs (حرب البسوس) — Bakr vs. Taghlib

### Takeaway
The traditional frame is a forty-year war between Bakr and Taghlib, c. 494–534/540 CE, triggered by Kulayb ibn Rabīʿa's killing of a she-camel belonging to a woman named al-Basūs. Modern scholarship treats it as a **composite narrative** — a bundle of separate raid-stories retro-fitted into one long war — and the sources disagree among themselves on sequence and participants.

### Cited Findings
- Dates given: **c. 494–540 CE** in the English Wikipedia infobox; the body also reports the war had already ended by the 530s — [Basus War, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- The user's brief cites a traditional c. 494–534 CE; the Dāḥis article independently glosses the Basūs War as **c. 494–534 CE**, so both end-dates (534 and 540) circulate in popular reference material — [Dahis and al-Ghabra, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- Traditional account: a forty-year war, with fighting **renewed about forty years after the first phase**, led by a younger generation, ending in a final Bakr victory — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- "Modern scholarship treats the war as a composite narrative, not a single prolonged conflict, and the sources disagree on its sequence and participants" — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- Tribes: **Taghlib** vs. **Bakr** (بكر), the latter including the **Shaybān** (شيبان) branch — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- **Kulayb ibn Rabīʿa** (كليب بن ربيعة), leading Taghlib figure, killed al-Basūs's camel — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- **Jassās ibn Murra** (جساس بن مرة) of Shaybān killed Kulayb after al-Basūs's lament — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- **Abū Laylā al-Muhalhil** (أبو ليلى المهلهل) — the leading Taghlib commander. He fled, was captured by a **Madhḥijī** tribe in South Arabia, and was forced to marry a woman of that tribe — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- **ʿAdī ibn Murra** (ʿUday ibn Murrah), a Bakrī chief, rebuked and recalled the Bakr subdivisions that had withdrawn from the war — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- Named days *actually attested in the source I could reach*: **Yawm al-Ḥazr** (Taghlib attacked Shaybān and won, most casualties on the Shaybān side) and **Yawm ʿalā Istirād** (one of Bakr's first victories over Taghlib) — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- Geography: the infobox gives the region of **Najd** (نجد), with associated unrest in **South Arabia**; other places named are the territory of **Zubayd**, and **Iraq**, where the Taghlib survivors dispersed and remained — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- Course: Taghlib struck Shaybān at Yawm al-Ḥazr, then confronted Zubayd a few days later with no reported casualties; the whole of Bakr gradually joined against Taghlib; Bakr withdrew to regenerate, returned ~40 years later, and won — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- A Ḥimyarite dimension: **Marthadʾilān Yunʿim**, the Ḥimyarite ruler, sent at least 500 soldiers to help **Imruʾ al-Qays** reunite Taghlib and Bakr against Banū Asad — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- Poetry: the **lament of al-Basūs**, presented poetically, is what provoked Jassās to kill Kulayb; the source states poetry from the war is still transmitted today, but reproduces none — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- Afterlife: the war is cited as a proverb/aphorism against grudges, family feuds, vendettas and violent rivalries — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)

### Inferences
- The ~40-year duration and the "renewed a generation later" structure are the tell-tale shape of **oral genealogical reckoning**, not a campaign record: the figure forty is a conventional round number in Arabic narrative, and the "younger generation returns" motif does narrative work. A VN can use the forty years freely as *tradition* but should not present it as dated history.
- The dispersal of Taghlib "into Iraq" is almost certainly the narrative's way of explaining a **later, historically attested** fact — Taghlib's presence in the Jazīra/lower Iraq as a Christianised, Lakhmid- and later Sasanian-adjacent tribe. That is an etiological ending, not an event.
- The Ḥimyarite/Imruʾ al-Qays material sits awkwardly inside a Bakr–Taghlib feud and looks like a separate tradition welded on — consistent with the "composite narrative" verdict.

### Gaps
- **Al-Muhalhil as ʿAdī ibn Rabīʿa** (عدي بن ربيعة): the source I reached names only "Abū Laylā al-Muhalhil" and does **not** give the equation with ʿAdī ibn Rabīʿa, nor state that he was Kulayb's brother. Needs a citable source.
- **Al-Ḥārith ibn ʿUbād** (الحارث بن عباد) — absent from every source I could reach. His famous role (the aged non-combatant who enters the war after his son Bujayr is killed, and the verse-refrain associated with him) is unverified here.
- **Jalīla** (جليلة bint Murra, Kulayb's wife and Jassās's sister) — absent from the sources I reached. Her lament/dilemma is a major dramatic asset for a VN but I cannot source it.
- **Al-Basūs's own name and lineage** (al-Basūs bint Munqidh, بسوس بنت منقذ) — not confirmed in any source I reached; only "a woman named Basus" / "her relation to Jassas is noted."
- **The named days**: Yawm al-Nahy (يوم النهي), Yawm al-Dhanāʾib (يوم الذنائب), Yawm Wāridāt (يوم واردات), Yawm Qiḍḍa (يوم قضة), Yawm Taḥlāq al-Limam (يوم تحلاق اللمم) — **none** of these appear in the sources I could reach, and I will not supply their locations or sequence from memory. The place to get them is al-Maydānī's *Majmaʿ al-amthāl* ch. 29 (see Q8) or Jād al-Mawlā's *Ayyām al-ʿArab fī al-Jāhiliyya*.

## Key Question 2: War of Dāḥis wa-l-Ghabrāʾ (حرب داحس والغبراء) — ʿAbs vs. Dhubyān

### Takeaway
A roughly forty-year feud between ʿAbs and Dhubyān, conventionally dated **c. 568–609 CE** (the dating is Sir William Muir's, i.e. a 19th-century European reconstruction, not a source date), set off by a rigged horse-race between the mares Dāḥis and al-Ghabrāʾ, and ended by two outsiders paying the blood-money for both sides.

### Cited Findings
- Dating: "c. 568–609 CE **by Sir William Muir**"; the article places the war in the 6th century, a few years after the Basūs War — [Dahis and al-Ghabra, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- Duration: about forty years — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- Location given: **al-Yamāma** (اليمامة) — central-eastern Najd, the region around modern **Riyadh / al-Kharj**, Saudi Arabia — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- Origin: **Qays ibn Zuhayr** (قيس بن زهير), chief of **Banū ʿAbs** (بنو عبس), and **Ḥudhayfa ibn Badr** (حذيفة بن بدر), chief of **Banū Dhubyān** (بنو ذبيان), organised a horse race. Qays entered **Dāḥis** (داحس); Ḥudhayfa entered **al-Ghabrāʾ** (الغبراء). Stake: at least a hundred camels from the losing tribe — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- The rigging: **Dhubyān men distracted Dāḥis during the race**, so al-Ghabrāʾ won. ʿAbs demanded the hundred camels; Ḥudhayfa refused; the dispute turned violent — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- Reciprocal killings: **Qays's brother** was stabbed to death; **Ḥudhayfa's brother** was stabbed to death. Ḥudhayfa himself died in the fighting — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- **ʿAntara ibn Shaddād** (عنترة بن شداد), the pre-Islamic poet, fought for Banū ʿAbs and **died in the fighting** — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- A Lakhmid economic thread: some of Banū ʿAbs stayed out of the fighting in order to **guard Lakhmid caravans** passing through al-Yamāma, promised rewards by **al-Nuʿmān III ibn al-Mundhir**; Dhubyānī envy of this escalated the conflict — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- Wider coalition: **Hawāzin** (هوازن) and **Ṭayyʾ** (طيء) supported Banū Dhubyān — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- Peacemaking: two merchants, **al-Ḥārith (ibn ʿAwf)** and **Ibn Sinān**, neutral men from neighbouring tribes, offered to pay the blood money for both tribes and for the slain chiefs; a truce followed and the war ended — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- Casualties: more than a hundred dead, including Ḥudhayfa ibn Badr and ʿAntara ibn Shaddād — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- Qays ibn Zuhayr appears again, independently, as the ʿAbs commander at **Shiʿb Jabala**, where he deprived his men's camels of water so they would charge toward the water — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- Dedicated biographical stubs exist for the principals: [Qays ibn Zuhayr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qays_ibn_Zuhayr), [Al-Harith ibn Awf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Awf), [Ibn Sinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Sinan)

### Inferences
- The 568–609 bracket should be labelled **"Muir's reconstruction"** in any timeline, not "the traditional date." It is a Victorian synthesis of Arabic generational data. The war's *end* around 608/609 is partly fixed by the need to place it before the Hijra-era generation and before al-Nuʿmān III's deposition (602), which is itself a datable anchor — so the later end is better constrained than the earlier.
- The Lakhmid-caravan motif makes the Dāḥis war chronologically **entangled with al-Nuʿmān III (r. 582–602)**. If that detail is authentic, the war's later phase cannot predate c. 582, which squeezes the "forty years" badly. This is a genuine internal tension worth showing the user.
- The claim that **ʿAntara died in this war** conflicts with other traditions in which ʿAntara survives into old age and is killed by an archer of Ṭayyʾ on a raid. Flag as contested; I could not source the alternative version here.
- Qays ibn Zuhayr appearing as a commander at both Dāḥis and Shiʿb Jabala is a useful *relative* chronological link: it puts the two episodes within one man's fighting life, which is the kind of anchoring the ayyām corpus actually supplies (relative, generational) as opposed to absolute years.

### Gaps
- **Named days within the war** (Yawm al-Mureiqib, Yawm al-Yaʿmuriyyāt, Yawm Dhāt al-Riqāʿ, Yawm al-Farūq, Yawm al-Hubāʾa, etc.) — the Wikipedia article explicitly names **no** battle-days. Unverified; needs al-Aghānī or Jād al-Mawlā.
- **Harim ibn Sinān** (هرم بن سنان): the source names only "Ibn Sinān," not "Harim." The identification is standard in Arabic literature but is not confirmed by anything I reached.
- **Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā's Muʿallaqa** (معلقة زهير بن أبي سلمى) celebrating the two peacemakers — the Dāḥis article does **not** mention it. This is a well-known association in Arabic literary tradition (the Muʿallaqa's closing gnomic section praises the men who bought peace with their own wealth) but I could not source it in this session. The user should verify against an edition of the Muʿallaqāt before building a scene on it.
- The relationship between **Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā** (the poet) and **Qays ibn Zuhayr** (the ʿAbs chief) — these are different men; the sources I reached do not clarify, and the name-collision is a real trap for a writer.

## Key Question 3: Yawm Dhī Qār (يوم ذي قار) — Bakr ibn Wāʾil vs. the Sasanians

### Takeaway
The best-dated of the great ayyām and the only one with a non-Arabic source trail. Muslim tradition spreads it anywhere from **602 to 624 CE**; modern scholarship (per *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, as reported) narrows it to **604–611 CE**. Its scale is genuinely disputed — from "turning point in Arab–Persian relations" down to "possibly no more than a skirmish."

### Cited Findings
- "Muslim traditions place the battle anywhere from **602 to 624 AD**" — [Battle of Dhi Qar, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- "Modern scholars, per *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, have narrowed this to **604–611 AD**" — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- Arab side: the **Bakr ibn Wāʾil** (بكر بن وائل) confederation, territory running from south-western Iraq into eastern Arabia. Most prominent constituent: **Shaybān** (شيبان). Others named: **Banū ʿIjl, Banū Dhuhl, Banū Qays ibn Thaʿlaba, Banū Taym-Allāh ibn Thaʿlaba, Banū Yashkur** — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- Sasanian side: Sasanian forces **plus Arab auxiliaries**, including **Banū Ṭayyʾ, Banū Taghlib, Banū Iyād**. The Ṭayyʾ leader **Iyās ibn Qabīṣa** had been appointed governor of the area — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- Numbers in traditional Arabic sources: **2,000 Persian** troops and **3,000 Arab auxiliaries** — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- Named leaders, pro-Sasanian: **Hāmarz / Hamrez al-Tasatturī, al-Nuʿmān ibn Zarʿa, Khālid ibn Yazīd al-Buhrānī, Khanābarīn** — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- Named leaders, Arab side: **Ḥanẓala ibn Thaʿlaba al-ʿIjlī, ʿAbd ʿAmr ibn Bashar al-Ḍubaʿī, Jabala ibn Bāʿith al-Yashkurī, al-Ḥārith ibn Waʿla al-Dhuhlī, al-Ḥārith ibn Rabīʿa al-Taymī** — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- **Per Landau-Tasseron, leadership among the Bakr shifted among various warriors, with no single commander-in-chief** — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- **The al-Nuʿmān III question:** he was **not** killed in the battle. He was **deposed in 602**, seized at **Ctesiphon**, imprisoned at **Khānaqīn**, and **died of plague** shortly before the rise of Islam — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- Abū ʿUbayda (Maʿmar ibn al-Muthannā)'s version of the cause: Khosrow II deposed al-Nuʿmān after he refused to marry the emperor's daughter and insulted Persian women; Khosrow then demanded al-Nuʿmān's family and armour from the Shaybān leader, who refused — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- **Competing causal account:** Ibn al-Kalbī (d. 819) attributes the conflict to **Bakrī raids after the deposition** and to the later Persian imprisonment of the Shaybānī leader, **rather than** to the al-Nuʿmān marriage/honour dispute — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- Source criticism: Ibn al-Kalbī's and Abū ʿUbayda's versions differ; **Abū ʿUbayda's is considered more fantastical and less reliable** — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- Location: **Southern Iraq, in the Sawād** (السواد). The article gives **no** specific site and does **not** mention al-Kūfa — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- Significance: remembered as a turning point in Arab–Persian relations and a blow to Persian control over Eastern Arabia; sometimes read as part of a prolonged Arab rebellion culminating in the early Muslim conquests — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- **Deflationary reading:** "It may have been no more than a skirmish"; **Rezakhani** is named as a historian taking this view — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- **Persian sources do not mention the conflict** — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- Textual tradition: the longest version is **Bishr ibn Marwān al-Asadī, *Ḥarb Banī Shaybān maʿa Kisrā Ānūshirwān*** (described as not necessarily representative); **al-Ṭabarī's *Taʾrīkh*** has the classical account; the **Khuzistan Chronicle** is earlier — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- An Arabic monograph on the day exists and is catalogued as *Ayyām al-ʿArab 1: Yawm Dhī Qār* — [abjjad listing](https://www.abjjad.com/book/2787770376/ايام-العرب-1-يوم-ذي-قار/2788818952)

### Inferences
- The user's premise that Dhī Qār "followed the killing of al-Nuʿmān III" is **wrong in its detail**: the tradition has him deposed and dying in prison of plague, not killed in or just before the battle. A VN can keep the dramatic logic (the Lakhmid king destroyed by Khosrow, his armour and family demanded, Shaybān refusing) while getting the mechanism right.
- The **602 deposition is the hard lower bound**: every account chains the battle to the aftermath of al-Nuʿmān's fall. That is why modern dating clusters 604–611 and why the tradition's upper figures (to 624) are untenable — 624 would put it after the Hijra, which collides with the "pre-Islamic" framing.
- The absence of the event from Persian sources plus Rezakhani's skirmish reading means **Dhī Qār's famous significance is largely retrospective Arab memory** — it became the day on which Arabs first beat the Persians because later Arab-Islamic historiography needed such a day. Dramatically that is *more* interesting, not less: the user can stage a small, confused fight that later becomes legend.
- "Dhū Qār" is traditionally a watering-place in the Iraqi desert west of the Euphrates, in the hinterland of what later became Kūfa/Dhī Qār governorate; the modern Iraqi governorate **Dhī Qār** (محافظة ذي قار, capital al-Nāṣiriyya, ~31.0°N 46.3°E) is named for it. **This localisation is my inference from the governorate name, not sourced** — the article declines to give a site.

### Gaps
- No precise coordinates or identified site for Dhū Qār in any source I reached.
- The *Encyclopaedia Iranica* article itself was unreachable (client error on `iranicaonline.org/articles/dhu-qar`); the 604–611 range is reported **at second hand** via Wikipedia's citation of it.
- No poetry text or named poet of Dhī Qār obtained (the day is traditionally associated with boasting verse by al-Aʿshā; unverified here).

## Key Question 4: The Fijār wars (حرب الفجار) and Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl

### Takeaway
A late-6th-century cycle of **eight fighting days over about four years** between the Quraysh–Kinana bloc and the Qays/Hawāzin bloc, fought largely at and around the **ʿUkāẓ** fair between Mecca and Ṭāʾif, and notorious because the fighting violated the sacred months — hence the name (*fijār*, "sacrilege"). Sources consistently report the young Muhammad present; his reported age varies 14–20. **Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl is not connected to the Fijār in any source I reached.**

### Cited Findings
- Date: **late 6th century**; the article gives no specific calendar years — [Fijar Wars, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Duration and structure: fighting lasted about **four years** and occurred on **eight days** in total — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- "Battles took place during the **sacred months**, when warfare was normally prohibited" — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Sides: **Quraysh and Kinana** (with **Ḥarb ibn Umayya**; **ʿAbd Allāh ibn Judʿān** of Banū Taym supplied armour) vs. **Qays** (excluding Ghaṭafān), including **Hawāzin, Banū Thaqīf, Banū ʿĀmir, Banū Sulaym**. **Abū Barāʾ** led the Banū ʿĀmir/Hawāzin side — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Described as a war between "two great confederations including townsfolk of **Mecca and Ṭāʾif**" — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- **Kilāb** and **Kaʿb** (branches of Banū ʿĀmir) were in the **Ḥums** pact with Quraysh and limited their role to the day of Nakhla — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Named days, in order:
  - **First three days** — brawls, sometimes counted as one war and sometimes as three
  - **Yawm Nakhla** (يوم نخلة), near Nakhla: Hawāzin attacked Quraysh and Kinana, who escaped to the **Ḥaram** at nightfall; usually counted the fourth day
  - **Yawm Shamṭa** (يوم شمطة / Shamza), near ʿUkāẓ: Hawāzin won, the following year
  - **Yawm al-ʿAblāʾ** (يوم العبلاء), at ʿUkāẓ: Hawāzin won
  - **Yawm ʿUkāẓ / Yawm Sharab** (يوم عكاظ / يوم شرب), at ʿUkāẓ: **Quraysh and Kinana won**
  - **Yawm al-Ḥurayra** (يوم الحريرة), at al-Ḥarra near ʿUkāẓ: the **eighth** day, won by Hawāzin; peace followed after a few skirmishes
  — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Result: **Hawāzin won four battles, Quraysh won one**, and relations were later restored — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Geography: between **Mecca and Ṭāʾif**; **ʿUkāẓ** (the annual Hijaz fair); **Nakhla**; **Shamṭa/Shamza** near ʿUkāẓ; **al-Ḥarra** near ʿUkāẓ; the **Ḥaram** sanctuary around Mecca — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Immediate trigger: **al-Barrāḍ ibn Qays** (البراض بن قيس), a Kinana man expelled from his tribe, ambushed and killed **ʿUrwa al-Raḥḥāl** (عروة الرحال) of Banū ʿĀmir **during the sacred months** and seized the caravan of the Lakhmid king **al-Nuʿmān III** — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Structural cause (the usual explanation): rivalry over **trade routes and revenues in Najd**; Quraysh dominated these routes and funded their allies' arms — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Modern historians often link the war to Quraysh efforts to **close the al-Ḥīra–Yemen caravan route through Ṭāʾif** (a commercial rival of Mecca) or to redirect it through Mecca — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- **Landau-Tasseron's alternative reading:** Banū ʿĀmir and Quraysh shared an interest in **joint control of Lakhmid caravans**; Bakr ibn ʿAbd Manāt (al-Barrāḍ's branch) was hostile to the **Jaʿfar** clan over a **cancelled protection covenant** — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Muhammad's participation: "Sources consistently report that Muhammad, a Quraysh member, took part in the war." **His reported age varies between 14 and 20.** Per hadith, his uncles brought him to the battlefield where he **shielded them from arrows**; he did not fight or carry a sword — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Classical Arabic accounts of the Fijār are freely readable: Ibn Kathīr, *al-Bidāya wa-l-Nihāya* — [ar.wikisource, حرب الفجار](https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/البداية_والنهاية/الجزء_الثاني/شهوده_عليه_الصلاة_والسلام_حرب_الفجار); and al-Ḥalabī, *al-Sīra al-Ḥalabiyya* — [ar.wikisource, باب حضوره حرب الفجار](https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/السيرة_الحلبية/باب_حضوره_حرب_الفجار)
- A dated-event entry for the Fijār exists in the Dār al-Iftāʾ historical encyclopaedia — [dorar.net/history/event/7](https://dorar.net/history/event/7)

### Inferences
- The "age 14–20" spread is doing chronological work in reverse: because tradition fixes Muhammad's birth to the **Year of the Elephant** (conventionally c. 570 CE), his reported age at the Fijār *is* the date of the Fijār. Age 14–20 therefore yields roughly **584–590 CE**, which matches the user's "c. 580s–590s" bracket. Present it that way: the war is dated **by** Muhammad's age, not independently.
- The al-Nuʿmān III caravan detail again constrains the date to his reign (582–602), reinforcing the 580s–590s window.
- The score (Hawāzin four, Quraysh one, three indecisive brawls) plus "relations were later restored" is a plausible shape for a real trade war fought in short annual bursts at a fair — much more credible than the forty-year feud narratives.
- **Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl**: the standard sīra tradition places this Meccan oath-pact (a Quraysh clan compact to defend the wronged) in Muhammad's youth, *around* the same period as the Fijār, but as a separate event. The Fijār article's silence means the user should treat any Fijār→Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl causal chain as **unverified**.

### Gaps
- **Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl (حلف الفضول)** — no source reached. Date, participating clans (conventionally Hāshim, Zuhra, Asad, Taym, al-Muṭṭalib), and the ʿAbd Allāh ibn Judʿān connection are all unverified here. Note that ʿAbd Allāh ibn Judʿān appears in the Fijār article as the armour-supplier, which is a suggestive but unconfirmed link.
- No coordinates for ʿUkāẓ. (It is conventionally placed on the Mecca–Ṭāʾif road, but the article gives no position.)
- No poetry of the Fijār obtained.
- Exact CE years for the Fijār from any scholarly source — none found; only the relative dating via Muhammad's age.

## Key Question 5: Yawm Ḥalīma (يوم حليمة) and Yawm ʿAyn Ubāgh (يوم عين أباغ)

### Takeaway
**Yawm Ḥalīma is the single best-anchored day in the whole corpus**: it is now commonly identified with a battle of **June 554 CE** near **Chalcis (modern Qinnasrīn, Syria)**, in which the Lakhmid king **al-Mundhir III** fell and the Ghassanid **al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala** lost his eldest son Jabala. **Yawm ʿAyn Ubāgh is a genuine gap** — I found no source for it.

### Cited Findings
- Date: infobox gives **554 AD**; the text says the battle is "now commonly identified with" a fight in **June 554**. "The page lists no competing dates." — [Yawm Halima, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_Halima)
- Location: the infobox spreads it across present-day **Iraq, Jordan and Syria**; the text places the June 554 battle **near Chalcis (modern Qinnasrīn, Syria)** — [Yawm Halima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_Halima)
- Belligerents: **Ghassanids** (Byzantine clients) vs. the **Lakhmid kingdom** (Sasanian clients) — [Yawm Halima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_Halima)
- **Al-Mundhir III ibn al-Nuʿmān** (المنذر الثالث), Lakhmid king, r. **503/5–554**, **fell on the field** — [Yawm Halima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_Halima)
- **Al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala** (الحارث بن جبلة), Ghassanid king, r. **528–569**, commonly identified as the Ghassanid commander; he **lost his eldest son, Jabala** — [Yawm Halima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_Halima)
- **Ḥalīma** (حليمة) was a **Ghassanid princess who assisted the warriors of her tribe**; the battle is named after her — [Yawm Halima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_Halima)
- Outcome: **Ghassanid victory**; the Ghassanids kept Chalcis and nearby areas — [Yawm Halima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_Halima)
- Casualties per infobox: Ghassanids 10+ killed, about a dozen injured; Lakhmids many killed, unknown injured — [Yawm Halima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_Halima)
- Scholarly sources cited: **Greatrex & Lieu (2002)**, *The Roman Eastern Frontier and the Persian Wars*; **Martindale (1992)**, *Prosopography of the Later Roman Empire* III; **Retsö (2003)**, *The Arabs in Antiquity*; **Shahîd [Shahîd/Shahîd = Irfan Shahîd] (2009)**, *Byzantium and the Arabs in the Sixth Century* II.2 — [Yawm Halima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_Halima)
- Further reading on the principals: [Al-Mundhir III ibn al-Nu'man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Nu%27man); [Al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah); [Lakhmid kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakhmid_kingdom)

### Inferences
- Ḥalīma is the **chronological keystone** for anyone building an absolute timeline of the ayyām: because al-Mundhir III's death is recorded in Greek/Syriac historiography (hence Greatrex & Lieu, Martindale), the Arabic *yawm* can be pinned to a dated external event. Every other day in the corpus is dated relative to things like this, or to the Hijra.
- The infobox's "Iraq, Jordan and Syria" vs. the text's "near Chalcis" is an unreconciled internal inconsistency in the source — probably the infobox is describing the two kingdoms' territories rather than the battlefield. Use **Qinnasrīn, northern Syria** (~35.9°N 37.0°E, SW of Aleppo) as the location, with the caveat that this identification is an **equation of the Arabic *yawm* with a Greek-sourced battle**, i.e. itself a scholarly reconstruction.
- Ḥalīma is dramatically the richest figure here for a VN: a named woman after whom the battle is remembered, who "assisted the warriors." The Arabic proverbial phrase *mā yawm Ḥalīma bi-sirr* ("Ḥalīma's day is no secret") is the kind of line that would be worth confirming — see Gaps.

### Gaps
- **Yawm ʿAyn Ubāgh (يوم عين أباغ)** — **no source found.** This is normally the day (traditionally c. 554 or c. 570) on which the Ghassanid or Lakhmid ruler fell, and it is sometimes conflated with or distinguished from Ḥalīma; some traditions put al-Mundhir III's death at ʿAyn Ubāgh specifically. I could not verify any of this. **The possible identity of or confusion between Ḥalīma and ʿAyn Ubāgh is a live question the report should flag as unresolved.**
- **The proverb** *mā yawm Ḥalīma bi-sirr* — the Wikipedia page explicitly **does not mention a proverb**. Unverified.
- No poetry of Ḥalīma obtained. (The day is associated with Ghassanid panegyric, esp. al-Nābigha al-Dhubyānī; unverified here.)

## Key Question 6: Other significant ayyām — Shiʿb Jabala, Raḥraḥān, al-Kulāb, Buʿāth, and the rest

### Takeaway
I obtained solid, citable detail for **Shiʿb Jabala** (with an explicit four-way dating dispute, including Caskel's figure) and **Yawm Buʿāth** (c. 617 CE). **Raḥraḥān** and **Dhū Najab** are datable *relative* to Shiʿb Jabala. For **Yawm al-Kulāb I and II, Khazāz/Khazāzā, Uḥāḍ al-Jaww, al-Nisār, al-Mushaqqar, al-Ṣafqa, Ṭikhfa, al-Firāḍ and al-Sullān** I found **no citable detail** and am reporting them as gaps rather than inventing dates.

### Cited Findings — Yawm Shiʿb Jabala (يوم شعب جبلة)
- **Dating, genuinely disputed four ways:** "Traditionally **551, 553**, or, most commonly, **570**. **Bosworth** places it under **al-Nuʿmān III (r. 582–602)**, and **Caskel** dates it to **c. 580**." — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- **The battle of Raḥraḥān (يوم رحرحان) came about a year earlier** — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- Combatants: **Qays** side — **Hawāzin** (incl. **Banū ʿĀmir**) and **Banū ʿAbs**. Opposing coalition — **Tamīm**, **Banū Dhubyān** of Ghaṭafān, **Banū Asad**, **Kindite** contingents (**Banū al-Jawn**), and **Lakhmid** forces — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- Leaders: **Laqīṭ ibn Zurāra** (لقيط بن زرارة, Tamīm, coalition leader); **al-Aḥwaṣ ibn Jaʿfar** (Banū ʿĀmir); **Qays ibn Zuhayr** (Banū ʿAbs); **Ḥassān** (Lakhmid commander, brother of al-Nuʿmān); **Muʿāwiya** and **ʿAmr** (Kindite princes); **al-Mundhir IV** (Lakhmid king, r. 575–580) — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- **Location: an isolated mountain pass called Shiʿb Jabala in ʿĀmir territory, about 150 km south of ʿUnayza in Najd**, Saudi Arabia — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- Cause chain: **Khālid ibn Jaʿfar**, a Hawāzin chief, was killed by **al-Ḥārith ibn Ẓālim**; Laqīṭ then protected al-Ḥārith, angering Banū ʿĀmir; at **Raḥraḥān** the ʿĀmir captured Laqīṭ's brother **Maʿbad**, who died in captivity; Laqīṭ gathered the coalition to avenge him — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- Course (vivid): **Qays ibn Zuhayr had his men's camels deprived of water so they would charge toward water.** The attack came in three stages — a sortie by the cameleers, an assault by foot soldiers, and a final charge by ʿĀmir and ʿAbs horsemen led by al-Aḥwaṣ — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- Outcome: decisive Qays victory. **Laqīṭ** and **one Kindite prince** were killed, along with **Zuhayr ibn ʿAmr ibn Muʿāwiya** of the ʿĀmir. The battle **weakened Kindite power in northern Arabia** and contributed to their withdrawal to **Ḥaḍramawt** — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- Alternative name: **yawm al-Jawnnayn** (يوم الجونين), "the day of the two men of al-Jawn" — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- Status: "**one of the three most famous *ayyām* battle days**" — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- Sequel: about a year later, the **Yarbūʿ** clan of Tamīm killed **Ibn Kabsha** at the **Day of Dhū Najab** (يوم ذي نجب), and killed or wounded several Kilābī chiefs — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- Related tribal/biographical entries: [Abu Bara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bara); [Banu Kilab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Kilab)

### Cited Findings — Yawm Buʿāth (يوم بعاث)
- Date: **c. 617 CE**, with the lead-up events in the 610s; placed at the start of Muhammad's prophethood and shortly before his move to Medina (the Hijra, dated by modern academics to **622**). **No alternative dates are offered** — [Yawm al-Buath, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath)
- Location: **Buʿāth, in Medina, in the Hijaz**. The source does **not** give Buʿāth's precise position relative to the city — [Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath)
- **Aws** (أوس): led by **Ḥuḍayr al-Katāʾib ibn Simāk al-Ashhalī**; supported by the Jewish tribes of **Qurayẓa** and **Naḍīr** and by **Banū Muzayna**; later gained **Ghassanid** backing — [Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath)
- **Khazraj** (خزرج): led by **ʿAmr ibn al-Nuʿmān al-Bayāḍī**, **killed in the fighting**; supported by **Banū Qaynuqāʿ**, **Banū Juhayna**, **Banū Ashjaʿ** — [Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath)
- Cause: long-standing feud. When the Aws sought Jewish allies, the Khazraj threatened to take Jewish children hostage; **ʿAmr ibn al-Nuʿmān seized hostages from the Jewish tribes and killed them** after they refused to hand over part of their property — which drove those tribes to back the Aws — [Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath)
- Course and outcome: **the Aws won.** They were losing at first, but **Ḥuḍayr's appeal to past Khazraj wrongs rallied them**; the Khazraj, having failed to secure outside allies, were routed and withdrew. Afterwards **both tribes chose ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy as a ruler to keep the peace** — [Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath)
- Significance: the **last armed clash between the two tribes before they converted to Islam**. ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy lost influence after Muhammad arrived in 622; both tribes converted, became the **Anṣār**, and fought with the Muhājirūn at **Badr in 624** — [Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath)
- Source base cited for Buʿāth: **Abū al-Faraj al-Iṣfahānī, *Kitāb al-Aghānī***; **Ibn Saʿd, *Kitāb al-Ṭabaqāt al-Kabīr***; **Ibn Hishām, *Sīra***; **C. E. Bosworth** in *Encyclopaedia of Islam*; **Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī** ḥadīth 3846; also Mubārakpūrī, *The Sealed Nectar*, and Tawfīq Barrū, *Taʾrīkh al-ʿArab al-Qadīm* — [Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath)
- A Shīʿī reference-work entry also exists — [en.wikishia.net, Battle of Bu'ath](https://en.wikishia.net/view/Battle_of_Bu%27ath)
- Tribal background entry — [Banu Aws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Aws)

### Inferences
- **Shiʿb Jabala is the best single illustration of how bad the chronology is.** The same event is placed at 551, 553, 570, c. 580, and "under al-Nuʿmān III (582–602)" — a spread of **~50 years** for one famous battle. Note also the internal contradiction in the article's own data: it names **al-Mundhir IV (r. 575–580)** among the leaders, which is compatible with Caskel's c. 580 but **incompatible** with 551/553/570 and with Bosworth's al-Nuʿmān III placement. The user should present Shiʿb Jabala as c. 580 (Caskel) with the traditional 570 as the folk date, and show the contradiction.
- A defensible **relative** chain emerges and is more trustworthy than any absolute date: *Khālid ibn Jaʿfar killed* → **Raḥraḥān** (Maʿbad captured) → **+1 year: Shiʿb Jabala** (Laqīṭ killed) → **+~1 year: Dhū Najab**. For a VN this three-beat sequence is usable as a solid internal timeline even if the CE year is uncertain.
- **Qays ibn Zuhayr** links Dāḥis to Shiʿb Jabala; **Banū Dhubyān** fights ʿAbs in Dāḥis and appears in the anti-Qays coalition at Shiʿb Jabala; **Hawāzin** backs Dhubyān in Dāḥis but is on the Qays side at Shiʿb Jabala and against Quraysh in the Fijār; **Abū Barāʾ** leads ʿĀmir/Hawāzin in the Fijār. These overlapping casts put **Dāḥis, Raḥraḥān, Shiʿb Jabala and the Fijār within roughly one generation, c. 570–600** — which is a stronger result than any of the individual dates.
- **Buʿāth at c. 617** is the chronological *floor* of the pre-Islamic ayyām: it is dated by its position relative to the Hijra (622), which is the one fixed point the Arabic tradition really has. Everything else is dated by counting backwards from here.
- The Jewish tribes of Medina as kingmakers at Buʿāth is directly load-bearing for what happens after 622 — the user's VN timeline should treat Buʿāth as the political precondition for the Medinan settlement, not a stray feud.

### Gaps
I found **no citable source** in this session for the following, and will not supply dates or places from memory:
- **Yawm al-Kulāb I and II** (يوم الكلاب الأول والثاني) — conventionally Tamīm/Kinda-related days in Najd, the first often linked to the collapse of Kinda's power and the second to Tamīm vs. a Yemeni/Madhḥij force. **Entirely unverified.** The Shiʿb Jabala article does *not* mention Yawm al-Kulāb (I checked; it mentions the *Kilāb* clan, which is a different word and a common source of confusion — كلاب "dogs" / the place al-Kulāb vs. بنو كلاب the clan. **Flag this homograph trap for the user.**)
- **Yawm Khazāz / Khazāzā** (يوم خزاز / خزازى) — traditionally a pan-Maʿaddite victory over Yemen under Kulayb ibn Rabīʿa, i.e. *before* the Basūs war. Unverified. A lexicographical entry exists and may define the toponym — [arabiclexicon.hawramani.com, خزاز وخزازى](https://arabiclexicon.hawramani.com/خزاز-وخزازى/)
- **Yawm Uḥāḍ al-Jaww** (يوم أحاض الجو), **Yawm al-Nisār** (يوم النسار), **Yawm al-Mushaqqar** (يوم المشقر), **Yawm al-Ṣafqa** (يوم الصفقة), **Yawm Ṭikhfa** (يوم طخفة), **Yawm al-Firāḍ** (يوم الفراض), **Yawm al-Sullān** (يوم السلان) — no usable source reached. A lexicon entry for al-Ṣafqa exists — [arabiclexicon.hawramani.com, الصفقة](https://arabiclexicon.hawramani.com/الصّفقةُ/?book=53) — but I did not fetch it.
- No coordinates for Shiʿb Jabala (only "~150 km south of ʿUnayza"), Buʿāth, or any other site.
- No poetry text for Shiʿb Jabala — the article explicitly mentions none.

## Key Question 7: Modern scholarly assessment — reliability, and attempts at an absolute chronology

### Takeaway
The consensus, as far as I could source it, is that the ayyām are **oral tribal history, flexible in transmission, lacking absolute dating and offering only limited relative chronology**, with poetry serving as the main device of contextualisation and authentication. Attempts at absolute chronology (Caskel, Caussin de Perceval, Muir) are **reconstructions**, and where I could compare them they disagree by decades.

### Cited Findings
- Toral-Niehoff (2021) — the main scholarly basis of the English Wikipedia survey — "describes the ayyām as **oral tribal history that was flexible and reshaped by performers and audiences**"; says the narratives "present themselves as realistic while being shaped by social and scholarly concerns," and that they "**lack absolute dating and offer only limited relative chronology**"; "**poetry serves as the main means of contextualization and authentication**" — [Days of the Arabs, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Days_of_the_Arabs)
- **Werner Caskel (1930)** and **Egbert Meyer (1970)** appear in the bibliography of that survey; Meyer's monograph is titled ***Der historische Gehalt der Aiyām al-ʿArab*** ("The historical content of the Ayyām al-ʿArab") — [Days of the Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Days_of_the_Arabs); catalogue record: [relbib.de/Record/218505612](https://relbib.de/Record/218505612)
- Also in the bibliography: **Ilse Lichtenstadter (1935)** and **E. Mittwoch (1993)** — [Days of the Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Days_of_the_Arabs)
- **Caskel dates Shiʿb Jabala to c. 580**, against the traditional 551/553/570 and against **Bosworth's** placement under al-Nuʿmān III (582–602) — [Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)
- **Sir William Muir** is the named source for the c. 568–609 dating of the Dāḥis war — [Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)
- **Werner Caskel** has a biographical entry — [Werner Caskel, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Caskel)
- **Ella Landau-Tasseron** contributes substantively on two of the days: on **Dhī Qār**, that "leadership among the Bakr shifted among various warriors, with no single commander-in-chief" — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar); and on the **Fijār**, a revisionist causal account (shared Quraysh–ʿĀmir interest in Lakhmid caravans; Bakr ibn ʿAbd Manāt vs. Jaʿfar over a cancelled protection covenant) — [Fijar Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fijar_Wars)
- Her publication record is catalogued at the Hebrew University — [cris.huji.ac.il, "The scholarly oeuvre of Professor Ella Landau-Tasseron"](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-scholarly-oeuvre-of-professor-ella-landau-tasseron/) — and in the OCIANA bibliography — [ociana.osu.edu/citations/6745](https://ociana.osu.edu/citations/6745), [ociana.osu.edu/citations/3859](https://ociana.osu.edu/citations/3859); also a BnF authority record — [catalogue.bnf.fr](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb16090592q)
- Transmission history: authors working with **ahl al-ḥadīth** methods "often excluded the genre"; **al-Ṭabarī mentions the ayyām only once, in connection with the Battle of Dhī Qār**; the genre **re-entered mainstream historiography** through authors such as **Ibn al-Athīr (13th c.)** — [Days of the Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Days_of_the_Arabs)
- Scale scepticism as a live position: on Dhī Qār, "it may have been no more than a skirmish," a view attributed to **Rezakhani**; and **Persian sources do not mention the conflict** — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- On the Basūs war specifically: "modern scholarship treats the war as a composite narrative, not a single prolonged conflict" — [Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- A general-reference framing of the genre exists at [Britannica, "The Days"](https://britannica.com/topic/The-Days)
- Arabic-language scholarship on the ayyām is active in Iraqi journals; e.g. "The Days of the Arabs and Persians before Islam in the Book of *Muʿjam mā Istaʿjam* of al-Bakrī (487 AH)," University of Mosul — [ojs.uomosul.edu.iq, article 5472](https://ojs.uomosul.edu.iq/index.php/jeh/article/view/5472), full text PDF: [download link](https://ojs.uomosul.edu.iq/index.php/jeh/article/download/44084/43835/44140)
- Another Mosul-journal PDF on the ayyām is freely downloadable — [ojs.uomosul.edu.iq PDF](https://ojs.uomosul.edu.iq/index.php/jeh/article/download/43191/42955/43260)
- M. J. Kister's bibliography (a standard resource for pre-Islamic Arabian tribal history) is freely browsable — [kister.huji.ac.il/node/26](https://kister.huji.ac.il/node/26)

### Inferences
- **The mechanism of the traditional chronology is visible in the sources even where it isn't named.** Three distinct anchoring devices appear: (1) counting back from the **Hijra/622** — this is how Buʿāth gets c. 617; (2) **Muhammad's reported age**, which is itself pegged to the Year of the Elephant (c. 570) — this is how the Fijār gets the 580s–590s; and (3) **synchronism with a datable Lakhmid or Ghassanid reign** — this is how Dhī Qār gets 604–611 (post-602 deposition) and how Shiʿb Jabala gets pushed to c. 580 (al-Mundhir IV, 575–580) or to 582–602 (al-Nuʿmān III). Method (3) is the most reliable, because Lakhmid and Ghassanid reigns touch Greek, Syriac and Persian records; (1) and (2) are internal to the tradition.
- **Where the traditional chronology breaks:** the "forty-year war" dates (Basūs c. 494–534/540, Dāḥis c. 568–609) are generated by the forty-year topos, and in the Dāḥis case the narrative detail (al-Nuʿmān III's caravans, r. 582–602) contradicts the start-date. Likewise the Dhī Qār tradition's upper date of 624 contradicts its own causal premise (al-Nuʿmān's 602 fall) and the pre-Islamic framing. These are the places the user should mark as "the tradition disagrees with itself."
- The user's worry is well founded: **there is no scholarly absolute chronology of the ayyām that commands assent.** For a VN, the honest and dramatically useful move is to adopt one scheme (Caskel's, where available) and treat it as the story's internal calendar, while letting characters disagree about when things happened — which is itself period-accurate, since the tradition is oral and contested.
- Toral-Niehoff's point that **poetry is the authentication device** is directly relevant to the user's project: in this culture the proof that a battle happened *is* the verse, which means a VN can legitimately stage the transmission of a poem as the stakes of an episode.

## Key Question 8: The standard Arabic compilations — editions and free online access

### Takeaway
The foundational compilation, **Abū ʿUbayda's** *ayyām* corpus, is **lost**; it survives only through later quotation, and it underlies most of the tradition. The one classical work with a *countable* catalogue of days that I could source is **al-Maydānī's *Majmaʿ al-amthāl*, chapter 29, which lists 132 pre-Islamic battle-days** with short summary accounts. Several classical texts and the Jād al-Mawlā compilation are available free in Arabic online, though I could not verify every link.

### Cited Findings
- **Abū ʿUbayda Maʿmar b. al-Muthannā** (110–209 AH / 728–824 CE), a Basran grammarian and lexicographer, "is credited with **forming the ayyām into a distinct corpus**." He wrote a **shorter work of 75 or 150 days** and a **larger *Maqātil al-fursān* (مقاتل الفرسان) of 1200 or 1600 days**. **Neither survives, but they underlie most later ayyām traditions.** — [Days of the Arabs, en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Days_of_the_Arabs)
- **Ibn al-Kalbī and his father Muḥammad ibn al-Sāʾib al-Kalbī** "took a more explicitly historical approach to the material" — [Days of the Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Days_of_the_Arabs)
- **Ibn al-Kalbī (d. 819)** and **Abū ʿUbayda (Maʿmar ibn al-Muthannā)** transmit **two divergent versions** of Dhī Qār; Abū ʿUbayda's is judged "more fantastical and less reliable" — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)
- **Al-Maydānī, *Majmaʿ al-amthāl* (مجمع الأمثال): "lists 132 pre-Islamic battle-days in chapter 29," with short, summary accounts** — [Days of the Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Days_of_the_Arabs)
- **Al-Ṭabarī** mentions the ayyām **only once**, in connection with Dhī Qār — [Days of the Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Days_of_the_Arabs); his *Taʾrīkh al-rusul wa-l-mulūk* nonetheless "contains the classical account" of that battle — [Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)

### Inferences
- **The practical research path for the user is al-Maydānī, chapter 29.** It is the only classical text I can point to with a *finite, enumerated* list (132 days) in summary form — exactly the index a worldbuilder needs — and *Majmaʿ al-amthāl* is long out of copyright and standard on shamela.ws and archive.org.
- **The loss of Abū ʿUbayda's two books is the central source-critical fact of this field.** The "1200 or 1600 days" figure for *Maqātil al-fursān* (note: the number itself is transmitted in two versions) tells you the corpus was vast and that everything we have is excerpt and quotation at several removes. Combined with Toral-Niehoff's point about performative reshaping, this is why no absolute chronology is recoverable.
- The variance in the reported sizes of Abū ʿUbayda's own works (75 *or* 150; 1200 *or* 1600) is itself a neat illustration of how unstable the transmission is — worth citing to the user as a one-line demonstration.
