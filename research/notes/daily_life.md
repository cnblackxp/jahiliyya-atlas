# Daily Life, Society and Material Culture in Pre-Islamic Arabia (c. 400–630 CE)

> **Source-critical note that applies throughout.** Almost everything "textural" we have about the Ḥijāz in 400–630 CE comes from two filtered bodies of evidence: (a) *pre-Islamic poetry* (الشعر الجاهلي), which was composed and transmitted orally and only written down from the 8th century CE onward, and (b) *Islamic-era prose* (ḥadīth, sīra, tafsīr, akhbār, lexicography, Kitāb al-Aghānī) written 150–400 years later by authors describing a Jāhiliyya they did not witness and had polemical reasons to characterise. A third, independent body — *inscriptions and archaeology* (Safaitic, Hismaic, Dadanitic, Sabaic, Nabataean; Qaryat al-Fāw, Ḥegra, Taymāʾ, Maʾrib) — is contemporary but says almost nothing about marriage law, women's status, or cuisine. Where the two bodies conflict I flag it. Items below marked **[later-source]** are Islamic-era literary reports; **[poetry]** means attested in the pre-Islamic poetic corpus; **[epigraphic/archaeo]** means contemporary material evidence.

## Tribal society and law: qabīla, sayyid, jiwār, ḥilf, diya, thaʾr, arbitration, slavery

### Takeaway
The segmentary vocabulary (qabīla / baṭn / ʿashīra / fakhdh) and the neat typology of jiwār / ḥilf / walāʾ are largely the product of Islamic-era systematisers; the *institutions* are real and corroborated, but Landau-Tasseron shows the terms were used loosely and inconsistently in the sources themselves, and that identifying the parties to any specific alliance is often impossible. The practical machinery — co-liability for blood, oath-sworn alliance, protected-guest status, arbitration instead of courts — is well attested.

### Cited Findings
- *Ḥilf* (حِلْف, "alliance") derives from the root for "oath" (ḥalafa حلف); alliances were so called because they were sworn. Landau-Tasseron distinguishes three working types: **limited alliances** between sections for a specific aim (the Muṭayyabūn and the Aḥlāf of Quraysh المطيبون والأحلاف are the stock example); **general alliances** between roughly equal groups, which could gradually merge, adopt a shared name and then invent a common ancestor (the Barājim البراجم, "finger-joints," and the Ribāb الرباب); and **hosting alliances**, where a weaker section attached itself to a stronger one, shared its co-liability and sometimes took its nisba or genealogy — [Landau-Tasseron, al-Qanṭara](https://al-qantara.revistas.csic.es/index.php/al-qantara/article/download/120/106/106)
- *Jiwār* (جِوار, protection) **overlaps with** ḥilf in actual usage; *walāʾ* (ولاء) is a separate pact that both overlaps with and differs from ḥilf. Landau-Tasseron explicitly treats the tidy classification schemes of other scholars as unclear and source-driven rather than real — [Landau-Tasseron, al-Qanṭara](https://al-qantara.revistas.csic.es/index.php/al-qantara/article/download/120/106/106)
- **The oath ritual is the most visualisable detail available.** A host individual performed the ceremony; the oath was spoken in the singular yet bound whole groups. Formulae included "my blood is your blood" (دمي دمك) and *hadmī hadmuka* (هدمي هدمك), plus promises of mutual inheritance, joint vengeance and shared blood-money. Rituals varied by tribe: dipping hands **in blood** (Khathʿam خثعم), dipping hands **in fruit juice** (the Ribāb), swearing **by a tree** (Madhḥij مذحج), and **writing the pact on parchment** (al-Adīm الأديم) — [Landau-Tasseron, al-Qanṭara](https://al-qantara.revistas.csic.es/index.php/al-qantara/article/download/120/106/106)
- Allies were a recognised, respectable status; informal attached residents — *zanīm* (زنيم) and *mulṣaq* (مُلصَق) — were viewed negatively. In an Umayyad case the descendants of the Companion Ṣuhayb b. Sinān drew a *higher* pension as allies than they would have as *mawālī* (موالي, clients), and the caliph reversed a cut after pressure from their allies — so ally ranked above client — [Landau-Tasseron, al-Qanṭara](https://al-qantara.revistas.csic.es/index.php/al-qantara/article/download/120/106/106)
- **The blood price (diya دية) rose.** Compensation for a life was **ten she-camels** in the older pre-Islamic practice; it was raised to **one hundred** in the region where Islam arose, and Muḥammad endorsed the hundred-camel figure. The halving of a woman's diya belongs to the later Islamic rules, not demonstrably to the Jāhiliyya — [Britannica, "diyah"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/diyah)
- Tribal co-liability was absolute in the literary tradition: a tribe was obliged to protect its member **even when he was the wrongdoer**, and a tribe that failed in this faced ridicule and contempt. **[later-source]** — [Delhi Univ., "Arabia before Islam"](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)
- *Thaʾr* (ثأر, vendetta) is described by Rodinson as one of the pillars of Bedouin society; with no courts or police the injured party enforced redress itself. **[later-source + modern synthesis]** — [Delhi Univ.](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)
- A chief's authority was **moral and personal, not institutional**; a raid-leader's authority rested on his followers getting a fair share of booty and **expired when the expedition ended**. Caravans and villages bought immunity from nomadic raiders for fixed sums (the khuwwa/protection-payment principle) — [Delhi Univ.](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)
- *ʿAṣabiyya* (عصبية) is glossed as clan spirit and is said to have taken precedence over ethics. **Important caveat:** Miller (2024) argues the *critique* of ʿaṣabiyya is an early-Umayyad literary development, later attributed back to Muḥammad and used to define the Jāhiliyya — so the concept as we receive it is partly a retrospective construct — [Delhi Univ.](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf); [Wikipedia, "Jahiliyyah" (citing Miller 2024)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyyah)
- **Poets as jurists.** Poets arbitrated disputes, resolved conflicts and negotiated hostage ransoms — the poet was a political actor, not only an entertainer — [Wikipedia, "Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabic_poetry)
- Slaves (ʿabīd عبيد) were bought and sold and formed the lowest social stratum. **[later-source]** — [Delhi Univ.](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)
- Enslaved women forced into prostitution appear in the Ibāḍī commentator Hūd ibn al-Muḥakkam and the Shīʿī al-Qummī — i.e. this charge against the Jāhiliyya reaches us only through sectarian Islamic-era exegesis — [Wikipedia, "Jahiliyyah" (citing Urban 2020)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyyah)
- On *aghribat al-ʿArab* (أغربة العرب, "the ravens of the Arabs"), the black poets of whom ʿAntara b. Shaddād (عنترة بن شداد) is the emblem: scholarly treatment exists (Lewis's and others' work on race in the poetry) but I could not confirm the term's attestation details from an accessible source — [LSU, "Race in pre-Islamic poetry: the work of Antara Ibn Shaddad"](https://repository.lsu.edu/fll_pubs/105)

### Inferences
- For a travelling couple, the single most dramatically useful institution is **jiwār**: the protected-guest relationship is what makes long-distance travel through others' territory possible at all, and its violation is an immediately legible catastrophe. The oath formulae above ("my blood is your blood," hands dipped in blood or juice, the pact written on parchment) are stageable scenes.
- The **expiry of a raid-leader's authority with the raid** is a good structural detail: authority in the desert is episodic and consensual, which undercuts any temptation to write a feudal-style chain of command.
- Because Landau-Tasseron shows the technical vocabulary was already blurry in the sources, a story can use the terms loosely without being "wrong" — characters arguing about whether a relationship is ḥilf or jiwār is historically plausible, not anachronistic.

### Gaps
- I could not source the segmentary chain **qabīla (قبيلة) / baṭn (بطن) / ʿashīra (عشيرة) / fakhdh (فخذ)** from a reliable scholarly page in the searches performed. The hierarchy is standard in handbooks but I will not assert a ranked order without a citation; Jawād ʿAlī (below) treats it at length and should be checked.
- No sourced detail found on *khalīʿ* (خليع, the outlawed/disowned man), *muʿāhada* (معاهدة), the formal procedure of *ḥukūma / taḥkīm* (حكومة/تحكيم), or named famous arbiters (e.g. Aktham b. Ṣayfī, Hāshim b. ʿAbd Manāf). These are well covered in EI² and in Jawād ʿAlī and should be looked up directly.
- No sourced confirmation of the ten-vs-hundred camel transition being attributed to ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib specifically.

## Women's lives: marriage, bride price, infanticide, women of standing, veiling, property

### Takeaway
The famous "four forms of Jāhiliyya marriage" rest on a **single ḥadīth** (Bukhārī 5127, ʿUrwa ← ʿĀʾisha) whose rhetorical purpose is to show Islam abolishing three of four forms — it is evidence of 8th/9th-century memory, not a survey of 6th-century practice. On female infanticide the position has shifted decisively: Lindstedt argues no pre-Islamic source in any language attests *waʾd al-banāt* as a custom, that the key Qurʾānic verses probably did not originally mean it, and that the charge began as **anti-Tamīm polemic after the second fitna**.

### Cited Findings
- **The four forms (Bukhārī 5127, Book 67 no. 63; ʿUrwa b. al-Zubayr ← ʿĀʾisha):** (1) *nikāḥ al-nās al-yawm* (نكاح الناس اليوم) — ordinary marriage with mahr, the one Islam kept; (2) *nikāḥ al-istibḍāʿ* (نكاح الاستبضاع) — a husband sends his wife to another man to conceive noble offspring and stays away from her until the pregnancy shows; (3) a group of **fewer than ten** men visit one woman, and after she bears a child she names the father, who cannot refuse; (4) open prostitution — women who set up **flags/banners at their doors** (الرايات), with a *qāʾif* (القافة, resemblance-expert / physiognomist) called to assign paternity. The report closes: Muḥammad "demolished all Jāhiliyya marriage except the marriage of people today" (هدم نكاح الجاهلية كله إلا نكاح الناس اليوم) — [Sunnah.com, Bukhārī 5127](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5127); [Delhi Univ. (same four, via Sayyid Quṭb)](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)
- Also in the literary tradition: no fixed limit on the number of wives, and a son **inheriting his father's widows** (other than his own mother) — the practice Q 4:22 forbids. **[later-source]** — [Delhi Univ.](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)
- **Female infanticide — the revisionist case (Ilkka Lindstedt).** "No pre-Islamic source (in Arabic or any other language) evidences the supposed custom of female infanticide." He does not deny individual killings in famine but denies a widespread custom — [Lindstedt, "The Portrayal of the Pre-Islamic Arabs as Murderers of Their Own Infants," PDF](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Lindstedt-I-–-The-portrayal-of-the-pre-Islamic-.pdf); journal version: [*Studia Orientalia Electronica*/journal.fi](https://journal.fi/ar/article/view/148924)
- Qurʾānic texts: general condemnations of killing children at **6:137, 6:140, 6:151, 17:31, 60:12**. The two verses traditionally read as female infanticide are **16:57–59** and **81:8–9**. Lindstedt reads *yadussuhu fī al-turāb* (يدسّه في التراب, Q 16:59) as **abandoning** a female infant rather than burying her alive, and *al-mawʾūda* (الموؤودة, Q 81:8) as "the one trodden/trampled" — from *waʾada* وأد "to tread" — with the "buried alive" sense possibly imported from the similar root *w-d-ʾ* — [Lindstedt PDF](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Lindstedt-I-–-The-portrayal-of-the-pre-Islamic-.pdf)
- The classic traditional evidence: the ḥadīth of **Qays b. ʿĀṣim al-Tamīmī** claiming to have buried eight daughters, plus al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī (d. 1035, on Q 81:8) and al-Qurṭubī treating it as widespread; reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās. Lindstedt calls the individual-killer narratives "tendentious and unrealistic," notes Qays is portrayed inconsistently (drunkard and child-killer, but also praised by the Prophet), and argues the accusation arose as anti-Tamīm polemic after the **second fitna (680–692 CE)** when Tamīm backed Ibn al-Zubayr: al-Akhṭal's pro-Umayyad attack on Tamīm does *not* mention infanticide, while al-Farazdaq's reply claims his uncle forbade it — implying the charge was already in circulation — [Lindstedt PDF](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Lindstedt-I-–-The-portrayal-of-the-pre-Islamic-.pdf); [Wikipedia, "Jahiliyyah"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyyah)
- The older consensus treated it as fact: **EI entries by Leemhuis (2012) and Giladi (2009)**. The shift is recent (Paraskeva; Lindstedt 2023) — so a 2026 writer should present it as contested, with the revisionist case now strong — [Lindstedt PDF](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Lindstedt-I-–-The-portrayal-of-the-pre-Islamic-.pdf)
- Ritual **wailing for the dead (niyāḥa نياحة)** is treated as a Jāhilī trait, but Juynboll, Halevi and Webb disagree over whether it was a real pre-Islamic practice or a later exaggeration — a useful warning that even "atmospheric" customs are contested — [Wikipedia, "Jahiliyyah"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyyah)
- **A real pre-Islamic woman you can look at:** *Hinat daughter of Wahbu* (هنات بنت وهبو), named in a tomb inscription at Ḥegra carved **c. 60–61 CE**. Skeletal analysis: age **40–50**, height **~1.6 m**, medium social status. Her tomb in Jabal Aḥmar preserved bones, **skin, hair, textiles, leather and plant matter**, and may have held up to 80 burials. The RCU facial reconstruction (CT scan → forensic modelling by Philippe Froesch with pathologist Philippe Charlier → stereolithographic silicone bust by Ramón Lopez) dressed her in **artisan-woven linen matched to textile fragments from the Ḥegra tombs** and **replica earrings copied from jewellery found at Ḥegra**. The article is candid that eye colour, skin tone and wrinkles were interpretive — a "subjective portrait" — [National Geographic, "Meeting Hinat"](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/paid-content-meeting-hinat)
- **Veiling:** the Ca' Foscari study implies an **existing Ancient Arabian dress code** that changing sociocultural conditions reshaped "to promote moral attitudes and social status," and attributes the call to concealment to specific events in Muḥammad's lifetime; keywords *khimār* (خمار), *jilbāb* (جلباب), *ḥijāb* (حجاب), *zīna* (زينة). Only the abstract is open, and it does **not** describe the earlier practice — [Edizioni Ca' Foscari, "The Practice of Veiling as an Expression of the Moral Behaviour of Women…"](https://edizionicafoscari.it/en/edizioni4/libri/978-88-6969-522-3/the-practice-of-veiling-as-an-expression-of-the-mo/)
- **Leila Ahmed's frame** (for the report-writer): *Women and Gender in Islam* (1992) Part One surveys gender practice in the region's pre-Islamic societies; Part Two treats Arabia at Islam's emergence. Her thesis is that Islam **aligned the Arabian organisation of women with patterns already widespread across the Middle East and Mediterranean** — i.e. the changes were regional convergence, not Arabian exceptionalism — [PDF summary](https://www.colleenlnugent.com/exams/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2021/06/gender_islam.pdf); [De Gruyter/Yale ed.](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.12987/9780300258172/html)
- South Arabian epigraphy touches women only obliquely: **Minaean lists record payments for foreign women admitted into Minaean society**, and Pliny reports that the sacred incense-harvesting families avoided contact with women — [Hoyland, *Arabia and the Arabs*, ch. excerpt PDF](https://courses.edx.org/asset-v1:NotreDameX+TH120.2x+3T2015+type@asset+block/ODL_TH1202x_M02_HoylandChapter9.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a wife-protagonist, the **honest** position is that the legal-institutional detail (ẓihār, īlāʾ, the four marriage forms) is Islamic-era testimony about a lost world, while the *lived* detail we can actually anchor is archaeological and personal: Hinat's linen, her earrings, her osteoarthritis, her being named in stone as "daughter of Wahbu" (patronymic, publicly, on a monument she commissioned or that was commissioned for her). That is a stronger basis for characterisation than the marriage typology.
- Lindstedt's argument matters for tone: a visual novel that stages routine female infanticide would be dramatising a 9th-century polemic rather than a 6th-century practice. If the user wants it, the safe framing is an individual act in famine, treated as horrifying by other characters.
- "Medium social status," 40–50 years old, 1.6 m, with chronic joint disease and dental infection — this is the realistic physical register for an adult woman of the period, and worth holding against idealised art direction.

### Gaps
- **Not found in accessible sources:** *nikāḥ al-mutʿa* (نكاح المتعة), *nikāḥ al-badal* (نكاح البدل), *shighār* (شغار), *ḍayzan* (ضيزن) — these appear in Jawād ʿAlī and in EI²/Encyclopaedia of the Qurʾān, not in anything I could open. Treat all four as late-reported and unverified here.
- No sourced detail on *mahr* / *ṣadāq* amounts, *ẓihār* (ظهار), *īlāʾ* (إيلاء), or women's property and inheritance before Islam.
- **Khadīja bint Khuwaylid**: searches returned only devotional and popular pages, no critical scholarship on the historicity of her commercial role. Her status as a merchant financing caravans is universally repeated in the sīra tradition but I found no source that interrogates it.
- No reliable material found on the *qayna* (قينة) beyond the item below, on women warriors / ḥidāʾ-singers urging on fighters, on priestesses (kāhina كاهنة), or on named queens of the 400–630 window (Mavia/Māwiyya, the Kindite and Ghassānid noblewomen). El-Badawi's *Queens and Prophets* (Oneworld, 2022) is the obvious recent trade-scholarly entry point — [publisher page](https://www.simonandschuster.ca/books/Queens-and-Prophets/Emran-Iqbal-El-Badawi/9780861544455)

## Material culture: the black tent, furnishings, clothing, adornment, weapons

### Takeaway
The best concrete description of the *bayt al-shaʿr* comes from 20th-century ethnography of surviving Bedouin practice (Aramco World, 1966) — technically continuous but **not** evidence for the 6th century; for the 400–630 window itself, Qaryat al-Fāw's frescoes are the only direct pictorial source for dress, banqueting and bodies, and Ḥegra's tombs the only source for actual textiles and leather. Weapons are poorly served: the standard sword scholarship (al-Kindī) is **9th-century** and tells us about Abbasid-era production, not Jāhilī blades.

### Cited Findings — the tent (ethnographic, 20th c.)
- Roof woven from long narrow strips of heavy **black or brown goat hair or sheep wool — not camel hair**; women spun the yarn themselves or bought it from village weavers. A wealthy shaykh's tent used about **six extra-broad strips roughly 75 feet long** sewn into one rectangle; a small one- or two-pole tent used **three or four strips about 25 feet long** — [Aramco World, "The Black Tent," 1966](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/196603/the.black.tent.htm)
- **Poles and guying:** four poles for a large tent, one or two for a small one; **two hemp ropes to each pole, three to each side**; rope ends pegged into firm ground or tied to brushwood clumps buried about **two feet deep in sand** — [Aramco World](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/196603/the.black.tent.htm)
- **Walls:** a separate long strip, the *rūāq / ruwāq* (رواق), pinned to three sides of the roof with **six-inch wooden pins**, hanging to the ground and buried or pegged there. **The open side always faces away from the wind.** In heat the rūāq is rolled up for airflow while still shading — [Aramco World](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/196603/the.black.tent.htm)
- **Interior division:** vertical patterned curtains called *qāṭaʿ / qāṭa* (قاطع) split the interior. The men's section doubles as the guest room; other sections are kitchen and the women's quarters, called the *muḥarram* (محرّم). In cold weather the qāṭa are drawn across the open end to close the tent and hold the fire's warmth — [Aramco World](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/196603/the.black.tent.htm)
- **Furnishings:** bare desert floor, covered with local rugs or (for the wealthy) Persian carpets; mattresses (the article gives the Gulf-Persian term *dōshak*) for sitting and sleeping. In Eastern Arabia the guest chamber is on the **east** side, with the master's rifle hung from a tent pole. The **hearth sits at the exact centre of the guest area**, and beside it: three coffee pots, a bean roaster, a wooden cooling tray, a grinder, and an incense burner. **The host's camel saddle serves as a guest's backrest** — [Aramco World](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/196603/the.black.tent.htm)
  - *Caveat for art direction:* coffee is a **16th-century-onward** import. The coffee kit is the single element to delete for a 6th-century scene; replace with the hearth, the milking vessel and the incense burner, and keep everything else.

### Cited Findings — clothing and bodies, from the period itself
- **Qaryat al-Fāw frescoes (3rd c. BCE – 3rd c. CE)** are the direct pictorial evidence. The *mrzḥ* (مرزح, ritual banqueting hall) in the Temple of Shams has painted walls showing **reclining banqueters, vine scrolls, cupids (erotes), amphorae, chariots and figures in Roman dress**, in a style paralleling Fayyūm mummy portraits; pink rose garlands and fruit motifs suggest a funerary register. The best-known fragment, the **"zky" fresco** (likely 1st c. CE, carrying a benediction inscription), shows **camel drivers** and mythological figures in cartouches framed by vines and grape clusters. A separate fragment shows a tower house with its inhabitants. **Palette: mainly white, ochre, red and black, with rare blue** — [Wikipedia, "Qaryat al-Faw"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw); [PSAS, "The mrzḥ of Qaryat al-Fāw"](https://www.archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/2262); [Oxford KRC, "The Art of Qaryat al-Faw"](https://krc.web.ox.ac.uk/node/4191436)
- **Actual surviving textiles and leather** from the period: Hinat's tomb at Jabal Aḥmar, Ḥegra, preserved **textiles, leather, hair, skin and plant matter**; the reconstruction's linen was woven to match recovered fragments, and the earrings copied excavated Ḥegra jewellery — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/paid-content-meeting-hinat)
- **Sculpture and the body:** the monumental **Liḥyānite statue from Dadan** (al-ʿUlā), **5th–3rd c. BCE**, **2.3 m tall, sandstone**, presumed to depict a Liḥyānite king; smooth surface, carefully modelled torso and abdominal musculature, limbs fragmentary; a distinct Liḥyānite sculptural school with possible early Egyptian influence. On five-year loan to the Louvre (7 Sep 2022 – 7 Sep 2027); shown in *Roads of Arabia* in 2010 — [Louvre press release](https://presse.louvre.fr/a-monumental-lihyanite-statuefrom-saudi-arabia-deposited-from-the-royal-commission-for-alula-rcuto-the-musee-du-louvre); **direct images:** [JPG 1](https://presse.louvre.fr/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/1662461337_image-colosse-lihyanite-darabie-dpot-commission-royale-pour-alulajpg.jpg), [JPG 2](https://presse.louvre.fr/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Statue-lihyanite.-Royal-Commission-for-alUla-RCU.2022.275-©-Museee-du-Louvre.-Thierry-Ollivier-2010-2-738x1000.jpg), [press PDF](https://presse.louvre.fr/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/PR-LOUVRE_Monumental-statue-lihyanite-from-AlUla-Saudi-Arabia_Loan-to-Department-of-Near-Eastern-Antiquities_VENG2.pdf)
- A **bronze head of a lioness from Najrān**, long held by the British Museum since the 1930s and returned, is in the Riyadh Museum of Archaeology and Ethnography, alongside rock-art photographs from the national survey and ʿUbaid-period pottery including a finely decorated beaker — [World Archaeology, "Saudi Arabian museums/archaeology"](https://www.world-archaeology.com/features/saudia-arabian-museumsarchaeology/)
- Qaryat al-Fāw small finds include a **hollow glass container (1st c. BCE)**, a **dromedary statue (2nd c. BCE – 2nd c. CE)**, a **bust of Artemis** from the burial area, and a **Maʿīnic inscription on a plaque with two ibexes (1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE)** — [Wikipedia, "Qaryat al-Faw"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw)

### Cited Findings — weapons
- **Al-Kindī's 9th-century treatise is the earliest systematic Arabic source on swords**, and the article states plainly that little is known about the *sayf* (سيف) beyond it — meaning the "pre-Islamic sword" is reconstructed, not documented. Al-Kindī ranks production centres best-first: **Yemen, Khurāsān, Damascus, Egypt, Rūm (Byzantium), Sri Lanka, Qalah (possibly Kedah)** — Yemen first, which supports the Yemeni-blade prestige the poetry assumes, but from a 9th-century vantage — [Wikipedia, "Arab sword"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_sword)
- Early chroniclers (via Zaky) distinguish **sayf anīth** (سيف أنيث, of iron) from **sayf fūlādh / muʾakkad** (سيف فولاذ, of steel) — [Wikipedia, "Arab sword"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_sword)
- The *mashrafiyya* (مشرفية) swords of Muʾta were prized enough that the 629 raid on the city is explained by the desire to capture them — a concrete named sword-type with a place attached — [Wikipedia, "Arab sword"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_sword)
- Blade vocabulary: *miqbaḍ* (مقبض, grip), *ḥalq* (pommel), *ḥāris* (quillon), *akmal* (fuller — some blades flat there instead), and the false vs. true edge (*ẓāfiya* / *ḥaqīqiyya*). Early Muslim Arabs carried swords in **baldrics**, not belts; the belt-and-sabre arrangement is an Abbasid change under al-Mutawakkil. The article explicitly does **not** classify pre-Islamic blades as straight or curved, and does not discuss armour — [Wikipedia, "Arab sword"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_sword)
- A dedicated Arabic study of Yemeni sword names from inscriptions plus Arabic sources exists and is the right place to pursue named swords — [*Jordan Journal for History and Archaeology*, "The Names Given to Ancient Yemeni Swords in the Light of Inscriptions and Arabic Sources"](https://jjournals.ju.edu.jo/index.php/jjha/article/view/1844)
- Armaments production in Yathrib is attributed in the tradition to the Jewish tribes (a monopoly claim) — treat as **[later-source]** — [Delhi Univ.](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)
- Warfare display skills named in the tradition: archery, fencing, horsemanship — [Delhi Univ.](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)

### Inferences
- The honest art-direction rule: **clothing silhouette and drape should be built from the Fāw frescoes and the Liḥyānite sculpture, not from later Islamic miniatures or from modern Gulf dress.** The Fāw material shows a Hellenistic-Roman-inflected visual world in central Arabia — reclining banqueters, vine scrolls, amphorae — which is a genuinely surprising and usable look.
- The restricted Fāw palette (white, ochre, red, black, rare blue) is directly usable as a limited colour script for a visual novel set in the interior; blue reads as expensive.
- Because the sword evidence is 9th-century, the defensible choice is a **broad, straight, double-edged blade carried on a baldric** — that combination is at least positively attested for the earliest Muslim Arabs, hence plausibly continuous with the generation before.

### Gaps
- **Not sourced here:** *izār* (إزار), *ridāʾ* (رداء), *qamīṣ* (قميص), *ʿabāʾa* (عباءة), *burnus* (برنس), *ʿimāma* (عمامة) and turban-winding styles, *burūd ḥibara* (برود حبرة), *ʿaṣb* (عصب) Yemeni cloth, dyes (indigo نيلة, saffron زعفران, madder فوة), footwear. The relevant literature exists — [Stillman/Sanders, *Arab Dress: A Short History* (Brill)](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/publications/arab-dress%3A-a-short-history-from-the-dawn-of-islam-to-modern-times) and an Indonesian-language study of early-Islamic Arab dress — [Thaqāfiyyāt, UIN Sunan Kalijaga](https://ejournal.uin-suka.ac.id/adab/thaqafiyyat/article/view/2325) — but I did not open either in depth.
- *Washm* (وشم, tattooing), kohl (كحل), henna (حناء), perfume and hairstyles: I found only modern-ethnographic and popular-journalism treatments (e.g. [UNESCO Silk Roads on Bedouin jewellery](https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/content/traditional-bedouin-crafts-jewellery); [Abu Dhabi Culture on kohl](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Kohl)), none of which is evidence for 400–630 CE. Flagging rather than asserting.
- Nothing sourced on *dirʿ* (درع) mail, the **Dāwūdiyya** (داوودية), shields, the *mighfar* (مغفر), the *rumḥ* (رمح), or the *qaws* (قوس) and arrow types. Jawād ʿAlī vol. on السلاح and EI² "Silāḥ" / "Dirʿ" are the route.
- No sourced detail on the *qubba* (قبة) as a distinct structure.

## Food and drink: dates, milk, meat, tharīd, wine, maysir, hospitality

### Takeaway
The one food-related institution I can document in rich, scene-ready detail is **maysir** (ميسر) — and it turns out to be a redistribution mechanism as much as a game: ten men, a camel bought on credit, named notched arrows, blanks that lose, and the meat going to the poor while the losers pay. Everything else about the diet is attested only generically in my sources.

### Cited Findings
- **Maysir, from Lane's Lexicon (compiled from the classical Arabic lexica):** ten unfeathered, headless arrows; ten players; a camel bought **on credit** and slaughtered; the carcass divided into **ten portions** (some accounts say twenty-eight). Each arrow has a name and notches matching its share: **al-Fadhdh الفذّ (1), al-Tawʾam التوءم (2), al-Raqīb الرقيب (3), al-Ḥils الحِلس (4), al-Nāfis النافس (5 — some place it fourth), al-Musbil المسبل (6), al-Muʿallā المعلّى (7, the highest)**. The eighth, ninth and tenth — **al-Safīḥ السفيح, al-Manīḥ المنيح, al-Waghd الوغد** — win nothing — [Lane's Lexicon, ميسر](https://www.laneslexicon.com/word/ميسر/n42474)
- **The draw:** arrows placed in a *ribāba* (ربابة), a quiver-like container (*kināna* كنانة); shuffled, sometimes by a person called the *hurḍa*; the *ḥakam* (حكم, judge) draws them one at a time, each in the name of a player, who takes the matching share — [Lane's Lexicon](https://www.laneslexicon.com/word/ميسر/n42474)
- **The social function, explicitly:** those who drew blanks **paid the price of the slaughtered camel**, and **the meat was given to the poor**. A man who refused to play was reproached and called *baram* (بَرَم) — a term of contempt for the non-participant. A *ghufl* (غفل) arrow requiring a fine is also mentioned. Later usage extended *maysir* to any game of hazard: Mujāhid included a children's walnut game, *nard* (نرد, backgammon/tables), and chess, which ʿAlī called "the Persians' maysir" — [Lane's Lexicon](https://www.laneslexicon.com/word/ميسر/n42474)
- Agriculture in the peninsula over the long run: **date-palm gardening, date honey, wheat, barley, cotton**, plus pearl and oyster harvesting on the coasts; sheep, goats and cattle herded in oases and coastal areas — [Hoyland, *Arabia and the Arabs* excerpt](https://courses.edx.org/asset-v1:NotreDameX+TH120.2x+3T2015+type@asset+block/ODL_TH1202x_M02_HoylandChapter9.pdf)
- At Qaryat al-Fāw, the market's standing remains include **grain-storage traces and baking ovens**; debris has buried nearly a full storey of what was a three- or four-level market structure — [Wikipedia, "Qaryat al-Faw"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw)
- **Wine (khamr خمر)** is a settled theme of the poetic corpus — Ṭarafa's *muʿallaqa* celebrates it as part of the heroic life — but Klasova reads this celebration as **defiance of death rather than a straightforward social value**, and historians doubt the poetry reflects pre-Islamic values broadly — [Wikipedia, "Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabic_poetry); [Wikipedia, "Jahiliyyah" (citing Klasova 2019)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyyah)
- The *khamriyya* (خمرية, wine poem) is now studied as a cross-cultural genre with a pre-Islamic starting point — the St Andrews project is the current scholarly hub — [St Andrews, "Khamriyya as a World Poetic Genre"](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/datasets/khamriyya-as-a-world-poetic-genre-comparative-perspectives-on-win/); [St Andrews, "Vanishing wine: from pre-Islamic Arabic poetry…"](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/activities/vanishing-wine-from-pre-islamic-arabic-poetry-to-the-apex-of-otto/)
- **Usury:** *ribā* (ربا) is described as widespread, with lenders typically charging 100 per cent; Q 3:130 prohibits usury "doubled twofold" (أضعافاً مضاعفة) — [Delhi Univ.](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)
- A caution on the hospitality theme: Ibn Isḥāq's catalogue of Jāhilī vices includes **poor treatment of guests** and **broken kinship ties** — the famous Arab hospitality is in part an Islamic-era *contrast* construct, with the sīra tradition itself asserting the opposite about the Jāhiliyya — [Wikipedia, "Jahiliyyah" (citing Guillaume 1955)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyyah)
- A peer-reviewed Arabic-language study specifically on food and food-suppliers in Mecca and Medina in the Prophet's era exists and is the right source for *tharīd*, *ḥays*, *sawīq* etc. — [*Journal of Historical Studies*, Mosul Univ.](https://rjps.uomosul.edu.iq/index.php/jeh/article/view/5267)

### Inferences
- **Maysir is the best single scene-engine in this whole research set.** It is a gambling game, a butchery, a public performance with a named judge, a charity mechanism and a test of masculine honour (refuse and you are a *baram*) all at once, and every prop has a name. The Qurʾānic prohibition then gives it a built-in historical clock.
- The ten-portion camel also quietly tells you the economics: one camel feeds a group well enough to be worth a day's elaborate ritual, and buying it **on credit** means the game is settled afterwards in obligation, not cash.
- Dates, milk and camel/sheep meat as the dietary base is uncontroversial in every handbook; I simply could not find a citable scholarly page stating it, so the report-writer should source it from Jawād ʿAlī or the Mosul article rather than from me.

### Gaps
- **Not sourced:** date varieties and processing, *laban* (لبن), *aqiṭ* (أقط, dried curd), *tharīd* (ثريد), *ḥays* (حيس), *sawīq* (سويق), bread types, game animals (oryx مها, gazelle غزال, ostrich نعام, hare أرنب), locusts (جراد), truffles (كمأة), *nabīdh* (نبيذ), and the **import of wine from Syria and al-Ḥīra**, the tavern and the wine-merchant (خمّار) as poetic figures. All of these are standard in Jawād ʿAlī and in the poetry itself; none is confirmable from what I opened.
- No sourced evidence on hospitality obligations (the three-day guest rule etc.) beyond the Ibn Isḥāq counter-claim above.

## Animals and travel: camels, horses, dogs, caravan logistics, navigation

### Takeaway
Two hard, usable numbers emerge: a day's march (*marḥala*) of **roughly 40–45 km**, and the camel-watering vocabulary, which turns out to be a precise technical system keyed to the **rising of Suhayl (Canopus)** — i.e. desert travel logistics were calendar-linked, not improvised.

### Cited Findings
- **A day's journey.** The *marḥala* (مرحلة) is a day's travel on foot or by camel under ordinary conditions, generally reckoned at **about 40–45 km (24.5–28 miles)**, with estimates varying. Routes — caravan and pilgrimage alike — were measured in these stages, and the geographers al-Balādhurī, Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī and al-Idrīsī use the term for distances between cities and stations — [Wikipedia, "Marhala (unit)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marhala_(unit))
- **Watering intervals — the technical vocabulary, from Lane's Lexicon.** *Ẓimʾ* (ظمء) is the interval between two waterings, counted from when the animals are kept from water until they reach it again; the word also means thirst, and by extension a lifespan. The named intervals:
  - *Ghibb* (غِبّ) — the **shortest**, used in the hottest season: drink one day, graze the next, drink on the third, i.e. water **every second day**.
  - *Ribʿ* (رِبْع) — **after the rising of Suhayl (Canopus)** the interval lengthens: graze two days, come to water on the **fourth** (وردت ربعاً).
  - *Khims* (خِمْس) and *Sids* (سِدْس) — the sequence continues (Lane names but does not define them).
  - *ʿIshr* (عِشْر) — the final and longest stage named.
  - Every such interval, long or short, is a *ẓimʾ* — [Lane's Lexicon, ظمء](https://www.laneslexicon.com/word/ظمء)
- **Camels as strategic infrastructure, not just transport:** Arab tribes supplied camels *and water* to Assyrian and Persian armies; Sasanian counter-nomad campaigns in east Arabia included **destroying wells and cisterns** — water infrastructure was a military target — [Hoyland excerpt](https://courses.edx.org/asset-v1:NotreDameX+TH120.2x+3T2015+type@asset+block/ODL_TH1202x_M02_HoylandChapter9.pdf)
- A dromedary statue (2nd c. BCE – 2nd c. CE) and fresco **camel drivers** from Qaryat al-Fāw are direct period images of camels and the men who handled them — [Wikipedia, "Qaryat al-Faw"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw)
- **The shadād (شداد) saddle** is treated as a deliberate technological invention for desert conditions, not a generic piece of tack — [Arab News, "Shaddad: Traditional invention…"](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2607796/saudi-arabia)
- **The salūqī (سلوقي).** Smooth-haired type: long narrow muzzle, deep chest, fine waist, low-set hocks, **23–28 inches at the shoulder**, any colour accepted but grey-to-white with grey markings commonest in Saudi Arabia; a larger reddish longer-haired type with feathered ears and tail is bred in Kuwait. Believed to hunt by sight. Training starts at **three months** on desert rats, sometimes alongside a falcon; by **six months** it hunts hare, by **eighteen months** larger game; estimated at **about 40 mph**, the female often faster. Quarry: mainly **hare and gazelle**. Not a pet: women and children feed and care for the dogs, bloodlines are a matter of pride, good puppies are **given, not sold**, and trainers do not beat them. Arabic: *salūqī* masc. sg., *silāqa* fem., plurals *sulqān* and *salaq*. Ancient evidence: Egyptian tomb paintings of salūqīs coursing gazelle, mummified examples, older carvings, and descriptions in pre-Islamic poetry — [Aramco World, "Greyhound of the Desert," 1961](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/196105/greyhound.of.the.desert.htm)
- **Hunting on the epigraphic record** is a studied subject in its own right — there is a paper specifically on hunting in pre-Islamic Arabia from the inscriptions, and the Safaitic/Hismaic rock-art record includes hunting scenes; I could not open the paper (library bot-wall) but the OCIANA corpus itself is browsable — [record (access-blocked)](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1700122673); [Macdonald 1996 citation in OCIANA](https://ociana.osu.edu/citations/851); [OCIANA](https://ociana.osu.edu/)

### Inferences
- **The Suhayl-keyed watering schedule is the most useful single travel mechanic found.** It means a journey's shape — how far you can get between wells, how long the herd can be off water — changes with the star calendar, so the same route is a different problem in high summer (*ghibb*, water every second day) than after Canopus rises (*ribʿ*, water every fourth). For a travelling-couple narrative this is a plot clock built into the landscape.
- 40–45 km/day gives workable arithmetic: Mecca–Medina (~450 km direct) is on the order of ten to twelve stages; the user can price any journey in days without inventing numbers.
- The salūqī detail that **dogs are given and not sold**, and that **women and children feed them**, is a small domestic texture that also encodes a gift-economy value — a good, cheap characterisation beat.

### Gaps
- **Not sourced:** the *naqa* (ناقة) vs *jamal* (جمل) distinction and named breeds, the *hawdaj* (هودج) howdah, the *raḥl* (رحل), branding (*wasm* وسم), grazing-cycle terminology, horses' prestige and **named horses**, falconry, the *dalīl* (دليل) guide and star-navigation practice, the *manāzil* (منازل) and water stops of specific routes, and route dangers. The relevant period study is Robert Bulliet-adjacent work; see also [Ricks/UChicago, "Camels and Camel Pastoralism in Arabia"](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/3210371), which I did not open.
- The *anwāʾ* lunar-mansion system (below) is the mechanism behind the Suhayl reckoning, but I found no source tying the two explicitly.

## Economy and trade: routes, Meccan trade and Crone, the fairs, oases and irrigation

### Takeaway
**Crone's argument is narrower than it is usually reported.** She does not deny Meccan trade; she denies that it explains the rise of Islam, on the grounds that the goods were modest rather than luxury, that the commercial boom was too short to dissolve tribal order, and that Muḥammad's message succeeded in Medina while Mecca "had to be conquered before they converted." On the fairs, only ʿUkāẓ is documented in any detail; the standard seven-or-eight-fair calendar is a later-source scheme I could not verify.

### Cited Findings
- **Crone's critique (target: W. M. Watt's social-malaise thesis).** (1) *Goods*: Meccan trade dealt in **modest goods, not luxuries**, which weakens the thesis though she concedes it is not fatal on its own. (2) *Timing*: a relatively brief commercial boom is unlikely to dissolve tribal organisation — she compares **19th-century Ḥāʾil**, which grew fast without comparable breakdown. (3) *Evidence of malaise*: the Qurʾān shows no change in attitudes to wealth or the poor, since complaints about greed were standard monotheist preaching; the tradition portrays Mecca as prosperous and tribally intact. (4) *Geography*: the message succeeded in **Medina**, not Mecca — the Meccans had to be conquered first — so the problems addressed were general Arabian, not Meccan. (5) *Feuding* was a constant of settled Arabian life, not a by-product of sedentarisation; what was new was the idea of **divinely validated state structures**. (6) *No spiritual crisis*: tribal gods were practical providers of rain and fertility, not answers about the afterlife — [Crone, *Meccan Trade*, excerpt at Fordham Sourcebook](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/med/crone.asp)
- **Crone's alternative:** political and military explanation — Muḥammad preached state formation and conquest, which fitted tribal militancy and interest; Persian and Byzantine influence in Arabia may have catalysed a **nativist movement**. On this view Meccan trade might explain how Islam *spread* but not why it *emerged* — [Fordham/Crone](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/med/crone.asp)
- Crone's companion article on **the Qurashī leather trade** is open-access at the IAS and is the place to see her positive account of what Mecca actually traded — [Crone, "Quraysh and the Roman Army" / leather trade, IAS PDF](https://www.ias.edu/sites/default/files/hs/Crone_Articles/Crone_Quraysh_Leather_Trade.pdf)
- **Sūq ʿUkāẓ (سوق عكاظ):** between Nakhla and al-Ṭāʾif in the Ḥijāz (21°28′28″N, 40°38′42″E); a seasonal market running **21 days each year in Dhū al-Qaʿda (ذو القعدة), immediately before the Ḥajj**; active c. **542–726 CE**, sacked by the Khārijites in **127 AH (725–726 CE)**. Its position on the western-Arabian spice route drove its 6th-century growth. Functions beyond commerce: **settling disputes, concluding treaties, poetry competitions, sporting events and religious gatherings**. **Majanna (مجنّة) and Dhū al-Majāz (ذو المجاز)** were both held near Mecca at the same season and competed with it — [Wikipedia, "Souk Okaz"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souk_Okaz)
- **Meccan caravan seasons** in the tradition: to **Syria in summer** and to **Yemen in winter**, with traffic also reaching Bahrain and Iraq. **[later-source]** — [Delhi Univ.](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)
- **The long-run incense economy:** frankincense and myrrh from south Arabia fed Mediterranean and Mesopotamian demand; the **Minaeans** ran caravans and reportedly held the frankincense trade through **hereditary families**. **Gerrha** in east Arabia handled aromatics and Indian goods, shifting between sea, Euphrates and overland routes as conditions changed. **Magan (Oman)** mined and smelted copper, with roughly **150 smelting sites** recorded; **Dilmun (Bahrain)** was the Bronze Age hub — [Hoyland excerpt](https://courses.edx.org/asset-v1:NotreDameX+TH120.2x+3T2015+type@asset+block/ODL_TH1202x_M02_HoylandChapter9.pdf)
- **Coinage circulated in Arabia**: Mesopotamian and later issues, including **Characene and Gerrhaean** coins, evidencing regional monetary exchange rather than pure barter — [Hoyland excerpt](https://courses.edx.org/asset-v1:NotreDameX+TH120.2x+3T2015+type@asset+block/ODL_TH1202x_M02_HoylandChapter9.pdf)
- **Imports found in Arabian excavations:** copper tools, Kassite seals, Bactrian combs, south Arabian alabaster vessels, Greek and Roman imports — a materially cosmopolitan record — [Hoyland excerpt](https://courses.edx.org/asset-v1:NotreDameX+TH120.2x+3T2015+type@asset+block/ODL_TH1202x_M02_HoylandChapter9.pdf)
- **Qaryat al-Fāw's water system:** planned canals and cisterns, a vast irrigation network, large ancient plantation pits, and **more than seventeen wells**; the city appeared in the mid-1st millennium BCE, lasted nearly a millennium, and was **abruptly abandoned around the 5th century CE after water resources were irreversibly depleted** — a documented case of oasis collapse inside the user's period — [UNESCO WHC 1712](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1712); [Wikipedia, "Qaryat al-Faw"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw)
- **Maʾrib:** the Sabaean dam fed canals irrigating a large area north and south of the city — described as **the largest artificial oasis in ancient Arabia**. Dating is contested: one framing puts major construction in the early 1st millennium BCE with expansion in the Classical Sabaean period (c. 800–400 BCE); another puts its active life "between the beginning of the Christian era and the end of the third century CE." Under Ḥimyarite rule reduced maintenance brought intermittent damage, and **the final catastrophic breach is traditionally dated to the late 6th century CE** — i.e. within living memory for a character in 630. The **Barrān temple (ʿArsh Bilqīs)** is known for **five columns each over twelve metres high**; the **Awwām temple (Maḥram Bilqīs)** was dedicated to Almaqah. The historic city had **multi-storey mud-brick buildings** — [Madain Project, "Marib"](https://madainproject.com/marib); [UNESCO WHC 1700, "Landmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib"](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1700) (inscribed 2023, seven component sites; physical fabric in very poor condition, war- and development-threatened)
- **Khaybar's dam, Sadd al-Bint**, is documented as a pre-Islamic hydraulic work in the northern Ḥijāz — [Madain Project, "Sadd al-Bint (Khaybar Dam)"](https://madainproject.com/sadd_al_bint_(khaybar_dam))
- South Arabian polities were organised as **cult communities**, each bound to a patron god and ruled by a king (*mlk* ملك), sometimes titled "unifier" (*mkrb* مكرب); the Sabaeans called themselves the **"progeny" (wld) of Almaqah**, and a wider federation, "Saba and the union" (*gw*), renewed its ties at a ceremony for ʿAthtar — [Hoyland excerpt](https://courses.edx.org/asset-v1:NotreDameX+TH120.2x+3T2015+type@asset+block/ODL_TH1202x_M02_HoylandChapter9.pdf)

### Inferences
- The **Dhū al-Qaʿda dating of ʿUkāẓ** is the key calendar fact for travel plotting: the fair sits in the sacred month immediately before the pilgrimage, so the whole year's long-distance movement funnels toward one season. A travelling couple's itinerary realistically bends toward that window.
- ʿUkāẓ as a **court, treaty-ground, poetry arena and sports meet** as much as a market is the single richest setting in the research: it legitimately hosts arbitration, poetic duels, reunions and commerce in one place, which solves a lot of staging problems.
- Qaryat al-Fāw's abandonment "around the 5th century CE" from water depletion is, for a story opening around 400–500, a **living catastrophe** — an abandoned great city within memory, with a drying irrigation network, is period-accurate and visually powerful.
- Crone's point that Meccan goods were modest is worth absorbing for art direction: the caravan should look like bulk leather, hides, raisins and cloth, not chests of jewels.

### Gaps
- **The fair calendar is largely unverified.** I confirmed only ʿUkāẓ (dates, duration, location, functions) and the bare existence of Majanna and Dhū al-Majāz. **Dūmat al-Jandal (دومة الجندل), Hajar (هجر), al-Mushaqqar (المشقر), ʿAdan (عدن) and Ṣanʿāʾ (صنعاء)** as fair sites, their months and their goods — not found. These come from al-Marzūqī's *Kitāb al-Azmina wa-l-Amkina* and Jawād ʿAlī; the report-writer should source them there, not from me.
- No sourced detail on weights and measures, or on al-Ṭāʾif / Yathrib / Taymāʾ agriculture specifically, or on *falaj* (فلج) / *qanāt* (قناة) construction technique.
- Michael Lecker's work (on Medina's agriculture and the Jewish tribes) did not surface in my searches and should be pursued directly.

## Culture and entertainment: music, the qayna, the rāwī, poetry contests, games

### Takeaway
The transmission chain — poet, *rāwī*, compiler — is well documented and, crucially, the *rāwī* is now understood as a **reviser and interpolator**, not a tape recorder; this is the single most important methodological fact for anyone writing scenes about poetry. The *qayna* is attested but the scholarship I could reach is thin and literary.

### Cited Findings
- **The rāwī (راوي) / ruwāt (رواة).** A distinct profession from the poet, though one person could be both; transmitters were usually tribally affiliated and are often named collectively and anonymously in the sources — e.g. "the transmitters of Ṭayʾ" (رواة طيء). **Khalaf al-Aḥmar said transmitters revised poets' work even in antiquity.** Named mid-8th-century figures: **Ḥammād al-Rāwiya, Khalaf al-Aḥmar, Abū ʿAmr b. al-ʿAlāʾ** — [Wikipedia, "Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabic_poetry); see also [Rāwī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C4%81w%C4%AB)
- **Three roles in the chain:** the poet who composed and memorised; the transmitter who preserved (and altered); the scholar who compiled written *dīwāns* from the 8th century. Tribes kept poetry as a **record of deeds and genealogy**, one generation reciting the previous generation's odes. Metre and rhyme aided memorisation and made unauthorised substitutions harder, since they broke the verse — [Wikipedia, "Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabic_poetry)
- **The authenticity debate, current state.** Scepticism began with **D. S. Margoliouth (1925)** and **Ṭāhā Ḥusayn (1926)**; Arafat replied to blanket rejection; recent historians have moved away from blanket scepticism and treat most poems as potentially genuinely pre-Islamic. **Reliability is genre- and transmission-circle-specific:** philologists' *dīwāns* are usually reliable, poetry quoted in *tafsīr* and chronicles usually is not. Positive indicators: toponyms naming real but later-unknown places, archaic grammatical forms, the archaic ethnonym **Maʿadd**. Warning signs: overtly Qurʾānic phrasing, or verses deployed to support political or exegetical claims. Al-Aṣmaʿī thought much attributed to **Imruʾ al-Qays** was actually composed by his wandering companions — [Wikipedia, "Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabic_poetry)
- **Qaṣīda architecture as usable dramatic structure:** *nasīb* (نسيب, prelude on lost lovers and abandoned camp-ruins) → *raḥīl* (رحيل, the journey, with the camel or horse) → *fakhr* (فخر, tribal boast) as the closing movement; *hijāʾ* (هجاء, invective) as the paired aggressive mode. Ṭarafa's *muʿallaqa* compares a racing horse to a wolf startled at a watering place — the kind of image the genre runs on — [Wikipedia, "Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabic_poetry)
- Later Islamic-era critics treated the Jāhilī poets as **the standard against which all later poets were measured** — which is itself a reason the corpus was preserved and also edited — [Wikipedia, "Jahiliyyah" (citing Miller 2024)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyyah)
- The root *j-h-l* (جهل) appears in the poetry of **Imruʾ al-Qays and al-Nābigha**; scholars disagree whether it means barbarism or ignorance — [Wikipedia, "Jahiliyyah"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyyah)
- **Nard (نرد) and chess** are classed with *maysir* in later juristic discussion — ʿAlī is quoted calling chess "the Persians' maysir," which places chess as a recognisably **Persian** import rather than an old Arabian game — [Lane's Lexicon, ميسر](https://www.laneslexicon.com/word/ميسر/n42474)
- **The qayna (قينة):** the one scholarly treatment I located focuses on *al-Jarādatān* (الجرادتان, "the two grasshoppers" / Jarādatā ʿĀd) — described as the two most famous female singing-slaves of the pre-Islamic era — reconstructed from **al-Ṭabarī's *Taʾrīkh*** and **al-Masʿūdī's *Murūj al-dhahab***, i.e. from Abbasid chronicles, and read partly as a myth linking song, drought and migration. Only the abstract is accessible — [*Journal of Arabic and Islamic Studies*, "La présence des qiyān à l'époque préislamique"](https://journals.uio.no/JAIS/article/view/10191)
- A general survey of Islamicate music history with a pre-Islamic sources section is available open — [Univ. of Alberta, "Pre-Islamic sources and Islamicate music"](https://www.artsrn.ualberta.ca/fwa_mediawiki/index.php/Islamic_sources_and_Islamicate_music); [overview](https://www.artsrn.ualberta.ca/ccewiki/index.php/The_history_of_Islamicate_music)
- The claim that the **Muʿallaqāt were hung in the Kaʿba** is repeated in teaching material, but note that "Suspended Odes" is itself a later explanatory gloss on the collection's name and should be presented as tradition, not fact — [Delhi Univ.](https://www.du.ac.in/du/uploads/departments/arabic/MA-Arabic-2nd-4th-Sem/Arabia%20before%20Islam.pdf)

### Inferences
- Writing a *rāwī* character is strongly supported and dramatically rich precisely because the role is **not** faithful preservation: a transmitter who improves, trims or politicises his poet's verse is historically accurate, and makes the authenticity debate a character conflict rather than a footnote.
- The *nasīb*'s abandoned campsite (الأطلال) is the genre's own opening move and maps almost exactly onto a visual novel's establishing shot — a couple arriving at the traces of a camp is both a literal travel beat and a citation of the form.

### Gaps
- **Not sourced:** the instruments — *ʿūd* (عود) / *mizhar* (مزهر), *duff* (دف), *miʿzafa* (معزفة), *nāy* (ناي) — their forms or attestation; the *qiyān* of **al-Ḥīra** and Mecca specifically; the **ḥidāʾ** (حداء) caravan chant; *munāfara* (منافرة) and *mufākhara* (مفاخرة) boasting contests; *naqāʾiḍ* (نقائض) poetry duels; horse-racing and archery contests; proverbs and the *qāṣṣ* storyteller. The Wikipedia poetry article explicitly does **not** cover munāfara, mufākhara, naqāʾiḍ or taverns.

## Calendar and timekeeping: months, nasīʾ, the anwāʾ, year-reckoning

### Takeaway
There are **two month-name systems** in the sources — an older set preserved by the lexicographers and the familiar Islamic set — and the *nasīʾ* question is genuinely unresolved: EI² and many historians hold Central Arabia used a **purely lunar** calendar in which nasīʾ merely shifted the sacred months, while al-Bīrūnī, al-Masʿūdī and Abū Maʿshar's tradition holds the calendar had become **lunisolar** with intercalary months. The *anwāʾ* system, by contrast, is independently confirmed by Safaitic inscriptions and is the real working calendar of desert life.

### Cited Findings
- **Older (pre-Islamic) month names paired with the Islamic ones**, from lexicographers including **Kurāʿ al-Naml** and **Ibn Manẓūr**, with al-Bīrūnī and al-Masʿūdī noting the Arabs also used the Islamic names: *muʾtamir / al-muʾtamir* (مؤتمر) = al-Muḥarram (المحرّم); *nājir* (ناجر) = Ṣafar (صفر); *khawwān / khuwwān* (خوّان) = Rabīʿ al-Awwal (ربيع الأول); *wabṣān* (وبصان) = Rabīʿ al-Ākhir (ربيع الآخر); *ḥanīn* (حنين) = Jumādā al-Ūlā (جمادى الأولى); *rubbā* (ربّى) = Jumādā al-Ākhira (جمادى الآخرة); *al-aṣamm / munṣil al-asinna* (الأصمّ / منصل الأسنّة, "the deaf one" / "the un-sheather of spearheads") = Rajab (رجب); *ʿādhil* (عاذل) = Shaʿbān (شعبان); *nātiq* (ناتق) = Ramaḍān (رمضان); *waʿl / waʿil* (وعل) = Shawwāl (شوّال); *warna* (ورنة) = Dhū al-Qaʿda (ذو القعدة); *burak / maymūn* (برك / ميمون) = Dhū al-Ḥijja (ذو الحجّة) — [Wikipedia, "Calendars in pre-Islamic Arabia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendars_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- **Nasīʾ (نسيء) — the two positions.** *Purely lunar view:* nasīʾ ("postponement") only shifted the forbidden months within the year to suit the pilgrimage, without manipulating the calendar; the **Encyclopaedia of Islam** supports this, and an **early Sabaic inscription recording the postponement of a ritual because of war** is cited as a parallel. *Lunisolar view:* about 200 years before the Hijra the calendar became lunisolar, with intercalary months inserted to keep the pilgrimage in the profitable trade season; here nasīʾ means intercalation (*kabīsa* كبيسة). **Abū Maʿshar al-Balkhī** proposed this and **al-Bīrūnī** and **al-Masʿūdī** agreed; the Arabs may have learned it from Jews. The **Kināna** tribe administered the practice, having taken it over from **Kinda**. The schemes disagree (seven intercalary months in 19 years, or nine in 24) and there is **no scholarly consensus** — [Wikipedia, "Calendars in pre-Islamic Arabia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendars_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia); [Wikipedia, "Nasi'"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasi%27); Qurʾānic locus **Q 9:37** — [parallel translations](https://alim.org/quran/compare/surah/9/37)
- **The anwāʾ (أنواء).** Asterisms on or near the zodiac belt; described as **the most important element of pre-Islamic astronomy**. Tied to the seasons and used to forecast **rain, temperature and wind**. Diviners invoked them in rainmaking rituals (*istisqāʾ* استسقاء). **Safaitic texts time rituals to the sun's position in particular anwāʾ**, e.g. Virgo (*ngm*) or Sagittarius (*ṯbr*) — this is contemporary epigraphic confirmation, not later report — [Wikipedia, "Calendars in pre-Islamic Arabia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendars_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- **The seasonal year of the Safaitic nomads — four seasons, in their own words:** *s2ty* (شتي, winter), *dṯʔ* (the later rains / spring), *ṣyf* (صيف, early summer), *qyẓ* (قيظ, the dry season). The Rwala Bedouin add a fifth, *aṣ-ṣferī* (الصفري) — [Wikipedia, "Calendars in pre-Islamic Arabia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendars_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- **Era-dating in practice:** after Rome annexed the Nabataean kingdom in **106 CE**, years were counted from that date in the **Arabian (Bostran) era**, with lunar months against the Roman solar framework; **Najrān inscriptions combine lunar months with the Roman provincial era** — so formal dated reckoning did exist in Arabia, in the north and south-west — [Wikipedia, "Calendars in pre-Islamic Arabia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendars_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- Note the **ribʿ/Suhayl link** above: Canopus's rising is the pivot for lengthening camel-watering intervals, which ties the anwāʾ system directly to travel logistics — [Lane's Lexicon, ظمء](https://www.laneslexicon.com/word/ظمء)

### Inferences
- *Munṣil al-asinna* ("the un-sheather / remover of spearheads") as the old name for Rajab is a vivid, usable detail: the month-name itself encodes the sacred truce in which spearheads come off.
- The Safaitic four-season vocabulary is the right seasonal frame for a nomad-focused story — it is what desert-dwellers of the period actually wrote, as against the twelve-month urban scheme.
- Because nasīʾ is unresolved, a story can show the pilgrimage season drifting relative to the solar year and a tribe (Kināna) claiming authority over the adjustment, without committing to either scholarly camp.

### Gaps
- **Year of the Elephant (عام الفيل) and Year of the Flood (عام السيل) not sourced.** The Wikipedia calendar article does not mention event-dating at all, so I cannot document the practice of reckoning years by famous events or the dates assigned to these two. This is a real gap and should be filled from EI² or Jawād ʿAlī.

## Archaeology and visual references for art direction (with URLs)

### Takeaway
For the user's purposes the usable visual corpus is: **Qaryat al-Fāw** (the only substantial *painted* images of people from the Arabian interior), **Ḥegra/al-ʿUlā** (surviving textiles, leather, jewellery, and a forensically reconstructed face), **the Liḥyānite colossus at the Louvre** (the body, monumental), **Maʾrib** (monumental architecture and hydraulics), and the **OCIANA inscription corpus** (free, downloadable photographs and facsimiles of rock texts and associated drawings).

### Cited Findings — sites and what they show
- **Qaryat al-Fāw (قرية الفاو), UNESCO site 1712** (inscribed 2024). City emerged mid-1st millennium BCE, lasted nearly a millennium, **abruptly abandoned c. 5th c. CE** on water depletion. Excavated by **King Saud University, 1970–2003**. Documented: residential quarter with **houses, squares, streets and a market**; **five temples** to different deities and periods; stone burial chambers topped by mausolea or towers for kings and nobility, with noblemen's and warrior tombs along the eastern periphery and the king's tomb separately to the northwest; funerary "avenues" of stone structures from the later 3rd to early 2nd millennium BCE; tumuli at the foothills of Jabal Ṭuwayq (2000–1900 BCE); forts/caravanserais; the sacred mountain Khashm Qaryah; **nearly 12,000 archaeological remains** in total. The **Temple of Wadd** is a rectangular limestone-ashlar building **30.75 × 25.20 m** with corner and intermediate towers, beside the palace and a fortified castle — [UNESCO WHC 1712](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1712) · **gallery: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1712/gallery/** · [Wikipedia, "Qaryat al-Faw"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw)
  - Scholarly image/analysis sources for the frescoes: [PSAS, "The mrzḥ of Qaryat al-Fāw"](https://www.archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/2262) · [Oxford Khalili Research Centre, "The Art of Qaryat al-Faw"](https://krc.web.ox.ac.uk/node/4191436) · [Oxford ORA conference item](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:4c46fb55-8a4d-4fa8-a2de-fa515383d871)

### Inferences
- The art-direction hierarchy that follows from the evidence: **OCIANA for what nomads drew and wrote about themselves** (camels, hunting, raiding, by their own hands, free to download at publication resolution) → **Qaryat al-Fāw frescoes for interior/urban scenes, clothing and bodies** → **Ḥegra textiles and Hinat for a real individual's clothing and face** → **the Liḥyānite colossus for monumental scale and the sculptural body** → **Maʾrib for architecture**. That sequence moves from nomad self-representation to settled monumentality, which maps well onto a travel narrative crossing between the two worlds.
- The nomad/settled interface the user asks about is **physically legible at Qaryat al-Fāw**: a walled multi-storey market and five temples sitting on a caravan route at the edge of the Empty Quarter, with forts/caravanserais, and then abandoned when the water failed. That one site contains the whole thesis.

### Gaps
- No single museum has a **searchable online database of pre-Islamic Arabian objects** that I could confirm. The Louvre's own collections portal did not surface a pre-Islamic Arabia landing page in my searches; the best image access remains the press release above, the Google Arts & Culture Ḥegra story, the UNESCO galleries, Factum Foundation, and OCIANA.
- I could not confirm whether the **Hinat bust** is on public display, or where.
- **Taymāʾ (تيماء)** excavation results (the German–Saudi project) did not surface in my searches and remain unsourced here, despite being one of the best-published sites in the region.
- **No reconstruction drawings** of pre-Islamic Arabian domestic interiors from a scholarly source were located; the Fāw tower-house fresco fragment is the closest thing to a period depiction of a dwelling.
