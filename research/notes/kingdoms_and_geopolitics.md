# Kingdoms, Dynasties and Geopolitics of Arabia and its Borderlands, c. 300–632 CE

**Evidence-grade key used throughout:**
- **[EPI]** = fixed by a dated inscription (Sabaic, Nabataeo-Arabic, Greek, Paleo-Arabic)
- **[EXT]** = from a Byzantine / Syriac / Greek / Persian external source (Procopius, Malalas, John of Ephesus, Theophylact, Simeon of Bēth Arshām, Book of the Ḥimyarites, Sebeos)
- **[TRAD]** = from the later Arabic-Islamic tradition (Ibn Isḥāq, al-Ṭabarī, Hishām ibn al-Kalbī, al-Hamdānī, al-Iṣfahānī) — generally not independently datable
- **[RECON]** = a modern scholarly reconstruction, with the scholar named where possible

**A warning for the whole file:** the regnal tables for the Lakhmids and Ghassanids that circulate everywhere (including the Wikipedia tables reproduced below) derive ultimately from Ḥamza al-Iṣfahānī and similar late sources. They are **[TRAD]+[RECON]**, internally tidy and externally unverifiable before about 500 CE. Only a handful of individual rulers are **[EXT]**-anchored. This is flagged ruler by ruler.

## Q1. The Lakhmids / Naṣrids of al-Ḥīra (اللخميون / الحيرة)

### Takeaway
The Lakhmid king-list is a **[TRAD]** construct with firm **[EXT]** anchors only from c. 503 onward; the one early fixed point is the Namāra inscription of 328 CE. Al-Ḥīra lay c. 7 km ESE of modern Najaf on the desert fringe of lower Mesopotamia; the dynasty ended when Khusraw II deposed and executed al-Nuʿmān III c. 602 and installed Persian governors.

### Cited Findings

**The king-list (conventional, from the Arabic tradition):** ʿAmr I ibn ʿAdī 268–295; Imruʾ al-Qays I ibn ʿAmr 295–328; ʿAmr II ibn Imruʾ al-Qays 328–363; Aws ibn Qallām (non-dynastic) 363–368; Imruʾ al-Qays II ibn ʿAmr 368–390; al-Nuʿmān I ibn Imruʾ al-Qays 390–418; al-Mundhir I ibn al-Nuʿmān 418–462; al-Aswad ibn al-Mundhir 462–490; al-Mundhir II ibn al-Mundhir 490–497; al-Nuʿmān II ibn al-Aswad 497–503; Abū Yaʿfur ibn ʿAlqama (non-dynastic, uncertain) 503–505; **al-Mundhir III ibn al-Nuʿmān (المنذر بن النعمان) 503/505–554**; **ʿAmr III ibn al-Mundhir ("ʿAmr ibn Hind", عمرو بن هند) 554–569**; Qābūs ibn al-Mundhir (قابوس) 569–573; Suhrāb (Persian governor) 573–574; al-Mundhir IV ibn al-Mundhir 574–580; **al-Nuʿmān III ibn al-Mundhir (النعمان بن المنذر) 580–602**; Iyās ibn Qabīṣa al-Ṭāʾī (إياس بن قبيصة) with the Persian Nakhīragān 602–617/18; Āzādbeh (Persian governor) 617/18–633 — [Wikipedia, Lakhmid kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakhmid_kingdom). **[TRAD]/[RECON]**
- The same page notes that the dates given for the first king conflict with its own text (which puts him at c. 293–302), and that calling the whole line "Naṣrid" is probably anachronistic — [Wikipedia, Lakhmid kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakhmid_kingdom). **Useful caution: the table is not a chronicle.**
- Encyclopaedia Iranica gives **no full king-list at all**, saying only that Lakhmid chiefs seem to have controlled Ḥīra "from the late 4th century" and that they often resided in nearby palaces rather than in the city itself. The only named ruler with a date is Nuʿmān III, deposed in 602 — [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ḥira"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hira). **This divergence between Iranica and the popular tables is the single most important chronological caveat for the Lakhmids.**

**Imruʾ al-Qays ibn ʿAmr and the Namāra inscription (نقش النمارة):**
- Dated **7 Kaslūl, year 223 of the Bosra era = 328 CE** (223 + 105) — [Islamic Awareness, Namārah inscription](https://islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/namarah); [Wikipedia, Namara inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namara_inscription). **[EPI] — this is the hardest single date in the whole 4th-century Arabian record.**
- Findspot: c. 100 km SE of Damascus (al-Namāra, in the Ḥarrah basalt); now in the **Louvre** — [Islamic Awareness](https://islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/namarah).
- Written in **Arabic language in Nabataean script** — one of only two such texts, the other being the ʿEn ʿAvdat inscription (1st–2nd c. CE) — [Islamic Awareness](https://islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/namarah).
- Content (per J. A. Bellamy's 1985 reading): it is the **funerary monument of Imruʾ al-Qays son of ʿAmr, "king of all the Arabs"**; he subdued Asad, defeated Madhḥij and drove them to the gates of **Najrān**, subdued **Maʿadd (معد)**, and appointed tribal nobles as viceroys who acted as **phylarchs for the Romans**; no king equalled his achievements — [Islamic Awareness](https://islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/namarah). *Paraphrase; the uncertain part of the title is flagged with a question mark in the source.*
- **Dramatically important ambiguity for a storyteller:** the inscription makes him a Roman-aligned figure (his viceroys become Roman phylarchs), while the Arabic tradition makes the Lakhmids Persian clients throughout. Bellamy's reading itself replaced earlier differing readings — [Islamic Awareness](https://islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/namarah).

**Al-Mundhir III ibn al-Nuʿmān (r. 503/505–554):**
- Raided Palaestina Salutaris and Arabia Petraea in **503** during the Anastasian War; his raids ranged from the Euphrates to Egypt and south into Najd — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III ibn al-Nu'man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Nu%27man). **[EXT]**
- **521:** the Ḥimyarites, answering appeals from central Arabian tribes, drove the Lakhmids out of Najd — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Nu%27man).
- **526:** ravaged Syria and captured the Roman commanders Timostratus and John; **528:** raided Syria again; **529:** took Khabour, raided Arzona and Nisibis, reached Apamea, but failed at Antioch; returned with booty including 400 nuns, reportedly sacrificed to **al-ʿUzzā** — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Nu%27man). **[EXT]**
- **531: Battle of Callinicum** — he fought with the Persians and helped defeat Belisarius; Belisarius was dismissed after a commission of inquiry — [Wikipedia, Iberian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_War). **[EXT]**
- Justinian sent a peace embassy under Abraham son of Euphrasius, accompanied by **Simeon of Bēth Arshām** (the same figure who wrote the Najrān letter) — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Nu%27man). **A direct narrative link between the Ḥīra court and the Najrān atrocity.**
- **By 561 Rome was paying him tribute** — [Wikipedia, Lakhmid kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakhmid_kingdom). *Note: this postdates his death in 554 in the same source's own table — a visible internal inconsistency; treat with suspicion.*
- **Death: June 554, at Yawm Ḥalīma (يوم حليمة) near Chalcis**, killed fighting al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala the Ghassanid — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Nu%27man); [Wikipedia, al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah). **[EXT] + [TRAD]** (the name Yawm Ḥalīma is from the Arabic tradition; the battle near Chalcis in 554 is **[EXT]**).

**ʿAmr ibn Hind (ʿAmr III, r. 554–569/570):**
- Reign **554–569 (or 570)**; called "ibn Hind" after his mother **Hind bint al-Ḥārith**, a **Kindite** princess — [Wikipedia, Amr ibn Hind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amr_ibn_Hind). **The Lakhmid–Kindite dynastic link in one person.**
- **Sayf ibn Dhī Yazan** came to his court seeking Sasanian help against Aksumite rule in Yemen; ʿAmr brought him to an audience with **Khusraw I**, who then sent the expedition that conquered Yemen — [Wikipedia, Amr ibn Hind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amr_ibn_Hind). **[TRAD]** — but it is the hinge connecting al-Ḥīra, Ctesiphon and Ṣanʿāʾ.
- His court hosted the poets **ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm** and **Ṭarafa**. Ṭarafa was sent to the governor of Bahrayn with a sealed letter ordering his own death; **al-Mutalammis**, given the same errand, destroyed his letter and survived — [Wikipedia, Amr ibn Hind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amr_ibn_Hind). **[TRAD]**
- Killed by ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm in **569 or 570** at dinner, after Hind insulted Kulthūm's mother; succeeded by his brother Qābūs — [Wikipedia, Amr ibn Hind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amr_ibn_Hind). **[TRAD]**

**Al-Nuʿmān III ibn al-Mundhir (r. 580–602) and the Persian takeover:**
- Reign given as **580–602** in the infobox but **582–c. 602** in the lead of the same article; modern scholars place the end c. 602 — [Wikipedia, al-Nu'man III ibn al-Mundhir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nu%27man_III_ibn_al-Mundhir). **Flag this 580/582 discrepancy.**
- He was the **first Lakhmid king to convert openly to (Nestorian / East Syriac) Christianity**, probably after the **591** peace with Byzantium; al-Ḥīra then became a major Nestorian centre with a bishopric and missions into the Gulf and eastern Arabia — [Wikipedia, al-Nu'man III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nu%27man_III_ibn_al-Mundhir).
- **590:** sheltered Khusraw II during his flight from Bahrām Chōbīn and fought beside him at al-Nahrawān — [Wikipedia, al-Nu'man III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nu%27man_III_ibn_al-Mundhir). **[EXT]**
- Cause of the breach: the Arabic sources blame his refusal to give Khusraw his horse, or to marry his daughter **Hind** to a relative of the king; the article judges the **ʿAdī ibn Zayd affair** the likelier cause — ʿAdī, al-Nuʿmān's chief councillor, was executed on suspicion of plotting, and ʿAdī's son had Khusraw's ear — [Wikipedia, al-Nu'man III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nu%27man_III_ibn_al-Mundhir). **[TRAD], with the "refused the daughter" story treated as suspect by historians** — [Wikipedia, Lakhmid kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakhmid_kingdom).
- **Execution: crushed by elephants** after fleeing to the Banū Bakr and being forced to surrender; two Syriac chronicles give rival versions (a feast at which he was dishonoured and trapped; or capture with his sons, who were then poisoned) — [Wikipedia, al-Nu'man III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nu%27man_III_ibn_al-Mundhir). **[EXT] (Syriac), divergent.**
- Khusraw gave al-Ḥīra to **Iyās ibn Qabīṣa al-Ṭāʾī**, ending the dynasty; the Bakr then revolted and defeated a Persian force at the **Battle of Dhī Qār (ذي قار)** — [Wikipedia, al-Nu'man III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nu%27man_III_ibn_al-Mundhir). *No firm date for Dhī Qār found in these sources — see Gaps.*
- Iranica stresses the strategic consequence: the 602 deposition **weakened the empire's south-western defences** — [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ḥira"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hira).

**Geography — al-Ḥīra, al-Khawarnaq, al-Sadīr:**
- **Al-Ḥīra (الحيرة):** ruins lie **c. 7 km (4 mi) east-south-east of Najaf**, on the "desert fringes of south-western Mesopotamia"; **al-Kūfa**, founded later as a military encampment nearby, eclipsed it — [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ḥira"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hira).
- **Al-Khawarnaq (الخورنق)** and **al-Sadīr (السدير)** are both named by Iranica as Lakhmid palaces **near** Ḥīra — [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ḥira"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hira). Khawarnaq was the most celebrated Lakhmid building, reportedly built by al-Nuʿmān I to house the young Sasanian prince **Bahrām V**; tradition says its Byzantine architect "Cenmar" (Sinnimār) was killed so he could not build its like elsewhere — [Wikipedia, Lakhmid kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakhmid_kingdom). **[TRAD]; no precise coordinates found for either palace.**
- Christianity at Ḥīra is **[EXT]**-anchored early: **Bishop Hosea attended the first synod of the Church of the East in 410**; a Christian community existed by the 5th century; the local Arab Christians were the **ʿEbād / al-ʿIbād (العباد)**; Bishop Īshōʿdād reportedly died c. 640 — [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ḥira"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hira).
- Ḥīra **surrendered to Khālid ibn al-Walīd in 12 AH / 633 CE** — [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ḥira"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hira).
- The Lakhmids built numerous monasteries, including those of **Hind the Elder** and **Hind the Younger** — [Wikipedia, Lakhmid kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakhmid_kingdom).

### Inferences
- The Lakhmid record splits cleanly into a legendary stratum (268–c. 500) and a historical one (c. 503–602). For worldbuilding, anything before al-Mundhir III can be treated as free narrative space; from 503 the external sources start constraining events year by year.
- The 328 Namāra text and the Arabic tradition are describing political realities a century and a half apart; the "Lakhmids were always Persian clients" frame is a back-projection. A 4th-century Lakhmid with Roman-facing phylarchs is epigraphically better supported than a Persian-facing one.
- Iranica's refusal to print a king-list is a deliberate scholarly signal, not an omission.

### Gaps
- No date found in these sources for the **Battle of Dhī Qār** (commonly placed c. 604–611 in the literature, but I could not confirm it from a cited source here).
- **Al-Sadīr** is attested only as a name; no description, date or location beyond "near Ḥīra."
- Reign lengths for the 4th–5th century kings have no independent verification; the apparent precision (e.g. "418–462") should not be reproduced as fact.
- Isabel Toral-Niehoff's monograph on al-Ḥīra appears in search results ([AKU scholars record](https://scholars.aku.edu/en/publications/al-h%C4%ABra-and-its-histories/)) but I found no open-access full text.

## Q2. The Ghassanids / Jafnids (الغساسنة / آل جفنة)

### Takeaway
Only the 6th-century Jafnids are historical in a hard sense: al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala (c. 528/529–569) and al-Mundhir (569–c. 581/582) are richly **[EXT]**-attested, and the dynasty is **[EPI]**-touched by the Jabal Usays (528) and Ḥarrān (568) inscriptions. The phylarchate was broken up after 584 and the pre-6th-century ruler list is **[TRAD]** fiction-adjacent.

### Cited Findings

**Early (legendary) rulers:** Jafnah I ibn ʿAmr 220–265; ʿAmr I ibn Jafnah 265–270; Thaʿlabah ibn ʿAmr 270–287 — [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids). **[TRAD] — these 3rd-century dates have no external support whatever.**

**Historical sequence:**
- **Jabalah IV ibn al-Ḥārith, r. 512–529** — [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids). Independently touched by **[EXT]**: a letter of **Simeon of Bēth Arshām written c. 520 CE places Jabalah IV's camp at al-Jābiya**, with a Ghassanid military camp nearby — [Wikipedia, al-Jabiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Jabiyah). *This is an unusually solid early anchor.*
- Jabalah died at the **Battle of Thannuris (528)**; his son al-Ḥārith became phylarch c. 528 — [Wikipedia, al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah); [Wikipedia, Iberian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_War). **[EXT]**

**Al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala (الحارث بن جبلة) / Greek Arethas, r. c. 528–569:**
- **Date discrepancy to flag:** his own article gives **c. 528–569**; the Ghassanids overview article gives **529–569** — [Wikipedia, al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah) vs. [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids).
- **529:** Justinian I raised him to kingship (Procopius: "to the dignity of king") and gave him **patrikios / patricius**, plus overall command of Byzantium's Arab allies in the East — [Wikipedia, al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah). The Ghassanids page calls patricius "the highest imperial title that was ever bestowed upon a foreign ruler" and says he received authority over all Byzantium's Arab allies — [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids). He was also raised to **vir gloriosus** as part of Justinian's centralisation of the Arab clients — [Wikipedia, Iberian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_War). **[EXT], Procopius.**
- **529:** helped suppress the Samaritan revolt — [Wikipedia, al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah).
- **531, Callinicum:** led a 5,000-strong Arab contingent. **Procopius says the Arabs fled and cost Byzantium the battle; Malalas says Ḥārith stood firm while other Arabs fled.** He was kept in command regardless — [Wikipedia, al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah). **A textbook case of two [EXT] sources contradicting each other — excellent dramatic material.**
- **541:** joined Belisarius's raid into Assyria — [Wikipedia, al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah).
- **542:** as a **Miaphysite**, he petitioned **Empress Theodora** to appoint Miaphysite bishops in Syria; she appointed **Jacob Baradaeus** and Theodore, and Jacob went on to build the Miaphysite (Syriac Orthodox) church — [Wikipedia, al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah); corroborated at [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids). **[EXT] — the single most consequential Ghassanid act for religious history.**
- **June 554, Yawm Ḥalīma near Chalcis:** defeated the Lakhmids and killed al-Mundhir III; **lost his own eldest son Jabalah** — [Wikipedia, al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah).
- **569:** died (the source says during a reported earthquake); succeeded by his son al-Mundhir — [Wikipedia, al-Harith ibn Jabalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Harith_ibn_Jabalah).

**Al-Mundhir ibn al-Ḥārith (المنذر بن الحارث) / Greek Alamoundaros, r. 569–c. 581/582:**
- Reign **569 to c. 581** per his own article; the Ghassanids overview gives **569–581** in the table and **569–582** elsewhere — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III ibn al-Harith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Harith); [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids).
- **572: the break with Byzantium** — Justin II ordered his assassination; al-Mundhir discovered the plot and cut ties with the Empire — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III ibn al-Harith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Harith); [Wikipedia, Byzantine–Sasanian War of 572–591](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_572%E2%80%93591). **[EXT]**
- **575: reconciliation**, brokered by the general Justinian **at Sergiopolis (Ruṣāfa)** — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III ibn al-Harith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Harith). **Ruṣāfa is thus datably a Ghassanid–Byzantine meeting place in 575.**
- **575:** the Ghassanids **sacked al-Ḥīra**, the Lakhmid capital — [Wikipedia, Byzantine–Sasanian War of 572–591](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_572%E2%80%93591). **[EXT]**
- **580:** Ghassanids defeated the Lakhmids again — [Wikipedia, Byzantine–Sasanian War of 572–591](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_572%E2%80%93591).
- **581/582: deposition.** After the failed campaign against Ctesiphon, Maurice and al-Mundhir blamed each other; al-Mundhir was arrested and held in Constantinople. When **Maurice became emperor in 582 he exiled al-Mundhir to Sicily**. A 13th-century Syriac chronicle says he returned home after Maurice's overthrow in **602** — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III ibn al-Harith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Harith); [Wikipedia, Byzantine–Sasanian War of 572–591](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_572%E2%80%93591). **[EXT]**
- **A Greek inscription celebrating al-Mundhir survives at the *ecclesia extra muros* at Sergiopolis (modern Ruṣāfa)** — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III ibn al-Harith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Harith). The Ghassanids overview confirms "Al-Mundhir commissioned a building at Resafa, which is identified by a Greek inscription" — [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids). **[EPI], but undated in both sources.**

**The breakup of the phylarchate:**
- **al-Nuʿmān VI ibn al-Mundhir, r. 581–583**; then a gap; then **Jafnah, r. 587–591** — [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids).
- **584:** the Byzantines arrested al-Nuʿmān and tried him for treason; the Ghassanid confederation fragmented into a loose tribal coalition — **"split into 15 member tribes"** per the Ghassanids page; "fragmented into a loose tribal coalition" per the war article — [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids); [Wikipedia, Byzantine–Sasanian War of 572–591](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_572%E2%80%93591). **[EXT]**
- Maurice reportedly **restored the confederation after two or three years** because the empire needed Ghassanid defence — [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids).
- End: defeated at **Yarmūk, 636**; annexed **638**; **Jabala ibn al-Ayham** (last ruler, listed 632–638) reportedly accepted Islam, left it, and withdrew to Byzantine Anatolia in **639** — [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids).

**Capitals and camps:**
- **al-Jābiya (الجابية):** the capital and royal residence, located **between the Ḥawrān plain and the Golan, west of Nawā** (modern Daraa Governorate, Syria). It was a gathering place for foreign envoys meeting Ghassanid kings. The court poet Ḥassān called it **"Jābiyat al-Jawlān"** — [Wikipedia, al-Jabiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Jabiyah). The Ghassanids overview likewise places the capital at "Jabiyah in the Golan Heights" — [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids).
- **Ruṣāfa / Sergiopolis (الرصافة):** a major cult centre of **St Sergios**; site of the 575 reconciliation and of al-Mundhir's building with its Greek inscription — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III ibn al-Harith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Harith). Oxford's *Cult of Saints in Late Antiquity* database has catalogued the Ruṣāfa Greek inscriptions with full texts and dates — see e.g. [E01440 (lintel, translation of Sergios's relics, Ruṣāfa)](https://portal.sds.ox.ac.uk/articles/online_resource/E01440_Greek_lintel_inscription_commemorating_the_translation_of_relics_of_Sergios_soldier_and_martyr_of_Rusafa_S00023_from_an_old_church_to_a_new_sanctuary_in_Rusafa_and_the_beginning_of_the_rebuilding_of_the_former_shrine_Found_in_Rusafa_/13807511/1), [E01460 (Basilica A, the Great Basilica)](https://portal.sds.ox.ac.uk/articles/online_resource/E01460_So-called_Basilica_A_the_Great_Basilica_in_Rusafa_Sergioupolis_northeast_Syria_Euphratesia_a_major_place_of_the_cult_of_Sergios_soldier_and_martyr_of_Rusafa_S00023_housing_the_relics_of_the_saint_probably_since_at_least_the_last_thir/13807712/1), and [E01805 (Sergios church at Dayr Qīta, dated 537 and 567)](https://portal.sds.ox.ac.uk/articles/online_resource/E01805_Greek_building_inscriptions_from_a_church_dedicated_to_Sergios_soldier_and_martyr_of_Rusafa_S00023_Found_at_D_r_Q_ta_between_Antioch-on-the-Orontes_and_Beroia_Aleppo_north_Syria_Dated_537_and_567_/13810856/1). **This Oxford database is free and is the best open source for dated Greek church inscriptions in Ghassanid Syria.**
- **Religion:** the Ghassanids were **Miaphysite** Christians, though the Wikipedia overview notes current scholarship sees "a more flexible Miaphysitism than older accounts suggest," and that Byzantine mistrust of Miaphysitism is what brought down al-Ḥārith's successors — [Wikipedia, Ghassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanids).

**The Ghassanid-adjacent dated inscriptions:**
- **Jabal Usays / Jabal Says (جبل أسيس), 528 CE** — dated "423 after Bosra" (423 + 105 = 528). Findspot: **Jabal Usays, Syria, c. 105 km SE of Damascus**, found in German excavations. Described as **"the only pre-Islamic Arabic inscription with historical content"** — [Islamic Awareness, Jabal Usays inscription](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/usays.html). **[EPI].** *Note: this page did **not** give the text or name Ibrāhīm ibn Mughīra / al-Ḥārith al-Malik — see Gaps.* See also [Wikipedia, Jebel Usays inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jebel_Usays_inscription) and the OCIANA records at [ociana.osu.edu/inscriptions/10127](https://ociana.osu.edu/inscriptions/10127).
- **Ḥarrān inscription (نقش حران), 568 CE** — Greek–Arabic bilingual, from **Ḥarrān in the Lejā district south of Damascus**, on the **martyrium of St John**. The Arabic says **Sharaḥīl son of Ṭalimu** built the martyrium in **"year 463 after the destruction of Khaybar"**; the Greek names him *Saraēlos Talemou*. Bosra era: 463 + 105 = **568 CE** — [Islamic Awareness, Harran inscription](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/harran). **[EPI]. The internal dating formula — "after the destruction of Khaybar" — is itself a remarkable datum: a local Arabian era reckoned from a raid on Khaybar c. 105 CE.** *The user's prompt calls this the "ʿAbd Shams/Ḥarrān inscription" and the builder "Sharāḥīl ibn Ẓālim"; the source reads Ṭalimu, not Ẓālim.*
- **Zabad inscription (نقش زبد), 512 CE** — trilingual **Greek / Syriac / Paleo-Arabic** on the lintel of the martyrium of **St Sergius** at Zabad, northern Syria; now in the Art & History Museum, Brussels. Greek and Syriac date it to **year 823 of the Seleucid era = 512 CE**. The Greek records the building, clergy, donors and the architects Symeon and Leontius; the Syriac names the writer Mara and the founders; the **Arabic asks God to remember a list of named individuals**, probably sponsors. It is the **earliest dated Paleo-Arabic inscription**, and Ghassanids took part in the construction — [Wikipedia, Zabad inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabad_inscription). **[EPI]**
- **Dūmat al-Jandal inscription, 548/549 CE** — a **Paleo-Arabic Christian graffito** found near al-Jawf, published 2017, dated **year 443** of its era = **AD 548/549**, opening "May be remembered. May God remember Ḥgʿ{b/n}w son of Salama…" — [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal). **[EPI]. Quotation under 15 words.**
- Zabad, Ḥarrān and Umm al-Jimāl together document the evolution of Nabataean script into Paleo-Arabic and its geographic spread — [Wikipedia, Zabad inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabad_inscription); see also [Wikipedia, Paleo-Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleo-Arabic) and the index at [Islamic Awareness, Arabic & Islamic Inscriptions](https://islamic-awareness.org/History/Islam/Inscriptions).

### Inferences
- The Ghassanid "kingdom" is better imagined as a **mobile phylarchate with a seasonal royal camp at al-Jābiya** and ceremonial/religious presence at Ruṣāfa, not a territorial state with a walled capital. Simeon's c. 520 letter describing a *camp* at al-Jābiya supports exactly this.
- The 572–584 sequence (assassination plot → break → reconciliation at Ruṣāfa → sack of Ḥīra → arrest → exile → dissolution) is the richest continuous dramatic arc in 6th-century Arab political history and is almost entirely **[EXT]**-documented.
- Miaphysitism is the Ghassanids' strategic liability, not just their faith: it made them indispensable to Syrian Christians and suspect to Constantinople simultaneously.

### Gaps

Its location (commonly placed in the Damascus Ghūṭa, sometimes identified with al-Jābiya itself) could **not be confirmed from any cited source here.** The report-writer should flag Jalliq as a poetically attested **[TRAD]** site of uncertain identification.

## Q3. The Kindite kingdom (كندة)

### Takeaway
Kinda was a Ḥimyarite-sponsored over-tribal kingdom in Najd (conventionally c. 450–550 CE) whose only externally attested king is al-Ḥārith ibn ʿAmr (d. 528); its collapse came through fratricidal war among al-Ḥārith's sons and the Sasanian takeover of the south, and the poet Imruʾ al-Qays belongs to this ruined house.

### Cited Findings
- **Kingdom of Kinda dated c. 450–550 CE** — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda). **[RECON]**
- **Ḥujr Ākil al-Murār (حجر آكل المرار):** founder of the Banū Ākil al-Murār line, **installed over the Maʿadd tribes by Ḥimyar**; long stable reign, died of old age at Baṭn ʿAqīl. **No exact dates given** — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda). **[TRAD]**
- The Ḥimyarite king **Abīkarib Asʿad campaigned in central Arabia and set Ḥujr over Maʿadd** — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda). *Abīkarib/Abū Karib Asʿad is dated c. 390–440 in the Ḥimyarite list (see Q4), which gives Ḥujr a rough early-to-mid-5th-century floruit.*
- **ʿAmr al-Maqṣūr:** succeeded Ḥujr in Najd; the Rabīʿa tribes rejected him and he was probably **killed in battle against them in the late 5th century** — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda).
- **Muʿāwiya al-Jawn:** ruled the **Yamāma**, founding the Banū al-Jawn house — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda).
- **Al-Ḥārith ibn ʿAmr (الحارث بن عمرو), d. 528:** best-known Kindite king and **the first attested in Byzantine sources**. **Byzantium made the Kinda federates in 502.** He held a **phylarchate in Palestine**. He was killed in the Syrian Desert, **either by the Lakhmid al-Mundhir III or by the Banū Kalb** — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda). **[EXT] for the 502 federate status and his existence; the manner of death is contested.**
- His sons **Ḥujr, Maʿdīkarib, Shuraḥbīl and Salama** divided command of the Maʿadd; **their rivalries and blood feuds wrecked Kindite control of Najd** — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda). **[TRAD]**
- **Collapse:** the **battle of al-Kulāb** among al-Ḥārith's sons weakened them in Najd; the al-Jawn lost their leader at the **battle of Shiʿb Jabala**, dated variously **c. 550, 570, or 580**; these losses plus the **Sasanian conquest of South Arabia** pushed the Kinda to abandon Najd and the Yamāma for **Ḥaḍramawt** — reportedly about **30,000 people** migrating — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda). **The three-way date spread for Shiʿb Jabala is a clean example of [TRAD] chronology's elasticity.**
- **Imruʾ al-Qays the poet (امرؤ القيس):** a **Kindite poet-king**, one of the most prominent poets in Arabic. Per **Shahîd**, the spread of his poetry together with the Kindite era helped accelerate a common standard Arabic — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda). **[TRAD]+[RECON]. Note: he is a different person from the Imruʾ al-Qays of the 328 Namāra inscription, who is Lakhmid/"king of the Arabs."** The tradition makes the poet a son of the Ḥujr killed in the Kindite collapse, i.e. mid-6th century.
- **Ḥimyarite overlordship:** Kinda were **sponsored by Ḥimyar**, which had taken Saba in the late 3rd century. Historian **Mohammed Bamyeh** reads this as a political pact: sedentary Ḥimyar preferred to rule nomads through a dependent — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda). **[RECON]**
- **Capital:** **Ghamr Dhī Kinda (غمر ذي كندة)**, also **Qaryat Dhāt Kāhil**, today **Qaryat al-Fāw (قرية الفاو)**, is the likely royal capital; **c. two days north-east of Mecca** on a trade route; earlier a Minaean settlement; the Kinda **minted coins there bearing the name of their god Kahl** — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda).
- **Important chronological conflict:** the Qaryat al-Fāw site article says the city emerged in the 4th century BC, flourished 3rd c. BC – 3rd c. AD, and that **"the Kinda left around 300 AD" and the site was abandoned shortly after**; it lies **c. 100 km south of Wādī al-Dawāsir, c. 700 km SW of Riyadh**, on the NW edge of the Empty Quarter, with 17+ wells, on the route linking southern Arabia to the north-east — [Wikipedia, Qaryat al-Faw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw). **This directly contradicts using Qaryat al-Fāw as the capital of a 450–550 CE Kindite kingdom.** The report-writer must flag this: the archaeological site is a *pre-300 CE* Kindite centre, and by the time of Ḥujr Ākil al-Murār and al-Ḥārith ibn ʿAmr the Kinda were a Najdī over-tribal power without a confirmed urban capital.
- Inscriptions from Qaryat al-Fāw include a fresco with *zky* (read as a benediction, "pious/blessed"), a Maʿīnic plaque, an Old South Arabian wall fragment, a Sabaean mausoleum-donation text, and the **easternmost attested *mrzḥ*** (ritual banqueting institution) — [Wikipedia, Qaryat al-Faw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw). **[EPI], but pre-300 CE.**
- Kinda were also administered from Yemen: **CIH 541 (548 CE) records Abraha appointing a *khlft* (deputy-governor) for Kinda** — [Wikipedia, CIH 541](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIH_541). **[EPI] — the single hardest datum for Kindite political status in the 6th century: in 548 Kinda is a Ḥimyarite administrative charge.**

### Inferences
- Kinda in the 5th–6th century is best understood as **a delegated Ḥimyarite instrument for governing the Maʿadd nomads**, bracketed by two documented facts: Ḥimyar installing Ḥujr, and Abraha appointing a deputy for Kinda in 548. Its "kingship" was contingent on southern power.
- Its double clientage — Ḥimyarite sponsorship *and* a Byzantine federate treaty in 502 *and* a Palestinian phylarchate — made Kinda the most structurally unstable of the Arab buffer polities, which is likely why it fractured fastest.
- The Qaryat al-Fāw / Ghamr Dhī Kinda identification is almost certainly conflating two phases of Kindite history separated by 150+ years.

### Gaps
- No regnal dates at all for Ḥujr Ākil al-Murār, ʿAmr al-Maqṣūr or Muʿāwiya al-Jawn.
- No date for the battle of **al-Kulāb**; **Shiʿb Jabala** triple-dated.
- No independent **[EPI]** or **[EXT]** attestation of the poet Imruʾ al-Qays's existence or dates was found.

## Q4. South Arabia: Ḥimyar, the Maʾrib dam, Dhū Nuwās, Aksum, Abraha, the Sasanians

### Takeaway
South Arabia is the **only** part of pre-Islamic Arabia with a genuinely epigraphic chronology, and it is exactly here that the Arabic tradition diverges most sharply: the Maʾrib dam has dated repairs in 450, 548 and 557 with final collapse c. 570–575, not a single Qurʾānic *Sayl al-ʿArim*; and the "Year of the Elephant" has an inscription dated 552 CE, nearly twenty years before the traditional 570.

### Cited Findings

**The Ḥimyarite era epoch (critical methodological point):**
- **CIH 541 is dated 548 CE**; a Ḥimyarite-era year of **658** is the figure conventionally given for it — [Wikipedia, CIH 541](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIH_541) (the page states the 548 CE date; the Ḥimyarite year is not printed there).
- **Ry 506 / Murayghān 1's text includes the date "six hundred and sixty-two" for the month dhu-ʿallān (September), and the inscription is dated 552 CE** — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha).
- **Inference (mine, arithmetic):** 662 − 552 = **110**, so the Ḥimyarite era epoch is **110 BCE**; to convert a Ḥimyarite-era year to CE, **subtract 110**. This is consistent with Ḥimyar's conventional independence date of **110 BCE** — [Wikipedia, Himyar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyar). **Caution: I could not find a source that states the epoch explicitly; the Himyar page explicitly says 110 BCE describes political independence, not a calendar start.** Treat "subtract 110" as a well-supported working rule, not a sourced statement.

**The Maʾrib dam (سد مأرب) — the dated evidence:**

- **2nd–3rd centuries CE:** migrations attributed to a dam break; unclear whether this is the same event.

- **557: last recorded repairs.** *(The user's prompt says 558; this source says 557.)*

**Abraha (أبرهة) and the "Year of the Elephant":**
- Tenure given as **535 to 560/570** in the infobox; the article text says he ruled from the 530s to the 560s, **but detailed inscriptions cover only 547 to 559 CE**; **Christian Julien Robin argues he probably died c. 570**, reasoning from the succession of his sons Yaksūm and Masrūq in the early 570s — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha). **Note: the firm [EPI] window for Abraha is 547–559, not 535–570.**
- **The last known dated Ḥimyarite text is the church inscription of 559/560 CE** — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha). **[EPI] — the South Arabian epigraphic record goes dark a decade before the traditional Year of the Elephant.**
- **The 552 CE inscription (Ry 506 = Murayghān 1):** records that Abraha raided **Maʿadd** for the fourth time after the **Banū ʿĀmir** revolted; Maʿadd was defeated, pledged allegiance and gave hostages; **ʿAmr ibn al-Mundhir** (i.e. ʿAmr ibn Hind, the Lakhmid) **submitted and gave his son as a hostage**. Its internal date is **year 662, month dhu-ʿallān (September) = 552 CE**. **Mecca is not named in the inscription** — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha). **[EPI]. This is the central document of the Elephant controversy.**
  - Note the Lakhmid implication: **in 552 a Ḥimyarite king took the son of the king of al-Ḥīra as hostage.** This is a hard **[EPI]** datum on Lakhmid–Ḥimyarite relations and a direct check on the Lakhmid king-list (ʿAmr ibn Hind acceded 554 in the conventional table, yet appears here in 552 — flag the tension, since the inscription names him "ʿAmr ibn al-Mundhir" and he may have acted as his father's deputy).
- **Murayghān 3:** Abraha's last notable inscription, celebrating his consolidation of much of the peninsula; it states he had **lost control of Maʿadd and then reconquered it, driving out ʿAmr ibn al-Mundhir**; believed to **postdate Ry 506**, but **no date is given** — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha). **[EPI], undated.**
- **The Bāriqī-Robin-Nāʿiṭ 1 inscription (published 2025):** a fragmentary nine-line Sabaic relief from **Nāʿiṭ, c. 50 km north of Ṣanʿāʾ**, naming **Abraha** and mentioning **Ṣanʿāʾ** and **Nāʿiṭum** with military/expeditionary activity. Line 5 has **ʾlfl, read as "elephants"**; line 6 **ʾlbʾ, "lions"**; line 7 has **"Raḥmānān who is in Heaven,"** a formula previously found mainly in Jewish inscriptions; line 8 probably restores Abraha as commissioner. If the elephant reading holds it would be **the first epigraphic reference to elephants in South Arabia** and rare contemporary support for the tradition linking Abraha to an elephant-led attack on Mecca (Qurʾān 105). **The article stresses the identification is unconfirmed** — the word is damaged and its etymology uncertain. The piece also notes **Murayghān 1 (Ry 506) is dated 552 CE** and that about **ten inscriptions are known from Abraha's reign** — [Wikipedia, Bāriqī-Robin-Nāʿiṭ 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C4%81riq%C4%AB-Robin-N%C4%81%CA%BFi%E1%B9%AD_1). **Newest evidence in the file; treat as live scholarship.**
- **The "Year of the Elephant" (عام الفيل) — the state of the question:**
  - **Traditional:** c. **570–571 CE**, the year Islamic sources give for Muḥammad's birth, and the start-point of Meccan pre-Islamic chronology — [Wikipedia, Year of the Elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_the_Elephant); [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha). **[TRAD]**
  - **568 or 569:** suggested by South Arabian archaeological finds, since the Sasanians overthrew Aksum-affiliated rulers in Yemen around 570 — [Wikipedia, Year of the Elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_the_Elephant).
  - **552:** **Iwona Gajda accepts 552 as the expedition's date**, noting that **Mecca is not named in the inscription** — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha). **[RECON] on [EPI] grounds — this is the argument the user asked about.**
  - **"One or two decades earlier than 570"** per other scholars (cited to Esposito 1995) — [Wikipedia, Year of the Elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_the_Elephant).
  - **Before Muḥammad's father was born:** a tradition from **Ibn Shihāb al-Zuhrī**, recorded by **ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Ṣanʿānī** — [Wikipedia, Year of the Elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_the_Elephant). **[TRAD] that itself contradicts the 570 birth-year synchronism.**
  - **14 February 572 (13 Muḥarram 51 BH):** proposed by **Ibrahim Zein and Ahmad el-Wakil** — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha).
  - **Robin considers a failed expedition historically plausible.** Many historians read the story as later Islamic tradition. **Daniel Beck** argues it draws on 2 and 3 Maccabees and questions the use of war elephants; **Michael Charles** argues Aksum did have access to elephants in the 6th century — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha).
  - **Islamic accounts of the Mecca campaign** (all **[TRAD]**): c. **13 war elephants**; a route through **Ṭāʾif**; a guide named **Abū Righāl** who died near **al-Mughammas**; and an Azdī leader, **ʿAbd Shams ibn Masrūḥ**, who defeated an expedition Abraha sent against the Azd, forcing a change of route — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha).
- **Abraha's Christology:** under Abraha the inscriptions shift **from a Trinitarian formula to wording about "the mercy of Raḥmān, of his Messiah and the Spirit of holiness"** — [IDEO Cairo](https://www.ideo-cairo.org/en/report-en/the-christianity-of-abraha-and-the-quran/). See also Carlos Segovia's open-access paper on Abraha's Christological formula — [PDF](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/Library/Segovia,%20C%20-%20Abraha's%20Christological%20Formula.pdf).

**The Sasanian conquest of Yemen:**
- **570:** a small expedition led by **Wahrīz / Vahrez (وهرز)** took the country; the **Siege of Ṣanʿāʾ is dated 570** — [Wikipedia, Sasanian Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Yemen).
- **Sayf ibn Dhī Yazan (سيف بن ذي يزن):** governor **571–575** per the governor table; appointed vassal king after the 570 conquest; **killed by the Ethiopians in an uprising in 575 or 578** — [Wikipedia, Sasanian Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Yemen).
- **575 or 578:** Wahrīz returned with **c. 4,000 men**, expelled the Ethiopians and became governor; he installed **Maʿdīkarib ibn Sayf** as king — [Wikipedia, Sasanian Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Yemen).
- **Bādhān (باذان):** last Sasanian governor, serving until **628**, unrelated to Wahrīz's family — [Wikipedia, Sasanian Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Yemen).
- The 572–591 war article lists "the Sasanian invasion of Yemen in **570**" among the causes of renewed Byzantine–Persian conflict — [Wikipedia, Byzantine–Sasanian War of 572–591](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_572%E2%80%93591). **This is the key link between Arabian and imperial chronology.**
- The Sasanian Yemen page **does not frame 570 vs 575 as a chronological debate**; it treats them as two events (conquest, then reconquest), flagging only the **575 vs 578** uncertainty — [Wikipedia, Sasanian Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Yemen). *This differs from the user's framing of a "570–575" conquest period.*

### Inferences
- **The dam:** the Arabic/Qurʾānic *Sayl al-ʿArim* is a single catastrophic rupture explaining the dispersal of the southern Arabs. The epigraphic record instead shows a **600-year maintenance cycle** with repeated breaches and repairs (449/450, 542, 548, with repairs in 450, 543 and 557) terminating in an **unrepaired** overtopping c. 570–575. The historically meaningful event is not a flood but the **collapse of the political capacity to repair** — which coincides exactly with the Aksumite-to-Sasanian transition. That is the real causal story and it is more dramatic than the legend.
- **The Elephant:** the honest position is that there is **one dated Abraha campaign northward into Maʿadd (552)**, that it does not mention Mecca, that Abraha's epigraphic record stops in 559/560, and that the traditional 570 synchronism was constructed to anchor Muḥammad's birth. A novelist can legitimately choose any of 552, c. 560s, or 570 — but 552 is the only one with an inscription behind it.
- **A chronological squeeze worth noting:** if Abraha's last dated text is 559/560 and the Sasanians arrive in 570, the two Aksumite sons (Yaksūm 570–571, Masrūq 571–572) occupy an extremely tight window, and the 570 conquest overlaps Masrūq's reign. The 570–572 sequence in the ruler list is strained.
- Najrān 523 is the best-documented event in all of pre-Islamic Arabia: a near-contemporary letter (Jan 524), a Syriac book, a hagiography, and three Sabaic inscriptions. Its death toll is also the clearest demonstration that **[TRAD]** numbers inflate by two orders of magnitude.

## Q5. Mecca, Medina/Yathrib and the Ḥijāz

### Takeaway
This is the weakest-evidenced region in the whole survey: **there is no inscriptional, archaeological or external documentary evidence for Mecca or Quraysh before 632** in the sources consulted, and the Meccan genealogical chronology is internally contradictory by a full century. Yathrib fares slightly better, with Yawm Buʿāth datable to c. 617.

### Cited Findings

**Mecca and Quraysh (قريش):**
- **"The article contains no inscriptional, archaeological, or external documentary evidence for Mecca or Quraysh before 632."** Its sources are literary — Ibn Isḥāq, Hishām ibn al-Kalbī. **W. Montgomery Watt notes the pre-Islamic accounts contain a legendary element and that most dating is uncertain** — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh). **This is the single most important negative finding in the file and must be stated plainly in the report.**
- **Qusayy ibn Kilāb (قصي بن كلاب):** a sixth-generation descendant of **Fihr ibn Mālik** (of Kināna); he unified Fihr's descendants and **took control of the Kaʿba**. The name "Quraysh" may derive from him. His sons included **ʿAbd Manāf** (chosen as successor as guardian of the Kaʿba), **ʿAbd al-Dār** (the eldest), ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā and ʿAbd — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh). **[TRAD]**
- **The chronological contradiction, stated explicitly:** **Hawting** says that if the traditional sources are believed, Qusayy's children "must have lived in the second half of the fifth century"; **Watt** holds that **Qusayy himself likely died in the second half of the 6th century.** The article does not resolve the discrepancy — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh). **A ~100-year disagreement about when the founder of Meccan political order lived. Flag loudly.**
- **Hāshim ibn ʿAbd Manāf (هاشم بن عبد مناف)** is the ancestor of the **Banū Hāshim**, whose later name was **"Banū ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib."** **ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib (عبد المطلب)** appears in the article only in this context; his sons included **Ḥamza, Abū Ṭālib and al-ʿAbbās** — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh).
- **The Īlāf (الإيلاف): the article gives no further detail on the Īlāf agreements** — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh). **See Gaps — this key question is essentially unanswered.** Qurʾān **Sūrat Quraysh (106)** is the locus classicus — [Quran.com, Surah 106 info](https://quran.com/surah/106/info).
- **Clan factions:** **al-Aḥlāf (الأحلاف, "the Confederates")** backed ʿAbd al-Dār — Sahm, ʿAdī, Makhzūm, Jumaḥ; **al-Muṭayyabūn (المطيبون, "the Perfumed")** backed ʿAbd Manāf — Taym, Asad, Zuhra, al-Ḥārith ibn Fihr. Geographically, **Quraysh al-Biṭāḥ ("of the Hollow")** settled around the Kaʿba and **Quraysh al-Ẓawāhir ("of the Outskirts")** on its edges — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh). **[TRAD], but excellent for the social geography of the town.**
- **Datable-ish Meccan events:** the **Fijār War** (Quraysh and Kināna against Qays tribes) broke out **toward the end of the 6th century**; **in 605 the Banū Umayya and Banū Nawfal split from the al-Muṭayyabūn faction**; **Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl** followed an incident involving al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil but **is undated** — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh). **[TRAD]**
- **Trade:** **winter caravans to Yemen; summer caravans to Gaza, Bosra, Damascus and al-ʿArīsh.** Quraysh maintained networks with merchants in Syrian cities and made alliances with Bedouin tribes to protect caravans. They bought estates in **al-Ṭāʾif** (cooler climate) and gained control of its trade. **By 600 CE, per Watt, Quraysh leaders had something like a monopoly over trade between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean** — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh).
- **Counter-view:** per **Peters**, Mecca's economy **in Qusayy's time was based on pilgrimage rather than commerce** — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh). **A real scholarly fault line (the Patricia Crone "Meccan Trade" debate sits here).** See the open-access thesis *Meccan trade prior to the rise of Islam* — [PDF, almuslih](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/Library/Aladieh,%20S%20-%20Meccan%20Trade.pdf); record at [HBKU](https://ibir-api.hbku.edu.qa/node/50009).

**Yathrib / Medina (يثرب / المدينة):**
- **Yawm Buʿāth / Battle of Buʿāth (يوم بعاث): c. 617 CE, at Buʿāth near Medina** — [Wikipedia, Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath). **[TRAD], but tightly anchored by its proximity to the Hijra.**
- **Alignments:** **Aws (أوس)** backed by the Jewish **Banū Qurayẓa (قريظة)** and **Banū al-Naḍīr (النضير)**, plus Banū Muzayna and (per the article) the Ghassanids; **Khazraj (خزرج)** backed by **Banū Qaynuqāʿ (قينقاع)**, Banū Juhayna and Banū Ashjaʿ — [Wikipedia, Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath). **The Jewish tribes are split across the Arab factions — not a Jewish/Arab divide.**
- **Cause:** the Khazraj chief **ʿAmr ibn Nuʿmān al-Bayāḍī** seized hostages from the Jewish tribes and killed them when they refused to hand over property, driving the Jewish tribes to the Aws — [Wikipedia, Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath).
- **Outcome:** the **Aws won**; **Ḥuḍayr al-Katāʾib** rallied them and ʿAmr ibn Nuʿmān was killed. Both tribes then **chose ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy as a ruler of Medina to keep the peace** — [Wikipedia, Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath). **Key political consequence: Buʿāth created the vacancy that Muḥammad's arrival in 622 filled.**
- *Note the contradiction:* the Qurayẓa article calls Buʿāth **"inconclusive"**, while the Buʿāth article says the Aws won — [Wikipedia, Banu Qurayza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Qurayza) vs. [Wikipedia, Yawm al-Buath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawm_al-Buath).
- **The Jewish tribes:** three major ones — **Qurayẓa, Qaynuqāʿ, Naḍīr**. Al-Iṣfahānī says the Qurayẓa first settled in **Maḥzūr, a wādī in al-Ḥarrah**. They traced descent from an Israelite patriarch (Ibn Isḥāq's genealogy runs back to **Aaron**, hence the "priestly tribe"). **The sources do not establish whether they were ethnically Israelite or Arab converts to Judaism**; they claimed Israelite descent, practised Judaism, adopted Arab customs and intermarried with Arabs — [Wikipedia, Banu Qurayza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Qurayza).
- **Pre-622 chronology for Yathrib:** **in the 5th century CE** the Qurayẓa lived at Yathrib alongside Qaynuqāʿ and Naḍīr; **toward the end of the 5th century** the Aws and Khazraj revolted against Jewish clientship (per most accounts); **the Aws and Khazraj fought for roughly a century before 620, at least since the 570s** — [Wikipedia, Banu Qurayza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Qurayza). **[TRAD]+[RECON]**
- **Alliance structure:** **Qurayẓa and Naḍīr allied with the Aws** (though they frequently fought each other, and are sometimes called "brothers" in the sources); **Qaynuqāʿ sided with the Khazraj. Historians disagree on whether the Jewish tribes were clients of the Arab tribes or retained political independence** — [Wikipedia, Banu Qurayza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Qurayza).
- **The Aws and Khazraj arrived from Yemen** and later became dominant at Yathrib — [Wikipedia, Banu Qurayza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Qurayza). **[TRAD] — and note this is the same southern-migration motif the dam legend serves.**
- **Qurayẓa material detail (627, just outside scope but concrete):** their stores held c. **1,500 swords, 2,000 lances, 300 suits of armour, 500 shields**; they held a fortified stronghold for 25 days — [Wikipedia, Banu Qurayza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Qurayza).

**The fairs and markets:**
- **Sūq ʿUkāẓ (سوق عكاظ):** located **between Nakhla and al-Ṭāʾif** in the Ḥijāz, at approximately **21°28′28″N, 40°38′42″E**. Founded **501 CE** per the infobox; active **c. 542–726 CE** per the text. Held for **21 days each year in Dhū al-Qaʿda, before the Ḥajj.** The Kharijites sacked and destroyed it in **127 AH (725–726 CE)** (the infobox's "745" conflicts with this) — [Wikipedia, Souk Okaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souk_Okaz).
- **Majanna (مجنة)** and **Dhū al-Majāz (ذو المجاز):** held near Mecca at the same time of year as ʿUkāẓ — [Wikipedia, Souk Okaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souk_Okaz).
- **Dūmat al-Jandal (دومة الجندل)** was one of the annual market-fair cities. **Uniquely among them, its sovereignty was disputed between the Arab clients of Byzantium and of the Sasanians.** Its markets were known for slavery and prostitution, and the **Banū Kalb** were the dominant local tribe — [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal). **Excellent setting material: a frontier fair with contested sovereignty.**

### Inferences
- For a visual novel, Mecca must be treated as **narratively rich and chronologically unfixed**. The Hawting/Watt century-wide disagreement about Qusayy means a writer has essentially free rein on Meccan internal chronology before c. 600, provided the sequence Qusayy → ʿAbd Manāf → Hāshim → ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib is preserved.
- Medina c. 617–622 is, by contrast, a tightly dated political situation: a post-civil-war oasis with a freshly appointed consensus leader (Ibn Ubayy), three Jewish tribes distributed across the two Arab factions, and exhausted elites. That is a usable "present day."
- The 605 Umayya/Nawfal split and the c. 617 Buʿāth bracket are the two most usable fixed points in Ḥijāzī politics.

## Q6. Eastern and central Arabia: Bahrayn, Tamīm, Yamāma, Najd

### Takeaway
Eastern Arabia under Sasanian suzerainty and the Yamāma of the Banū Ḥanīfa are the thinnest-documented part of the survey; al-Yamāma's chief town was **Hajar**, the Banū Ḥanīfa were **settled agriculturalists** (not nomads), and the region supplied wheat to Mecca.

### Cited Findings
- **Al-Yamāma (اليمامة):** a historical region in **south-eastern Najd**, counted as part of **Najd (نجد)**, the central Arabian plateau. **Its principal historical town was Hajar (هجر).** In the 19th century "al-Yamāma" meant a town in the **al-Kharj** area, **c. 70 km south-east of modern Riyadh** — [Wikipedia, al-Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Yamama). **Important caution: the source does not equate Hajar with modern Riyadh.**
- **Al-Hamdānī (10th c.)** described the region as having isolated fortresses, palm groves, gardens, and silver and gold mines — [Wikipedia, al-Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Yamama).
- **Economy:** an important agricultural centre known for **dates, wheat and meat**, and **it supplied wheat to Mecca** — [Wikipedia, al-Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Yamama). **A concrete trade link between Najd and the Ḥijāz.**
- **Banū Ḥanīfa (بنو حنيفة):** the predominant tribe of the Yamāma, living a **settled, agricultural life**. Their **skilled workers were employed in Mecca**, including by Muḥammad for clay used in the Medina mosque. **Hawdha ibn ʿAlī** was the major political figure in Muḥammad's lifetime — [Wikipedia, al-Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Yamama).
- **Musaylima (مسيلمة)**, a Ḥanīfa *kāhin* (soothsayer), proclaimed himself a prophet in his village of **Haddār in the Falj valley before the Hijra (c. 622)** — [Wikipedia, al-Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Yamama). **[TRAD], but a datable-ish pre-622 religious event in central Arabia.**
- **Banū Tamīm (بنو تميم):** a large tribe present **throughout north-eastern Arabia**. Its small branch, the **Banū Usayyid**, guarded the Yamāma's sacred space. The Tamīmī prophetess **Sajāḥ** gained a following among her tribesmen — [Wikipedia, al-Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Yamama).
- **Ṭarafa, the poet, was sent to his death by "the governor of Bahrayn"** acting for ʿAmr ibn Hind — [Wikipedia, Amr ibn Hind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amr_ibn_Hind). **This implies Lakhmid (and thus indirect Sasanian) administrative reach into Bahrayn in the 550s–560s. [TRAD].**
- Al-Nuʿmān III's Nestorian al-Ḥīra conducted **missionary activity in the Persian Gulf and eastern Arabia** — [Wikipedia, al-Nu'man III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nu%27man_III_ibn_al-Mundhir). **This is the best-attested institutional link between Ḥīra and eastern Arabia.**
- **Mazūn (مزون)** was the Sasanian province covering south-eastern Arabia/Oman — [Wikipedia, Mazun (Sasanian province)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazun_(Sasanian_province)). *Not fetched in detail.*
- **Al-Zārah (الزارة)** was a settlement in Bahrayn — [Wikipedia, Al-Zarah (Bahrayn)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Zarah_(Bahrayn)). *Not fetched.*
- **Fulayj: a late Sasanian fort on the Arabian coast** — excavation report, open PDF via Cambridge: [PDF](https://www-cambridge-org.ezproxyberklee.flo.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/AE705F074C93B4EEE392F94130A3C7F1/S0003598X18000649a.pdf/fulayj-a-late-sasanian-fort-on-the-arabian-coast.pdf). **[ARCHAEOLOGICAL] — the best hard evidence for a Sasanian military presence on the Arabian coast. Note this URL is an institutional-proxy link; the report-writer should find the canonical *Antiquity* DOI.**

### Inferences
- The Banū Ḥanīfa being **settled farmers supplying Mecca with wheat** inverts the usual "nomad periphery" picture of Najd and gives a logistical reason why Yamāma mattered to the Ḥijāz.
- Bahrayn/eastern Arabia appears in the sources only **through** other powers: as a Lakhmid governorate, a Nestorian mission field, and a Sasanian fortified coast. There is no indigenous political narrative recoverable from accessible sources.

### Gaps
- **Hajar** and **al-Mushaqqar (المشقر)** — the user asked specifically about these. **Hajar** is attested only as "the region's chief settlement and seat of its governor"; **al-Mushaqqar is not mentioned in any source I reached.**
- No ruler names, dates or governor-list for **Sasanian Bahrayn** before the Islamic conquest; **al-Mundhir ibn Sāwā** (the traditional Bahrayn ruler at the time of Muḥammad) did not appear in any source I reached.
- Najdī tribal geography (the Banū Asad, ʿAbs, Dhuhl, Bakr and Taghlib dispositions, the Basūs war) is not covered.

## Q7. The Byzantine–Sasanian wars as the macro-frame

### Takeaway
The five wars (502–506, 526–532, 540–562, 572–591, 602–628) are the metronome: almost every Arabian event the user asks about slots into one of them, and the Arab clients function as the wars' deniable southern wing until Byzantium and Persia each dismantled their own client in 581–584 and 602 respectively.

### Cited Findings

**502–506, Anastasian War:** **in 505 Anastasius I began fortifying Dara** as a counter to Sasanian Nisibis; after the war a **seven-year truce** was agreed, which in fact lasted nearly twenty years — [Wikipedia, Iberian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_War). **The Lakhmid al-Mundhir III's 503 raids on Palaestina Salutaris and Arabia Petraea belong to this war** — [Wikipedia, al-Mundhir III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mundhir_III_ibn_al-Nu%27man). **Kinda became Byzantine federates in 502** — [Wikipedia, Kingdom of Kinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kinda). **The Anastasian War is thus the moment all three Arab client systems enter the record.**

**526–532, Iberian War:** fought mainly over **Iberia**, a Sasanian client that defected to Byzantium; **overt fighting broke out 526–527**; **Justinian I succeeded Justin I in 527** — [Wikipedia, Iberian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_War).
- Battles: **Thannuris 528** (Belisarius defeated by Xerxes; **Jabalah IV the Ghassanid died here**); **Dara 530** (Belisarius defeats a larger Persian force under Perozes); **Callinicum 531** (Belisarius defeated by Persian and Lakhmid forces; dismissed afterwards) — [Wikipedia, Iberian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_War).
- **Perpetual / "Eternal" Peace, 532:** negotiated in spring 532 after **Khusraw I succeeded Kavādh I**; all wartime conquests returned; the Dux of the eastern forces moved from Dara to Constantia; **Byzantium paid 11,000 lbs (5,000 kg) of gold**; the Sasanians kept Iberia and Byzantium kept Lazica — [Wikipedia, Iberian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_War); [Wikipedia, Perpetual Peace (532)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_Peace_(532)).
- **Arab roles:** Lakhmids under al-Mundhir III raided Roman territory in **525–526** and damaged Syria **528–529**, then fought at Callinicum in 531; Ghassanids under al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala were centralised under his leadership by Justinian and raised to **vir gloriosus** — [Wikipedia, Iberian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_War).

**540–562:** *Not directly fetched.* The **Fifty-Year Peace of 562** closes it; the 572–591 war article references it only as the treaty that preceded the new war "by less than a decade," without describing its terms — [Wikipedia, Byzantine–Sasanian War of 572–591](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_572%E2%80%93591). A legal-historical analysis of the 561/562 treaty is open access in Spanish — ["Pax fundata cum persis," Univ. de Alcalá](https://portalcientifico.uah.es/documentos/620c9d50bd89a06a02bb444a). **Note: Yawm Ḥalīma (554) and the 541 Assyria raid fall inside this war.**

**572–591:** all from [Wikipedia, Byzantine–Sasanian War of 572–591](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_572%E2%80%93591) unless noted.
- **Causes included Byzantine talks with the Gökturks, the Sasanian invasion of Yemen in 570, and the Armenian revolt beginning 571.** **This is the explicit linkage of the Yemen conquest to the imperial war.**
- **572:** Armenians seized Dvin; **Justin II refused to keep paying tribute**; Justin **ordered the attempted assassination of the Ghassanid al-Mundhir**, who broke with Byzantium.
- **573:** Byzantine win at Sargathon and siege of Nisibis, then retreat; **Khusraw I took Dara after a four-month siege**; Adarmahan raided Syria including Apamea.
- **573–574:** truces in Mesopotamia, fighting continues in the Caucasus.
- **575: the Ghassanids sacked al-Ḥīra**; al-Mundhir reconciled with Byzantium at Sergiopolis.
- **576:** Khusraw I invaded Anatolia, sacked Sebasteia, was defeated near Melitene; he reportedly **barred future Sasanian kings from leading armies in person except against another monarch.**
- **577–578:** Byzantine raids into Caucasian Albania; Tamkhosrow defeated Justinian in Armenia; the Mesopotamian truce ended and **Maurice raided across the Tigris**.
- **579: Khusraw I sought peace but died before agreement; Hormizd IV (r. 579–590) ended the talks.**
- **580:** Ghassanids defeated the Lakhmids again; Khusraw II (then a prince) restored most Armenian rebels to Sasanian allegiance.
- **581–582:** Adarmahan's campaign failed; Maurice and al-Mundhir blamed each other; **al-Mundhir arrested**; Battle of Constantina (dated 582 in text, 581 in the link); **Maurice proclaimed emperor**.
- **584:** **al-Nuʿmān arrested; the Ghassanid kingdom fragmented.**
- **586:** Byzantine victory at Solachon. **588:** mutinies among unpaid troops, then victory at Martyropolis. **589:** Martyropolis fell by treachery; Comentiolus won at Sisauranon; **Bahrām Chōbīn** defeated at the Araxes, dismissed by Hormizd, revolted.
- **590: Hormizd IV overthrown and killed; Khusraw II succeeded; Bahrām seized the throne as Bahrām VI; Khusraw fled to Byzantine territory** (and, per the Lakhmid sources, was sheltered by **al-Nuʿmān III** — [Wikipedia, al-Nu'man III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nu%27man_III_ibn_al-Mundhir)).
- **591:** Maurice backed Khusraw's restoration; Khusraw won over the main army at Nisibis; **Ctesiphon taken for him; decisive battle at Blarathon near Ganzak.**
- **591 treaty terms:** Khusraw returned **Dara and Martyropolis**; the Caucasus was repartitioned, Byzantium gaining Tigranokert, Manzikert, Yerevan, Ani, Kars and western Iberia (Ardahan, Lori, Dmanisi, Mtskheta); Cytaea went to Lazica. **No Byzantine payments — a major shift in the balance of power.**

**602–628:** all from [Wikipedia, Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_602%E2%80%93628).
- **602: Phocas overthrew and murdered Maurice; Khusraw II declared war, claiming to avenge him.** *(Same year as al-Nuʿmān III's deposition — the two events are plainly linked, Khusraw clearing his southern flank.)*
- **605:** Persians took Dara. **608:** Heraclius the Elder revolted in Africa; Shahīn raided to Chalcedon. **609–610:** Mardin, Amida, Erzurum, Edessa fell. **610:** Nicetas took Egypt for the rebels.
- **613:** Persians defeated Byzantines at **Antioch**, looted it, killed the patriarch; Tarsus, Damascus, Apamea, Emesa fell.
- **614: Jerusalem captured after a three-week siege; the True Cross carried off to Ctesiphon.**
- **615:** Persians reached Chalcedon. **617:** Shahīn took Chalcedon and refused peace. **618:** Persians invaded Egypt; **Alexandria fell after a year-long siege**; the Constantinople grain dole was abolished. **619:** plague. **620 or 622:** Ancyra fell.
- **622: Heraclius left Constantinople on 4 April** to begin his counteroffensive; defeated Shahrbarāz in autumn. *(Same year as the Hijra.)*
- **623:** escaped an Avar ambush at Heraclea. **624: invaded Persia's heartland, sacked Dvin, defeated Khusraw's army at Ganzaka with help from loyal Arabs.** **625:** night attack at Aliovit; recaptured Amida and Martyropolis; beat Shahrbarāz at the Sarus.
- **626: Avar–Slav siege of Constantinople** from late June, failed in early August after Theodore beat Shahīn at the Lycus; **Heraclius allied with the Western Turkic Khaganate, which sent 40,000 men.**
- **627: 12 December, Battle of Nineveh** (c. 6,000 Persians killed), then the plundering of Dastagird. **628: Khusraw II overthrown and killed; Kavād II sued for peace; the treaty restored territory, prisoners, an indemnity and the True Cross.**
- **Arab involvement:** Arabs fought on the Byzantine side at **Ganzaka in 624**; the **Ghassanids are listed as Byzantine supporters, the Lakhmids as Sasanian supporters**; the article gives no more. Afterwards, **the exhausted states could not resist the Rashidun invasions of the 630s.**

**Reigns (for the spine):** Kavādh I to 531; **Khusraw I Anūshirwān r. 531–579**; **Hormizd IV r. 579–590**; Bahrām VI 590–591; **Khusraw II Parvīz r. 590–628**; Kavād II 628 — [Wikipedia, Byzantine–Sasanian War of 572–591](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_572%E2%80%93591); [Wikipedia, Iberian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_War). Byzantine: Anastasius I (to 518), Justin I (518–527), **Justinian I 527–565**, **Justin II** (to 578), Tiberius II, **Maurice 582–602**, **Phocas 602–610**, **Heraclius 610–641** — same sources, assembled.

### Inferences
- **The clients were liquidated in the same twenty years by both empires:** Byzantium broke the Ghassanid phylarchate in **581–584**; Persia destroyed the Lakhmid kingdom in **602**. Both did so immediately before the final, mutually ruinous war of 602–628. The removal of the Arab buffer systems is a precondition, not merely a backdrop, of the 630s conquests.
- **The 570 Yemen conquest is formally one of the listed causes of the 572–591 war.** South Arabia is not a sideshow; it is a theatre of the imperial contest.
- **The 602 coincidence is the structural pivot of the whole period:** Maurice murdered, Khusraw declaring war, and al-Nuʿmān III executed, all in one year.

### Gaps
- **The 540–562 war was not fetched** (the Byzantine–Sasanian wars overview URL 404s and the Roman–Iranian relations article does not cover it). Its chronology, the 545/551 truces, the Lazic War and the full terms of the **562 Fifty-Year Peace** need a separate source — start from [Wikipedia, List of Byzantine wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Byzantine_wars) and the Alcalá treaty study linked above.
- Exact accession/death dates for Justin II, Tiberius II were not individually sourced.

## Q8. Trade and geography: routes, caravan cities, stations

### Takeaway
The incense route's northern nodes are archaeologically well-attested, with **Dūmat al-Jandal** as the best-documented crossroads; the Ḥijāzī leg and the station-list between Yemen and Syria could not be pinned to specific cited distances in this session.

### Cited Findings
- **Dūmat al-Jandal (دومة الجندل):** an ancient ruined city, the historical capital of modern **al-Jawf Province**, NW Saudi Arabia; **c. 37 km from Sakāka, north of the Nafūd desert, at one end of Wādī Sirḥān, in an oasis** — [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal).
- **Its route position:** at a major intersection of the incense-trade network. **One branch linked India and South Arabian goods to Lower Mesopotamia; another linked the Persian Gulf, through Wādī Sirḥān, to southern Syria** — [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal). **This is the clearest sourced statement of a major route geometry in the file.**
- **Nabataean / Roman layers at Dūma:** Roman-Nabataean pottery indicating a prosperous community probably within the Nabataean realm; a **large open-air Nabataean banqueting hall (triclinium)** used from the late 1st c. BC to the late 1st / early 2nd c. AD. **In AD 106 Trajan incorporated Dūmah into the Empire after annexing the Nabataean Kingdom**; it became part of **Arabia Petraea** and in the 4th century was redefined as **Palaestina Salutaris**; it was the **easternmost settlement on the Limes Arabicus for over four centuries** — [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal). **[ARCHAEOLOGICAL]+[EXT]. Note: Palaestina Salutaris is also exactly what al-Mundhir III raided in 503 — the same frontier.**
- **In AD 269 Zenobia of Palmyra mentioned the city, and Mārid Castle (قصر مارد) withstood her attack** during her revolt against Rome — [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal). **[EXT]/[TRAD] — the Palmyrene legacy in Arabia, with a named standing building.**
- **Al-Ṭāʾif (الطائف):** Quraysh bought estates there for its cooler climate and took control of its trade — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh). It also lies on the reported route of Abraha's Mecca expedition — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha). Sūq ʿUkāẓ lay **between Nakhla and al-Ṭāʾif** — [Wikipedia, Souk Okaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souk_Okaz).
- **Caravan directions from Mecca:** winter south to **Yemen**; summer north to **Gaza, Bosra, Damascus and al-ʿArīsh** — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh).
- **Qaryat al-Fāw** sat on the route linking southern Arabia with the north-east and had **17+ wells** — [Wikipedia, Qaryat al-Faw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw).
- **Khaybar (خيبر)** appears in a remarkable epigraphic context: the Ḥarrān inscription of 568 dates itself **"463 years after the destruction of Khaybar,"** implying a local Arabian era with its epoch c. 105 CE — [Islamic Awareness, Harran inscription](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/harran). **[EPI]**
- **Al-Yamāma supplied wheat to Mecca** — [Wikipedia, al-Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Yamama).
- **General route resources (free):** [Livius.org, Incense Route](https://livius.org/articles/place/incense-route); [Madain Project, Cities, Trade Points and Nodes of the Incense Trade Route](https://madainproject.com/cities_trade_points_and_nodes_of_the_incense_trade_route); [Madain Project, Incense Route](https://madainproject.com/incense_route); [Saudi Archaeology site index](https://saudi-archaeology.com/sites/); [Ithra, "The Fragrant Roads of the Incense Trade Route"](https://www.ithra.com/en/news/fragrant-roads-incense-trade-route). **Madain Project is an aggregator — usable for orientation and site photographs, not as a primary citation.**

### Inferences
- The two documented route geometries — **Yemen → Ḥijāz → Gaza/Damascus** (the Quraysh caravans) and **Gulf → Wādī Sirḥān → southern Syria** plus **South Arabia → Lower Mesopotamia** (via Dūma) — intersect in north-west Arabia. That intersection, with its sovereignty contested between Byzantine and Sasanian clients and its slave market, is the natural dramatic crossroads of the period.
- Dūmat al-Jandal's four centuries as the easternmost Roman *limes* settlement means it is the one place in interior Arabia with a continuous documented imperial presence from Trajan to late antiquity.

### Gaps
- **The station-by-station itinerary between Yemen and Syria is not recoverable from any cited source I reached.** No distances, no named waystations, no stage-lengths.
- **Taymāʾ (تيماء)** and **Khaybar (خيبر)** as places: no dedicated source consulted. Taymāʾ in particular has a major Nabataean/Babylonian epigraphic record that is simply absent from this file.
- **Petra** and **Palmyra** as legacies: covered only indirectly (the Nabataean annexation of 106 and Zenobia's 269 attack on Dūma).
- No sourced account of the **Mecca–Ṭāʾif–Ṣanʿāʾ** road or of how often the ʿUkāẓ/Majanna/Dhū al-Majāz fair cycle actually ran.

**Undated but historically loaded:**
- **Murayghān 3:** Abraha's consolidation of much of the peninsula; records that he **lost and then reconquered Maʿadd, driving out ʿAmr ibn al-Mundhir**; believed to postdate Ry 506 — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha).

**Safaitic and Hismaic — what they do and don't fix:**
- **Safaitic (الصفائية):** c. **3rd century BCE – 4th century CE**. Named after **al-Ṣafā**, the basalt desert SE of Damascus, where they were first found in **1857**. Distribution: **southern Syria, eastern Jordan, NW Saudi Arabia**, with isolated examples at **Palmyra, Wādī Ḥawrān and Ḥāʾil**; the densest concentration is the **Ḥarrat al-Shamah**. About **30,000 recorded**, with many more presumed undiscovered — [Wikipedia, Safaitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safaitic).
- Content: mostly graffiti on grazing camels, mourning the dead, genealogies; also **raids and prayers for booty**, and religious practice. **A few were written by women.** Some accompany rock art of hunts, battles, camels and mounted riders, camp scenes, and female figures — [Wikipedia, Safaitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safaitic).
- **Datability — the critical caveat:** "The page gives no method for dating individual inscriptions; it offers only the overall period," and inscriptions on cairns **may be earlier or later than the cairns**, often indeterminably — [Wikipedia, Safaitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safaitic). **So: Safaitic fixes almost nothing chronologically. It is a vast ethnographic and onomastic archive, not a dating instrument.** Some Safaitic texts do mention datable external events (Roman campaigns, named emperors) in the wider literature, but **I found no cited instance here.**
- **Hismaic** is mentioned only in connection with mixed letter-shapes; **no date range or distribution given** — [Wikipedia, Safaitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safaitic).

- **The Bosra era** is the operative calendar for the Syrian Arabic inscriptions: **add 105 to the stated year to get CE** (confirmed independently for Namāra 223→328, Jabal Usays 423→528, and Ḥarrān 463→568) — [Islamic Awareness](https://islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/namarah), [Usays](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/usays.html), [Harran](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/harran). **Three independent confirmations of the same conversion rule — the firmest methodological point in the file.**

### Inferences
- **Three different era-systems are in simultaneous use in 6th-century Arabia and its borderlands:** the **Bosra era** (+105) in Roman Syria, the **Seleucid era** at Zabad, the **Ḥimyarite era** (−110) in the south, and the **"destruction of Khaybar" era** at Ḥarrān. For a story, this is a gift: no two regions count years the same way.
- The epigraphic density is radically uneven. **South Arabia has a dense dated corpus; the Syrian frontier has about half a dozen dated Arabic texts; the Ḥijāz has none.** Any timeline becomes progressively more speculative as it moves south-west into the Ḥijāz and progressively firmer as it moves to Yemen or into the Byzantine/Syriac orbit.
- The **Bāriqī-Robin-Nāʿiṭ 1** publication (2025) means the Elephant question is live scholarship right now, not settled. A novel written today can honestly dramatise the uncertainty.

## Cross-cutting: where tradition and epigraphy disagree (summary for the report-writer)

### Takeaway
Four head-on collisions, in descending order of importance for the user's project.

### Cited Findings
1. **The Year of the Elephant.** **[TRAD]: c. 570–571**, synchronised with Muḥammad's birth — [Wikipedia, Year of the Elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_the_Elephant). **[EPI]: the only dated Abraha campaign against Maʿadd is 552 (Ry 506), and it does not mention Mecca**; Gajda accepts 552 — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha). Abraha's dated epigraphic window closes at **559/560** — same source. Competing proposals: 568/569, 552, "one or two decades before 570," 14 Feb 572, and a tradition from al-Zuhrī placing it before Muḥammad's father's birth — [Wikipedia, Year of the Elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_the_Elephant); [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha).
2. **The Maʾrib dam / Sayl al-ʿArim.** **[TRAD]/Qurʾān 34:15–17: one catastrophic flood causing dispersal.** **[EPI]: breaches in 449, 450, 542, 548 with repairs in 450, 543 and 557, then an unrepaired overtopping in 570 or 575.** Many scholars instead link the Qurʾānic *Sayl al-ʿArim* to the **c. 145 BC** breach — [Wikipedia, Marib Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam).
3. **Meccan chronology.** **Hawting: Qusayy's children lived in the second half of the 5th century. Watt: Qusayy himself died in the second half of the 6th century.** Unresolved — [Wikipedia, Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh). And: **no pre-632 inscriptional, archaeological or external documentary evidence for Mecca or Quraysh at all** — same source.
4. **Najrān's death toll.** **c. 200** in near-contemporary sources; **70,000** in sources written centuries later — [Wikipedia, Christian community of Najran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_community_of_Najran).
5. **The regnal tables themselves.** Encyclopaedia Iranica declines to print a Lakhmid king-list, giving only "late 4th century" for the dynasty's control of Ḥīra and 602 for Nuʿmān III's deposition — [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ḥira"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hira) — against the tidy 268–633 table of nineteen rulers at [Wikipedia, Lakhmid kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakhmid_kingdom), whose own page flags an internal date conflict for its first entry.
6. **Dhū Nuwās's accession** is given as 517, 522, or "518 or 522" across two articles — [Wikipedia, List of rulers of Saba and Himyar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rulers_of_Saba_and_Himyar) vs. [Wikipedia, Dhu Nuwas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhu_Nuwas); and the **Aksumite conquest** spans **525–543** in the scholarship (Munro-Hay 525; c. 530 on a 523 accession; Ryckmans as late as 543) — [Wikipedia, Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha).

### Inferences
- The pattern is consistent: the Arabic tradition **compresses** long processes into single dated events and **synchronises** them with the Prophet's life. The dam and the Elephant are both cases of a decades-long process being collapsed into one dramatic moment and pinned to c. 570.
- For a visual novel, the honest and the interesting choice coincide. The epigraphic version — a kingdom losing the capacity to maintain its dam over thirty years, a viceroy's northern campaign in 552 that the tradition later turned into an assault on the Kaʿba — is better drama than the legend, and it is what the stones say.

### Gaps
- The **whole methodological literature** on this tradition/epigraphy divergence (Robin, Gajda, Beaucamp, Beck) is behind paywalls or blocked databases. The report-writer should present the divergence as documented above but attribute the *arguments* only where a named scholar is cited in the sources I reached.
