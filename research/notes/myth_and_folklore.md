**Epistemic warning for the report-writer — read first.**

2. **The genuinely contemporary evidence is epigraphic (Safaitic, Nabataean, Sabaic) and it is almost silent on the beings the user is asking about.** I found **no** source placing jinn in Safaitic inscriptions. This is a real and important negative finding (see Gaps under Q1).

## Key Question 1: The jinn (الجن) — pre-Islamic belief vs. later Islamic demonology

### Takeaway
The pre-Islamic jinn, as far as the evidence allows reconstruction, were **local, tribal, morally ambivalent place-spirits** — closer to Roman *genii loci* or Palmyrene *gennayē* than to the fallen-angel demonology of later Islam. North of the Hijaz the words "jinnī" and "ilāh" (god) were used **interchangeably**, which is the single most story-useful fact in this whole section. The systematic demonology — jinn as a created species of smokeless fire, led by Iblīs, organised into believing and unbelieving sects, assigned one-per-human as a *qarīn* — is **Qurʾānic and post-Qurʾānic**, not pre-Islamic.

### Cited Findings

**Terminology and etymology**
- The root *j-n-n* means roughly "to hide" / "to conceal"; hence *majnūn* = "possessed by a jinnī," the ordinary Arabic word for insane — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn); [Mohamed, "The Jinn and the Shayatīn"](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA)
- Competing etymologies: Latin *genius* (disputed), Aramaic *ginnāyā* ("tutelary deity"), a possibly pre-Zoroastrian Persian *Jaini* (a wicked female spirit). Wensinck argued for a purely Arabic origin — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- "Genie" entered English through French, via 18th-century translations of the *Thousand and One Nights* — i.e. the word's familiar connotations are an 18th-century European overlay — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)

**Jinn as gods / place-spirits (the key pre-Islamic datum)**
- **In regions north of the Hijaz, including Palmyra and Baalbek, "jinnī" and "ilāh" (deity) were often used interchangeably** — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- Palmyrene *gennayē* are compared to jinn as **protectors of places and people**. Note: the source presents this as a *comparison*, not as an inscription naming jinn — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- Wellhausen (cited): jinn were thought to **haunt desolate desert places** — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- Muqātil ibn Sulaymān records that **Banū Ḥanīfa travellers sought refuge with a valley's "master" (sayyid) of jinn** — describing pre-Islamic practice, recorded later — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- A tradition attributed to ʿIkrima (in al-Rāzī's commentary) describes jinn **afflicting** people who sought refuge with them — i.e. the practice had risk attached — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- Qurʾān 72:6 condemns exactly this: travellers who "sought refuge with jinn" in fear increased the sin of both groups — [Al-Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Jinn)
- Pre-Islamic Arabs held that **angels were offspring of a union between God and jinn**, and worshipped jinn on that basis; the Qurʾān rejects it (6:100). The source gives no citation for this claim and it should be treated cautiously — [Mohamed, "The Jinn and the Shayatīn"](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA)
- An animal sacrifice at Mecca reported by ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, in which **an unseen creature shouted at the sacrificer** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī no. 3866) — [Mohamed](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA)
- **Relevant tutelary deities actually attested in Safaitic/ANA inscriptions** (the real contemporary layer): **Ruḍā** (رضى), major solar god, in Thamudic and Safaitic; **Shayʿ al-Qawm** (شيع القوم), god of war, night and caravan protection, described in inscriptions as "who drinks no wine, who builds no home"; **Allāt**; **Dushara** (mountain god, possibly an aspect of Ruḍā); **Yathaʿ** ("Savior"); **Nuhā** (solar/emotions, Najd inscriptions); **Aʿrā** (North Arabian tutelary god at Bosra, whose cult image was stained with sacrificial blood); **Abgal** (tutelary god of nomads in the Palmyra region) — [List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)
- **Fortune-spirits:** *Gad* (جد), a pan-Semitic god of fortune, also attested in Arabia; *Saʿd*, fortune god of Banū Kināna, whose cult image was **a tall stone in the desert** where animals were sacrificed for blessings — [List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)
- **Place-bound deities with strong story value:** *Dhāt-Badan*, goddess of the oasis, worshipped **in tree-circled pools**; *Dhāt-Anwāṭ*, a **tree deity between Mecca and Yathrib where devotees hung their weapons**; *Fils*, associated with animals, sanctuary on Jabal Ajaʾ; *Quzaḥ*, weather/mountain god at Muzdalifa — [List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)

**Jinn as tribal, marrying humans, kidnapping**
- **Al-Jāḥiẓ** (*Kitāb al-Ḥayawān*): jinn were thought to **have tribes**, and could **protect, marry, kidnap, possess, or kill** people. He classed jinn by behaviour, with *mārid* and *shayṭān* among the categories — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- "Love, marriage, and even children between humans and jinn were features of pre-Islamic Arab lore" — [Mohamed](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA) (note: the source attaches **no citation** to this sentence)
- The story of **the jinnī Manẓūr (منظور), who loved the woman Ḥabba (حبّة) and taught her healing** — undated — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- The claim that jinn can father children with humans rests on a hadith that **some scholars considered fabricated**; al-Suyūṭī records it. **Imām Mālik found no textual prohibition on human–jinn marriage but disapproved of it** — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn); [Mohamed](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA)
- Al-Kalbī's *Kitāb al-Aṣnām* (*Book of Idols*, a later source) tells of a jinnī named **Abū Thumāma** advising **ʿAmr ibn Luḥayy** — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- **Bilqīs** is described in later Islamic tradition as a **jinn-hybrid** — see Q5 — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)

**The Qurʾānic layer (dateable to the 7th century, i.e. inside the VN's window)**
- Jinn are mentioned **about 29 times, all in Meccan surahs**; created from "smokeless fire"; both humans and jinn were sent prophets — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- **Sūrat al-Jinn (72)**: 28 verses, Meccan, dated by Yusuf Ali and Muhammad Ali to **c. 2 BH**, when Muhammad was preaching near present-day Ṭāʾif. Content: jinn hear the Qurʾān and reject false gods (1–2); men and jinn criticised for ascribing offspring to God (3–7); **jinn and devils once spied at heaven's gates to get news for soothsayers and are now driven off by meteors (8–9)**; jinn fall into different classes, some Muslim, some unbelieving (10–14); believing jinn rewarded, unbelievers punished (15–18) — [Al-Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Jinn)
- **Muqātil's account**: jinn were created **before** humans and were **led by Iblīs**; the jinn then fought among themselves, and God sent angels to subdue them — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)

**Later layer, explicitly post-period**
- **ʿIfrīt (عفريت)** appears **once** in the Qurʾān (27:38–40), as an **epithet**, not a species-name — always in the phrase "among the jinn" — and **does not appear in pre-Islamic poetry**, though the related forms *ifriya* and *ifr* are recorded before the Qurʾān. Etymology: Arab philologists derive it from a root meaning to rub/roll in dust; Johnny Cheung argues for an Avestan *āfriti-* — [Ifrit, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifrit)
- The later folk ʿifrīt is **a ghost of the dead**: al-Ibshīhī's *Mustaṭraf* describes ifrits either as devils preying on women or as spirits of the dead; in Egyptian belief **a person who dies naturally has no malevolent ifrit, but a murdered person may produce a vengeful one drawn to the victim's blood — driving an unused nail into the blood prevents it**; martyrs, saints and prophets produce no ifrit at all. In folklore ifrits are chthonic spirits of the seven earths, formed of **smoke and fire** — contrasting with the Qurʾānic jinn of **smokeless** fire — [Ifrit, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifrit)
- **Mārid (مارد)**: a rebellious devil; in al-Jāḥiẓ one of his behavioural classes of jinn; in the *Nights* ("The Fisherman and the Jinnī") the second spirit from the jar may be a mārid, easily tricked — [Al-Rawi 2009, n.3](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf); [Ifrit, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifrit)
- **Nasnās (نسناس)**: Lane's description (19th c.) — "half a human being; having half a head, half a body, one arm, one leg, with which it hops with much agility." Attested in the *Nights* ("The Story of the Sage and the Scholar," where a man becomes a nasnās after a magician applies kohl to one eye), in some Shīʿī sources tied to Q 2:30, and in Flaubert and Borges. **Not found in any Sunni Qurʾān commentary.** A related Somali creature, the *xunguruuf*, is said to be the offspring of a jinn called *shiqq* and a human — [Nasnas, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasnas)
- **Al-Shiblī** (*Ākām al-Marjān*) cites two Ḥanbalī scholars who permitted **praying behind a jinnī** — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- **Ibn Taymiyya** held that jinn can possess people and that **belief in jinn is essential to Islam**; al-Qazwīnī (13th-c. MS) and the *Book of Wonders* (14th c.) supply the **illustrated** jinn — the visual tradition is 13th–14th century, not 6th — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)

### Inferences
- The interchangeability of *jinnī* and *ilāh* north of the Hijaz, plus the "master of the valley" practice, plus the Safaitic tutelary gods of specific mountains, pools and trees, converge on one reconstruction: **in the 5th–7th c. the supernatural was primarily territorial, not taxonomic.** A traveller's question was not "what species is this?" but "whose ground am I on, and have I paid?" This is the single most useful corrective to "Arabian Nights" fantasy, and should be the VN's organising principle.
- The Qurʾānic polemic is itself evidence: 72:6 attacks refuge-seeking with jinn, so the practice was live in the 610s–620s. The Qurʾān is thus a **contemporary witness to the belief it is attacking** — the most securely in-period source the user has.
- The "jinn eavesdrop at heaven's gates and are pelted by meteors" motif (72:8–9) is where the *kāhin* and the jinn interlock: it supplies the **mechanism** of soothsaying and simultaneously declares it defunct. For a VN set c. 610–630 this is a perfect dramatic hinge — the diviners' channel going dead.
- ʿIfrīt and mārid should be kept out of a 6th-century mouth as creature-categories. Their systematised, ranked use is medieval.

## Key Question 2: The jinn as the poet's inspiring companion — Wādī ʿAbqar and the named jinn-familiars

### Takeaway
The belief is real, well-attested in early Islamic akhbār, and **genuinely reflects pre-Islamic poetics**: poet (*shāʿir*) and soothsayer (*kāhin*) were **not clearly distinguished**, and the poet's gift was thought to come from an unseen companion. But the **tidy roster of named jinn paired one-to-one with named poets is late and unstable** — the names vary between sources, and the one list I could verify comes from a 9th/10th-century anthology via a low-grade Arabic reference site. **Two of the three names in the user's brief do not check out as given.**

### Cited Findings

**The core belief**
- **Al-Aʿshā (الأعشى, d. after 3/625)** credited a jinnī friend, **Misḥal (مسحل)**, with inspiring his poetry. Medieval sources call this spirit a *hājis* (هاجس); the modern scholar J. ʿAlī calls him a *tābiʿ* ("following spirit") — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn); [Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia)
- A variant writes the name **Misjal**; one opinion gives al-Aʿshā a possible **female** jinnī, **Jihinnām (جهنّام)**, though Yosefi notes this may instead name a human opponent — [Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia)
- **Ḥassān ibn Thābit (حسان بن ثابت, d. 54/674)** described an inspirational jinnī, in a verse surviving in two variants — [Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia); [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- **Al-Jāḥiẓ** (*al-Ḥayawān*) has a section on **"the shayāṭīn of the poets"**, stating that the great poets (*fuḥūl*, الفحول) were accompanied by *shayāṭīn* — [Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia)
- Yosefi's terminological point, important for the user: ***shayṭān* in the poetic sense is best translated "daemon," not "devil"** — the Qurʾānic sense is "devil," and conflating them distorts the pre-Islamic belief — [Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia)
- Yosefi's reconstruction: early *shuʿarāʾ* and *kuhhān* were **not clearly distinguished**; the late pre-Islamic poet was seen as **an artist whose imagination was linked to unseen powers, not as someone fully possessed**. The Qurʾān, by contrast, treats poets and soothsayers alike as inspired by *shayāṭīn* — [Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia)
- **The *qarīn* could inspire poets** in pre-Islamic Arabian belief, a spirit the source calls "reminiscent" of Greek *daimones*; and ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm uses *qarīn* in the **Muʿallaqāt** metaphorically — fear of his tribe is "a qarīn," a constant companion — [Qareen, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qareen)
- Caution: the "**dogs of the jinn**" (كلاب الجن) in ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm's poem is **likely a later copyist's variant, not his original wording** — a good example of how this corpus accreted — [Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia)

**Wādī ʿAbqar (وادي عبقر) and the named familiars**
Source quality warning: the only reachable source for the full roster is **Marefa** (an Arabic wiki), which cites only **Abū Zayd al-Qurashī's *Jamharat Ashʿār al-ʿArab* (جمهرة أشعار العرب)**. Al-Qurashī is "supposed by various scholars to have lived in the 8th, 9th or 10th centuries"; the anthology's own date is unknown and its selection of the seven odes is "somewhat idiosyncratic" — [Jamharat Ashʿar al-Arab, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamharat_Ash%27ar_al-Arab). **Treat the roster as a late-antique/early-medieval literary construct.**

- Wādī ʿAbqar is described as **a deep valley (وادٍ سحيق) in Najd**. Jinn poets are said to have lived there long; **anyone who spends a night in the valley may be visited by a jinn poet who teaches him verse**; each pre-Islamic poet supposedly had a jinn companion from the valley — [وادي عبقر, Marefa](https://www.marefa.org/%D9%88%D8%A7%D8%AF%D9%8A_%D8%B9%D8%A8%D9%82%D8%B1)
- **Calling someone *ʿabqarī* (عبقري, "genius") is said to derive from the valley's name** — [وادي عبقر, Marefa](https://www.marefa.org/%D9%88%D8%A7%D8%AF%D9%8A_%D8%B9%D8%A8%D9%82%D8%B1)
- The roster as Marefa gives it:
  - **Lāfiẓ ibn Lāḥiẓ (لافظ بن لاحظ)** — companion of **Imruʾ al-Qays** ✅ *name as in the user's brief, VERIFIED in this source*
  - **Hubayd ibn al-Ṣalādim (هبيد بن الصلادم)** — companion of **ʿAbīd ibn al-Abraṣ** and **Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim**. ⚠️ **Marefa spells it al-Ṣalādim, NOT al-Ṣalāṣil as in the user's brief.** The report should flag the variant rather than silently pick one.
  - **Hādhir ibn Māhir (هاذر بن ماهر)** — companion of **al-Nābigha al-Dhubyānī**
  - **Mudrik ibn Wāghim (مدرك بن واغم)** — companion of **al-Kumayt ibn Zayd** (note: al-Kumayt is an **Umayyad-era** poet, which shows the list is not purely Jāhilī)
  - **Mismaḥl al-Sakrān ibn Jundal (مسحل السكران بن جندل)** — companion of **al-Aʿshā**. ⚠️ Marefa gives "Mismaḥl," not "Misḥal"; the independent scholarly source (Yosefi, above) gives **Misḥal/Misjal** for al-Aʿshā's jinnī, so **Misḥal is the better-supported form** and Marefa's "Mismaḥl" is likely a corruption.
  - **Jālid ibn Ẓill (جالد بن ظل)** — companion of **ʿAntara ibn Shaddād**
  - **Al-Saffāḥ ibn al-Raqrāq (السفاح بن الرقراق)** — companion of **al-Ḥārith ibn Muḍāḍ** — all from [وادي عبقر, Marefa](https://www.marefa.org/%D9%88%D8%A7%D8%AF%D9%8A_%D8%B9%D8%A8%D9%82%D8%B1)

**The later literary elaboration (explicitly post-period)**
- **Ibn Shuhayd's *Risālat al-Tawābiʿ wa-al-Zawābiʿ* (رسالة التوابع والزوابع)** — an Andalusī literary travelogue in which the narrator's own familiar spirit is named **Zuhayr**, and which **interviews the daemons of Imruʾ al-Qays, Ṭarafa, Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm, Abū Tammām, al-Buḥturī, Abū Nuwās and Abū al-Ṭayyib (al-Mutanabbī)**. Yosefi judges it **less useful for reconstructing collective belief** than popular anecdotes, because it is a literary composition alluding to the tradition rather than reporting it — [Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia)

**Living parallel (modern, but illuminating)**
- In contemporary Yemen the **hājis (هاجس)** is a **male** muse, "a friend in need" who supplies wording when the poet asks for help; the **ḥalīla (حليلة)** is a **female** muse associated with imagination. Rodionov reports Ḥaḍramīs saying **she may drive a poet mad by overstimulating his mind**. Caton uses *hājis* for poetic talent and *ḥalīla* for imagination; Miller associates *ḥalīla* with raw, fleeting energy. Yosefi notes *hājis* is specifically linked to **al-Aʿshā's story, set in Yemen on his way to Ḥaḍramawt**, but he does **not** claim direct continuity — [Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia)

### Inferences
- The **male-helper / female-madness** split in the living Yemeni tradition (hājis vs ḥalīla) is an exceptionally good VN mechanic and has at least a suggestive, non-continuous link to the classical material. It gives a poet-character two possible relationships to inspiration rather than one.
- The roster of named jinn is **best used in-fiction as something a character cites, not as ground truth** — which is also historically accurate, since that is exactly how it reaches us. A poet in-game boasting of "Lāfiẓ ibn Lāḥiẓ" is period-plausible as a claim to lineage of inspiration.
- That the list includes an Umayyad poet (al-Kumayt) and that the names vary between sources confirms it was still growing in the Islamic period. The VN should avoid presenting it as an ancient fixed canon.

- **Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī's *Muʿjam al-Buldān* entry on ʿAbqar** — not reachable. This would be the authoritative geographical attestation and the report should name it as the next step. (al-maktaba.org / shamela.ws host the Arabic text but I could not retrieve the entry.)
- No source I reached confirms **Wādī ʿAbqar's actual location** beyond "a deep valley in Najd." Whether it was ever a real identifiable place is unresolved.
- **Michael Sells** and **Suzanne Stetkevych** on the mythic substrate of the *qaṣīda*: I could confirm only the bibliographic fact of Stetkevych's *The Mute Immortals Speak: Pre-Islamic Poetry and the Poetics of Ritual* (Cornell UP, Myth and Poetics series) — [MIT Press Bookstore listing](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780801480461); [Project MUSE book page](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/68389). I could not access its argument, so **I report no findings from it.** Its thesis (the *qaṣīda* as rite of passage) is the natural scholarly frame for this whole section and the report should flag it as an unread key source.

## Key Question 3: The ghūl, siʿlāh, shiqq and related beings — what is pre-Islamic?

### Takeaway
The **ghūl (الغول) is the best-attested genuinely pre-Islamic Arabian monster**, a shape-shifting, usually female desert predator that lures travellers — and crucially, **a kind of jinn, not a separate species**. The *siʿlāh/siʿlwa* is its female counterpart, the *shiqq/nasnās* a distinct one-sided being. The most important single fact for the user: a canonical hadith **explicitly denies the ghūl's existence**, alongside four other pre-Islamic beliefs — which proves all five were live beliefs in the 7th century and gives the VN a dateable religious fault line.

### Cited Findings

The principal scholarly source for this section is **Ahmed Al-Rawi, "The Mythical Ghoul in Arabic Culture," *Cultural Analysis* 8 (2009): 45–69** — open access, with responses by Aref Abu-Rabia and Amira El-Zein in the same issue: [berkeley.edu PDF](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)

**Ghūl (الغول)**
- Etymology: from *ghāla* (غال), "to seize." An earlier etymology proposed a root meaning "to kill," citing **Ibn Manẓūr (1232–1311)**. Male = *ghūl*, female = *ghūla* (غولة); **early Arabic treated "ghūl" as feminine**, and only later did it become masculine with *siʿlāh* as its feminine counterpart — [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)
- **Ghouls belong to the jinn**, and the concept originated in pre-Islamic Arabian religion — [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)
- Al-Rawi's **Mesopotamian hypothesis**: the Akkadian underworld demon **Gallu**, said to have abducted the vegetation god Damuzi (Tammuz), may have influenced the Arabic ghūl through Bedouin–Akkadian trade contact. Al-Rawi presents the *ghāl*/Gallu root-link as an etymological *suggestion*, not a demonstration — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **Al-Jāḥiẓ (*al-Ḥayawān*)**: ghouls **lure travellers with night fires**; the *siʿlwa* is a female genie; a ghūl is a genie that harasses travellers. **A single sword blow kills the siʿlwa, while two strikes prolong its life** — a vivid and specific piece of folk combat-lore. Al-Jāḥiẓ also offers a **rationalist** account: fear and solitude in the desert cause hallucinations — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **Physical form:** generally female, ugly, shape-shifting, sometimes into attractive women. **Al-Masʿūdī gives the ghūl cloven, goat-like feet; al-Manāwī gives ass-like feet.** Al-Rawi summarises the composite as **snake + goat + ass, with fangs and cloven feet**. Al-Qazwīnī describes it as ugly and notes Arabs stressed its repulsive features. Doughty's Bedouin informant (19th c.) described **a cyclops eye, beak-like jaws, tusks, bird-like arms, an ass's hoof and an ostrich's foot** — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **It takes the form of the person it most recently ate.** It could appear male or female but usually appeared female to lure male travellers. In tales told to girls and young women, a **"Mother Ghoul"** appears to men as a long-lost female relative or an unassuming old woman; **female relatives often see through the illusion, and men survive only if they believe them** — [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)
- **Al-Dimashqī**: ghouls are cave-dwelling animals that leave only at night, avoid sunlight, and eat both humans and animals — [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)
- **Al-Qazwīnī's origin myth:** meteors thrown at eavesdropping devils turned some into **crocodiles** and others into **ghouls** — directly linking the ghūl to the Qurʾānic meteor-pelting of 72:8–9 — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **Al-Masʿūdī's *Murūj al-Dhahab* preserves a creation myth from the older works of Ibn Isḥāq and Wahb ibn Munabbih**, distributing the beings by terrain: **the ghūl lives in the wilderness, the *quṭrub* is cat-like, the devils settle in the seas, and the *siʿlwa* dwells in waste areas** — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **Quṭrub (قطرب)** is described (al-Rawi n.2, citing **Ibn Durayd**) as **the male ghoul** — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb and the ghūl:** travelling to Syria, he met a female ghūl which **asked where he was going and turned its head all the way around to frighten him**; he struck it between shoulder and neck, and when he returned later the body was gone. Al-Rawi files this among pre-Islamic tales, though **ʿUmar was a Muslim caliph, so the dating is internally inconsistent** — flagged by al-Rawi himself — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf); also [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)

**Shiqq (شقّ) and nasnās (نسناس)**
- **Shiqq, or nasnās, means "half,"** and the being is described as having **one eye, one hand, one leg** — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- Al-Rawi cites **a pre-Islamic story of ʿAlqama ibn Ṣafwān, who died fighting a shiqq at Yaḥūmān** — one of the few explicitly pre-Islamic monster-combat narratives in the literature — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **Al-Qazwīnī details the shiqq and "Dilhāb"** (likely the *dalhān*) — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)

**The ghūl in hadith — and the denial (the key dating evidence)**
- The Prophet is said to have advised **Abū Dharr** to pray against the mischief of human and jinn devils, calling the creatures ghouls — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **Abū Ayyūb al-Anṣārī's dates were eaten by ghouls**; the Prophet advised a formula, and later **Āyat al-Kursī** (the Throne Verse, Q 2:255), which worked — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **Al-Damīrī** cites a hadith that lonely travellers escape a ghūl's attack by **reciting the adhān**; and a ghūl, unlike a demon, **may convert to Islam** on hearing the Throne Verse — [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)
- **The denial: Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2220.** Chapter title in Arabic: **باب لا عدوى ولا طِيَرَةَ ولا هَامَةَ ولا صَفَرَ** — "There Is No *ʿAdwā*, No *Ṭiyara* (evil omens), No *Hāma*, No *Ṣafar*, No *Nawāʾ*, And No **Ghūl**." Hadith 2220a (Abū Hurayra), Arabic: **لا عدوى ولا صفر ولا هامة**. A desert Arab objects that camels in the sand look healthy as deer until a scabbed camel mixes with them; the Prophet replies, **"Who infected the first one?"** — [Sahih Muslim 2220a, sunnah.com](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2220a); chapter listing also at [sounah.com](https://sounah.com/en/bab/4960/)
- **Ibn Jābir** transmits: "No ghoul, no *ʿadwā*, and no *ṭayra*" — denying the ghūl's existence. Later scholars reconciled the contradiction by saying **ghouls existed earlier and then perished**, or that they merely **lost the power to change shape** — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **The Muʿtazilites denied that ghouls existed** at all — [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)
- **Ghouls are not mentioned in the Qurʾān** — [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)

**Hyena-lore (adjacent, and strongly pre-Islamic in feel)**
- **Al-Damīrī**: hyenas dig graves for human flesh, and Arabs compared ugly women to hyenas — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)

**Later European afterlife, to be kept out of the VN**
- **Antoine Galland's** French *Thousand and One Nights* introduced the ghoul to Europe as **a corpse-eater in cemeteries** — a narrowing not present in the Arabic material. The English word first appears in **William Beckford's *Vathek* (1786)** — [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)
- The Persian **Ghūl-e Bīyābān** inhabits the wildernesses of Afghanistan and Iran — a regional, not Arabian, form — [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)

### Inferences
- **Muslim 2220 is the single most valuable source in this entire research brief for the user's stated purpose.** It is a canonical 7th-century-referent text that *names five pre-Islamic supernatural beliefs in one breath in order to abolish them*: contagion (*ʿadwā*), bird-omens (*ṭiyara*), the death-owl (*hāma*), the *ṣafar* belief, and the ghūl. Anything on that list is **certified live belief in the VN's window**. The report should foreground this.
- The ghūl's defining trait is **not** corpse-eating (that is Galland's Europeanisation) but **predatory impersonation**: it wears the face of whoever it last ate, and it lures with a fire in the night. That is the period-authentic horror and it is far better drama than a graveyard scavenger.
- The "Mother Ghoul" gender dynamic — women see through the disguise, men do not, and men survive only by believing women — is a ready-made scene structure, though the source does not date it, so it should be treated as folk-tale rather than 6th-century.
- Al-Jāḥiẓ's two-blow rule ("one stroke kills, two revive") is the kind of concrete, internally-consistent folk rule that makes invented worlds feel researched. It is 9th-century, but it is reporting popular lore.
- ʿIfrīt should be used, if at all, in its folk sense as **the ghost of the murdered** — which rhymes exactly with the *hāma* below, suggesting a single underlying Arabian idea: **violent death produces a persisting, vengeance-seeking entity.**

### Gaps
- **Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's ghūl encounter**: see Q4 — partially verified, with an important discrepancy.
- **Hātif (هاتف, the disembodied voice)**: **I found no source for it.** Al-Rawi's article explicitly does not mention *hātif*, *ghaddār*, *dalhān* (except as "Dilhāb") or *daʿlah*. The ʿUmar story's talking ghūl and the shouting unseen creature at the Meccan sacrifice are the nearest attested things to a disembodied voice, but **the term *hātif* itself is unverified here.**
- **Tābiʿa (تابعة)**: only the masculine *tābiʿ* is attested, and only as a **modern scholar's label** (J. ʿAlī) for al-Aʿshā's Misḥal, plus in the title of Ibn Shuhayd's 11th-century *Risālat al-Tawābiʿ*. **The feminine *tābiʿa* as a distinct being is unverified.**
- **Rūḥ (روح)** as a discrete Arabian supernatural being, as opposed to the general word for spirit/breath: no source reached treats it as a creature. Recommend dropping it from the creature-list.
- Whether the **siʿlāh** is attested in actual pre-Islamic poetry (as opposed to being reported by al-Jāḥiẓ and al-Masʿūdī) — unresolved. The EI "Ghūl" entry would settle it.

## Key Question 4: The hāma/ṣadā — the owl-bird of the unavenged dead

### Takeaway
The *hāma* (هامة) is **certified pre-Islamic by the fact of its denial in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2220**, and it is the most striking supernatural image in the whole corpus: the soul or blood of a murdered man rises from his skull as a bird, circling his tomb and crying for drink, and it will not rest until the murderer's blood slakes it. But the English-language sourcing is thin, old, and partly unreliable, and **I could find nothing at all on the *ṣadā*.**

### Cited Findings
- **Al-Rawi (2009, p. 59)** is the scholarly anchor: before Islam, **"the predatory bird *hāmah* or the owl" was a bad omen**; pre-Islamic belief held that **the bones or souls of the dead could turn into predatory birds**; and if a murder went unavenged, **the hāma was believed to be a worm leaving the victim's skull, circling the tomb and saying "water me."** Al-Rawi also reports that **Jews in Arabia believed the hāma circled a man's tomb for seven days before departing**, and that the Prophet "emphasized the falsity" of the belief — all cited via [esoterx summary of Al-Rawi 2009 p.59](https://esoterx.com/2013/05/05/the-theriomorphic-arabian-hamah-an-angry-bird-before-it-was-cool/), original at [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **Note the internal variation:** al-Rawi gives the hāma as **a worm** emerging from the skull in the unavenged-murder version, while also linking *hāma* to **the owl** as a bad omen. These may be two distinct beliefs fused in the later tradition. The report should not smooth this over.
- **Edward William Lane (1843, p. 35)**: some believed the hāma **formed from the blood near a dead person's brain**, others that it was animated by **"the soul of him that is unjustly slain."** Lane gives the cry as **"Iskoonee! Iskoonee!"** glossed **"Give ye me to drink!"** Once vengeance is taken, the bird flies away — [Lane, *Selections from the Ḳur-án* (1843)](https://esoterx.com/2013/05/05/the-theriomorphic-arabian-hamah-an-angry-bird-before-it-was-cool/)
- **F. E. Hulme (1886, pp. 141–42)**: the hāma "is said to spring from the blood of a murdered man"; its cry is "Iskoonee," "give me to drink"; and the bird **"rests not, day nor night, till its thirst is quenched in the murderer's blood"** — [Hulme, *Myth-land* (1886)](https://esoterx.com/2013/05/05/the-theriomorphic-arabian-hamah-an-angry-bird-before-it-was-cool/)
- **Mesopotamian parallel**: Jastrow's translation of the Descent of Ishtar texts describes the dead as **"clothed like birds"** — [Jastrow, *The Civilization of Babylonia and Assyria* (1915), pp. 453–54](https://esoterx.com/2013/05/05/the-theriomorphic-arabian-hamah-an-angry-bird-before-it-was-cool/)
- **The Islamic replacement image**: a hadith in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, Kitāb al-Imāra, has martyrs' souls living **in green birds** until Judgment Day — the same soul-as-bird structure, reassigned from the unavenged murder victim to the martyr — [esoterx, citing Sahih Muslim Kitab al-Imara](https://esoterx.com/2013/05/05/the-theriomorphic-arabian-hamah-an-angry-bird-before-it-was-cool/)
- **The denial**: *hāma* is the third item in the Muslim 2220 chapter heading — **ولا هَامَةَ** — and appears in the Arabic text of 2220a: **لا عدوى ولا صفر ولا هامة** — [Sahih Muslim 2220a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2220a)

### Inferences
- The *hāma* is the **mythic engine of the blood-feud**, and therefore the point where this research file connects directly to the sibling file on the *Ayyām al-ʿArab*. If an unavenged killing literally produces a thirsty thing that will not stop crying, then vengeance is not merely honour — it is **laying a ghost**. For a VN this converts tribal feud from a social obligation into a supernatural one, and it is period-authentic.
- The Islamic green-birds hadith looks like deliberate **substitution** rather than simple abolition: the soul-bird survives, but it belongs to the martyr and it is at peace, not thirsty. That is a theologically and dramatically rich transition for a story set across the religious change.
- The *hāma*/*ʿifrīt*-of-the-murdered convergence (Q3) suggests the underlying Arabian intuition is that **violent death leaves something behind that is owed something.** Treat it as one belief with several names rather than several creatures.

### Gaps
- **The ṣadā (صدى): I found no source whatsoever.** No reachable source pairs *ṣadā* with *hāma*, and the one dedicated hāma source explicitly states it does not mention *ṣadā*. **The report must not assert the hāma/ṣadā pairing.** (The word's ordinary sense is "echo," which is suggestive of a disembodied-voice belief, but that is my speculation, not a finding.)
- **Primary pre-Islamic poetic attestation of the hāma** — not located. Lane (1843) and Hulme (1886) are Victorian compilers, not primary sources, and al-Rawi is reporting the belief rather than citing a Jāhilī verse. **The belief's pre-Islamic status rests on the hadith denial, not on a dated poem.** That is sound but worth stating precisely.
- Whether the hāma was specifically an **owl** (*būma*, بومة) or a non-specific bird of prey, or a worm, is genuinely unclear in the sources — a real contradiction, not a gap in my searching.
- Goldziher, Wellhausen, W. Robertson Smith and Henninger are the scholars who treated this material systematically; **none of their relevant work was reachable**, and the one page summarising the hāma explicitly does not cite them.

## Key Question 5: Taʾabbaṭa Sharran, al-Shanfarā and the ṣaʿālīk as sources of supernatural imagery

### Takeaway
The brigand-poets (*ṣaʿālīk*, صعاليك) are the richest in-period vein of uncanny material the user has, because their poetry stages the poet as **having crossed over** — kin to wolves and hyenas, at home where humans are not, able to meet a ghūl and win. Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's ghūl-fight is genuinely attested in early Islamic akhbār, but with a **discrepancy in the creature's form** between sources, and the attribution of al-Shanfarā's famous *Lāmiyya* has been **disputed since medieval times**.

### Cited Findings

**Taʾabbaṭa Sharran (تأبط شرا)**
- His real name was **Thābit ibn Jābir ibn Sufyān** — [Al-Rawi 2009, n.6](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- He is **"the most famous figure in a ghoul story"** — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **In al-Rawi's version, the ghūl appears in the shape of a ram.** ⚠️ This is a notable, specific detail and it differs from the vaguer "fight with a ghoul" in the general accounts. Al-Rawi cites **al-Iṣfahānī, *Kitāb al-Aghānī* (1983 ed., p. 144)** — so the story is attested in a **10th-century** anthology, i.e. early Islamic akhbār, not a pre-Islamic source — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- **A famous poem narrates the fight**, and **al-Jāḥiẓ denounced Taʾabbaṭa Sharran for boasting about his victory** — i.e. a 9th-century rationalist treating the claim as a lie, which is itself excellent material — [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)
- **Al-Maʿarrī** likewise treated the story as **a myth contrary to reason** — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)
- Al-Maʿarrī's *Risālat al-Ghufrān* places **both Taʾabbaṭa Sharran and al-Shanfarā in hell** — [Al-Shanfara, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shanfara)

**Al-Shanfarā (الشنفرى)**
- Died **c. 525 CE**; described as **"semi-legendary"**, known mainly through poems attributed to him — [Al-Shanfara, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shanfara)
- Famous for the **Lāmiyyat al-ʿArab (لامية العرب)**, "the L-poem of the Arabs," presenting a first-person outlaw living at the edge of society and criticising it from outside. **Its attribution has been disputed since medieval times**; by contrast the *Tāʾiyya* attributed to him is rarely disputed — [Al-Shanfara, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shanfara)
- **Taʾabbaṭa Sharran was al-Shanfarā's companion.** Scholars read the poem's ***umm ʿiyāl* (أم عيال, "mother of the hearth and home") section as describing Taʾabbaṭa Sharran**, who provisioned their band — a gender inversion the sources do not fully explain. Both were reportedly among the very few men who **could run down an antelope** — [Al-Shanfara, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shanfara)
- **The death and the skull — a complete revenge-myth:** he was traditionally killed in revenge for killing **Ḥaram ibn Jābir**, who had murdered his father. Ambushed at night, bound, taken back to the enemy tribe; asked where he wished to be buried, he answered in verse that **burial was forbidden to them, and told the hyena to rejoice** (the only animal reference on the page). He had vowed to kill **100 men of the Azd and died having killed 99**. Later **an Azdī man kicked his skull, took a splinter, and died of the infection** — which the tradition treats as **completing the vow** — [Al-Shanfara, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shanfara)
- Terminological caution: early sources **sometimes confused the *ṣaʿālīk* with the "crows of the Arabs" (أغربان العرب / aghribat al-ʿArab)**, Arabs with African mothers; al-Shanfarā is counted in that group by some and rejected by others — [Al-Shanfara, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shanfara)

**ʿAntara ibn Shaddād (عنترة بن شداد) — the historical poet**
- Lived **c. 525–608 CE**, born in Qusaiba (al-Qaṣṣīm), died at Ḥāʾil aged c. 82–83; his mother was an Ethiopian woman named **Zabība (زبيبة)**; one of his poems is in the **Muʿallaqāt**, the seven "hanging odes" legendarily suspended at the Kaʿba — [Antarah ibn Shaddad, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- He is also assigned the jinn-familiar **Jālid ibn Ẓill** in the ʿAbqar roster (Q2)

### Inferences
- **The *ṣaʿālīk* are the user's best single answer to "how do I do Arabian supernatural without Arabian Nights."** The uncanny here is not a creature-roster; it is a *condition* — the outlaw who has become slightly inhuman, whose kin are wolves and hyenas, who sleeps where ghouls walk. That is in-period (al-Shanfarā d. c. 525, ʿAntara d. 608, both inside the VN's window) and it is literary rather than folkloric, so it carries the prestige of poetry rather than the taint of superstition.
- The **al-Jāḥiẓ and al-Maʿarrī debunkings** are a gift: they let the VN stage **9th-century-style scepticism** as a position a character can hold, without anachronism, because the sceptics were arguing against exactly this story.
- Al-Shanfarā's skull completing the vow after his death is structurally the same idea as the *hāma*: **the unfinished claim of the dead acts in the world.** The two should be presented together in the report as one Arabian intuition rather than two unrelated anecdotes.
- The **ram-shaped ghūl** (al-Aghānī) vs. the shape-shifting female lurer (al-Jāḥiẓ, folk tales) is a genuine source conflict. For the VN this is usable as *variation in testimony*, which is more interesting than a settled bestiary entry.

### Gaps
- **The content of Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's ghūl poem**: al-Rawi explicitly "does not describe the poem's full content," and the English Wikipedia article on Taʾabbaṭa Sharran returned URL-not-found on two attempts. **I cannot report what the poem actually says**, only that it narrates a fight and that he boasts of the victory. The *Kitāb al-Aghānī* text (p. 144 in the 1983 edition) is the place to look; Arabic text is on shamela.ws / al-warraq.com but I could not retrieve the passage.
- **The supernatural content of the *Lāmiyyat al-ʿArab*** — the Wikipedia article "says little about this" and **does not mention wolves or kinship with wild beasts at all**. The famous wolf/hyena/wild-ass "family" passage, which the user's brief assumes, is therefore **unverified in my sources**. Only the hyena in his burial-verses is attested. The report should say so rather than assert the wolf-kinship from general reputation.
- **Library of Arabic Literature** open-access volumes include relevant ṣaʿālīk material, but I did not reach the LAL site in this session.

## Key Question 6: The extinct Arabs (العرب البائدة) — ʿĀd, Thamūd, Ṭasm, Jadīs, Jurhum, the ʿAmāliq

### Takeaway
These peoples sit in **three incompatible registers** that the report must keep apart: (1) the **Qurʾānic** register, where ʿĀd and Thamūd are moral exempla destroyed for rejecting prophets; (2) the **akhbār** register, where Ṭasm/Jadīs, Jurhum and the rest get dynastic genealogies, tyrant-kings and 6th-century dates; and (3) the **epigraphic/archaeological** register, which — crucially — **contradicts the first two for Thamūd**, showing a real people who survived for centuries *after* their supposed annihilation, and whose supposed city is in fact Nabataean. Pre-Islamic poets already treated Thamūd as a long-lost people, so **the "lost race" idea itself is in-period**, even though it was factually wrong.

### Cited Findings

**ʿĀd (عاد) and Iram dhāt al-ʿImād (إرم ذات العماد)**
- Qurʾānic: **Q 89:6–14**, where **89:7** refers to "the people of Iram" with their great stature; the same passage names **ʿĀd, Thamūd and Pharaoh**, with the punishment at 89:13. Arabic *Iram dhāt al-ʿimād*, sometimes rendered **"Iram of the tentpoles"** — and **"the pillars" may well be tent-poles**, not architecture. Interpreters split on whether Iram is a city, an area, or a tribe — [Iram of the Pillars, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iram_of_the_Pillars)
- ʿĀd appears in **Hūd (11), al-Aʿrāf (7), al-Aḥqāf (46), al-Ḥāqqa (69), al-Qamar (54), al-Fajr (89)**, among others; **sūra 11 is named after their prophet Hūd**, whom some identify with the biblical Eber — [Hud (prophet), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hud_(prophet))
- They are described as **powerful builders, "tall in stature and great builders,"** settling a large part of southern Arabia, their monuments and pillared structures linked to Iram — [Hud, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hud_(prophet))
- **Destruction:** after a **three-year famine** and continued refusal to heed warnings, a severe wind destroyed them — the Qurʾān's **al-rīḥ al-ʿaqīm (الريح العقيم)**, "the barren wind," one that brings no benefit to life — [Hud, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hud_(prophet))
- **Location: al-Aḥqāf (الأحقاف), "the Sandy Plains,"** believed to be in South Arabia, possibly eastern Yemen or western Oman — [Hud, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hud_(prophet))
- **Identification attempts and their failure:** Nicholas Clapp (1998) proposed Iram = Ubar = the site of **Shiṣr in Oman**; most scholars reject it and **Clapp later disavowed the Shiṣr identification**. Ubar was described in ancient and medieval sources as **a land, not a city**. Archaeologist **Juris Zarins**, who worked the 1991 settlement proposed as Ubar, **concluded it did not represent a city called Ubar**, arguing the name referred to a region and its people. Edgell (2004) treats the "lost city of the Arabian Sands" as a myth — [Iram of the Pillars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iram_of_the_Pillars); [Hud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hud_(prophet))
- **The most plausible candidate the source offers is Wādī Ramm, Jordan**, where **ancient inscriptions at the al-Lāt temple near Jabal Ramm may mention Iram and possibly ʿĀd** — [Iram of the Pillars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iram_of_the_Pillars)
- **Pre-Qurʾānic Palmyrene inscriptions mention people named Hūd and the people of ʿĀd** — [Hud, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hud_(prophet))
- **Hūd's tomb: Qabr Hūd (قبر هود) in Ḥaḍramawt, Yemen**, the best-known site, drawing frequent pilgrims; rival sites near the Zamzam well and in the Umayyad Mosque — [Hud, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hud_(prophet))
- **Later accretions, explicitly post-period:** the *Thousand and One Nights* tale of Iram (**nights 277–279**), and the mystic **al-Dabbāgh's** claim that the tents were gold-plated and that gold coins remain buried there — [Iram of the Pillars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iram_of_the_Pillars)

**Thamūd (ثمود), Ṣāliḥ and the she-camel — and the clearest Qurʾān-vs-archaeology clash**
- Attested in outside sources **from the 8th century BCE to the 5th century CE**. Several possibly unrelated groups may have borne the name; they probably spoke **Old Arabic** — [Thamud, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
- Qurʾānic count: the article gives **two different figures, "twenty-six times" and "twenty-three times"** — an internal inconsistency worth noting — [Thamud, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
- Qurʾānic narrative: God sent **Ṣāliḥ (صالح)**; they demanded a sign; God sent **a she-camel (نَاقَةُ ٱللَّه, nāqat Allāh)** to graze freely (Q 7:73) which they must not harm; **they hamstrung it** (Q 7:77); Ṣāliḥ warned them **three days remained** (Q 11:65); Ṣāliḥ reminded them of the **stone castles and palaces they built, and of the fate of ʿĀd** (Q 7:74, referring to palaces on plains and homes carved into mountains); **Sūrat al-Naml 27:48–49** says **nine men plotted** to kill Ṣāliḥ and his family and were struck down three days later; all the disbelievers died and Ṣāliḥ and the believers survived (Q 7:78–79) — [Salih, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salih)
- The city is **al-Ḥijr (الحجر), "The Stonetown,"** identified with the Nabataean city of **Hegra** — [Salih, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salih)
- **Exegetical additions (al-Ṭabarī and others), NOT Qurʾānic:** Ṣāliḥ summoned the people to a mountain where **a rock split open to reveal the she-camel and her calf**; the camel **drank from the water source on alternate days, and gave milk on the days she was barred from water**; God told Ṣāliḥ that **a boy born to the tribe would hamstring her**, and the boy **grew unnaturally fast**; **the calf cried out three times**, and the people's faces turned **yellow, then red, then black**, and they died on the third day. Some scholars say **Hājar, mother of Ismāʿīl, was Ṣāliḥ's granddaughter**. A similar tradition appears in **John of Damascus's 8th-century commentary** and in Ibn Kathīr — [Salih, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salih); [Thamud, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
- **The historical/epigraphic record, which does not match:**
  - **Sargon II's Annals** name the **"Ta-mu-di"** among desert Arabs whom Sargon defeated and deported to Samaria; the historian **Israel Ephʿal doubts the account**, suggesting a trade arrangement embellished as submission — [Thamud, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
  - A 6th-c. BCE **Nabonidus** letter mentions a **"Te-mu-da-a Ar-ba-a-a,"** probably a merchant or official — [Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
  - **Agatharchides** (2nd c. BCE) places them on the coast south of the Gulf of Aqaba; **Diodorus** (1st c. BCE) near Yemen; **Ptolemy and Pliny** give conflicting locations — [Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
  - **The *Notitia Dignitatum* lists two Thamud units in Roman service, in Egypt and Palestine** — i.e. they were still a going concern centuries after their alleged annihilation — [Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
  - **Two Safaitic inscriptions mention a war between Gšm and "the tribe of Thamūd"** — [Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
  - **The Ruwāfa temple inscription (c. 165–169 CE)**, bilingual Greek and Nabataean Aramaic, says **"the Thamūd of Robathū" built a temple to the deity ʾlhʾ with Roman support**; these Thamūd were **Roman auxiliary troops** — [Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
  - **"Thamudic" inscriptions** — about **15,000** of them — are a residual, unclassified category, and **epigraphists generally agree there is no evidence linking them to the Thamūd tribe**. The label is a misnomer and the report should say so — [Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
  - **The stone constructions at Hegra are mostly Nabataean, especially 1st century CE.** The rock-cut tombs adjacent to Hegra were used by members of various religious groups; the Wikipedia article on Ṣāliḥ **does not describe them as Thamūd dwellings** — [Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud); [Salih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salih)
  - A hadith in **Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī** has Muhammad calling Hegra **"the land of Thamūd"** and warning against drinking its water — so the identification is early-Islamic, not modern — [Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
  - **Ibn Saʿd** (9th c.) **identified the Thamūd with the Nabataeans** — an early recognition of the problem — [Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
  - **No archaeological evidence for a catastrophe** is reported — [Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
- **The decisive datum for the user:** ***Pre-Islamic poets already described the Thamūd as a long-lost tribe***, even though inscriptions and Roman records show they survived for centuries after — [Thamud, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)
- The site is called **Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ (مدائن صالح), "the cities of Ṣāliḥ."** Ṣāliḥ's resting place is variously given as **Hasik (Oman)** and **Maqām Nebī Ṣāliḥ in Acre** — [Salih, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salih)

**Ṭasm (طسم) and Jadīs (جديس)**
- Two extinct tribes who dominated **al-Yamāma** (Najd), traditionally linked to **Shem son of Noah**; active from around **110 BCE** until the Ḥimyarite Kingdom destroyed them in the **6th century CE**; presented as contemporaries of ʿĀd, Thamūd and Amalek — [Tasm and Jadis, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasm_and_Jadis)
- Genealogy: **Ṭasm a son of Lūd**; Jadīs's father disputed, **Lūd or Aram**; the Buyid-period historian **Miskawayh** (*Tajārib al-umam*) made both sons of Lūd — [Tasm and Jadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasm_and_Jadis)
- **The tyrant and the *jus primae noctis* motif:** both tribes were ruled by a king named **ʿImlīq (عمليق)**, who **claimed the right to deflower Jadīs's virgins before their marriages**. Another tradition instead has him decree full control over the trade routes. Jadīs resented him; **when he attended a special performance, Jadīs men drew swords hidden in the sand and killed him** — [Tasm and Jadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasm_and_Jadis)
- **The Ḥimyarite reprisal:** **a woman of Jadīs warned her people of an invasion, but they ignored her**; the Ḥimyarites entered al-Yamāma, killed the unprepared Jadīs, and **took the children into slavery**. Dated to the **6th century CE**, under **Ḥassān Yuhaʾmin**. Ṭasm later became extinct too — [Tasm and Jadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasm_and_Jadis)
- Al-Ṭabarī's version: **the Ḥimyarite armies wiped out Jadīs in the 6th century CE in retaliation for the assassination of the king of Ṭasm, an ally of Ḥimyar**; adults slaughtered, children enslaved — [Extinct Arabs, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinct_Arabs), citing *The History of al-Ṭabarī* vol. 4
- **Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī's *Muʿjam al-Buldān* describes palaces and fortresses built by both tribes**; the **Banū Ḥanīfa** later used those palaces — [Tasm and Jadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasm_and_Jadis)
- Other sources named: **Ibn Saʿīd al-Andalusī, *Nashwat al-Ṭarab***; **Hughes' *Dictionary of Islam*** for the trade-route tradition — [Tasm and Jadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasm_and_Jadis)

**Zarqāʾ al-Yamāma (زرقاء اليمامة)**
- A legendary **blue-eyed** woman of al-Yamāma, of the **Jadīs** tribe — [Zarqa al Yamama, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarqa_al_Yamama)
- ⚠️ **Range discrepancy:** Wikipedia says she could see riders **from a distance of one week**, not the three days in the user's brief. The report should use "a week" or state the variation.
- **The camouflage trick:** the enemy **hid behind trees they carried** toward her tribe; **Zarqāʾ spotted it and warned that the trees were moving, and her people thought she was going mad and ignored her** — [Zarqa al Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarqa_al_Yamama)
- **Invader: Ḥassān al-Ḥimyarī, identified as Ḥassān ibn Tubbaʿ Asʿad Abī Karib** — [Zarqa al Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarqa_al_Yamama)
- **Her fate: the invaders killed every man in the camp, tore out her eyes, and crucified her** — [Zarqa al Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarqa_al_Yamama)
- **Modern afterlife:** *Zarqāʾ al-Yamāma* was staged as **the first Saudi opera** — [Saudi Press Agency](https://www.spa.gov.sa/en/N2095764); [SPA on the production](https://www.spa.gov.sa/en/N2094353)

**Jurhum (جرهم)**
- Ruled **Mecca** in ancient times; **forced out in the 2nd century CE by Banū Khuzāʿa and Banū Bakr ibn ʿAbd Manāt**, who were angered that the Jurhum profited from the Kaʿba — [Extinct Arabs, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinct_Arabs), citing Mahrān, *Kitāb Dirāsāt fī tārīkh al-ʿArabiyya al-qadīm*, and Mubārakpūrī, *The Sealed Nectar*
- In the ʿAbqar roster (Q2), the jinn **al-Saffāḥ ibn al-Raqrāq** is the familiar of **al-Ḥārith ibn Muḍāḍ** — a name traditionally associated with the Jurhumī chiefs of Mecca, which neatly links the jinn-lore to the Jurhum legend — [وادي عبقر, Marefa](https://www.marefa.org/%D9%88%D8%A7%D8%AF%D9%8A_%D8%B9%D8%A8%D9%82%D8%B1) (the link between the two is **my inference**, not stated in the source)

**ʿAmāliq / Amalekites (العماليق) and the general category**
- Listed among the Extinct Arabs, but the source gives **no genealogy, no location, no account of how they perished**, and notes they are **not mentioned in the Qurʾān** — [Extinct Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinct_Arabs)
- The category is framed by **Q 9:70**, which names the peoples destroyed for rejecting their messengers; **Madyan (Midian)** is named there alongside ʿĀd and Thamūd — [Extinct Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinct_Arabs)

### Inferences
- **The Thamūd case is the intellectual centrepiece of this whole research brief.** A people the Qurʾān and the pre-Islamic poets both treat as annihilated in the deep past were in fact serving in Roman auxiliary units in the 4th century and dedicating a temple in 165–169 CE. For a VN set in the 5th–7th century, this means **the "ancient ruined civilisation" was itself a live belief of the period, held about people who had only recently stopped existing.** Characters can walk past Nabataean tombs at Hegra and call them the houses of a vanished race destroyed by God — and that is not the author's error, it is period-accurate.
- The recurring **ignored-woman-warner** motif (Zarqāʾ al-Yamāma; the unnamed woman of Jadīs; Ṭarīfa the *kāhina* in Q7; the Maʾrib king's wife) is the single most persistent narrative shape in this entire corpus. It is worth flagging to the user as a structural pattern, not four separate anecdotes.
- The ʿĀd/Iram material is **the weakest archaeologically and the strongest poetically** — "Iram of the tentpoles" rather than Iram of marble columns is both more likely philologically and more interesting: a nomad grandeur, not a Hollywood lost city.
- Note the **chronological incoherence** in the akhbār: Ṭasm and Jadīs are "contemporaries of ʿĀd and Thamūd" (deep past) yet destroyed in the 6th century CE, within living memory of the VN's setting. This is the characteristic flattening of legendary time and should be presented as such.

### Gaps
- **The tyrant's name**: Wikipedia gives **ʿImlīq**, and the article on Ṭasm and Jadīs **does not mention Jaww (جوّ) at all** — so the user's "tyrant of Jaww" framing is **unverified in my sources**. (Jaww is an old name for the al-Yamāma region / Ḥajr area; see [Hajr, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hajr) which surfaced in search but which I did not fetch.)
- **The Ṭasm/Jadīs and Zarqāʾ al-Yamāma stories are told on separate Wikipedia pages that do not cross-reference each other**, and the Ṭasm/Jadīs page **does not mention Zarqāʾ** while the Zarqāʾ page **does** make her a Jadīsī. The traditional sources evidently join them; **I could not verify the join from a scholarly source.**
- The Zarqāʾ page's own sourcing is weak: **Doris Jedamski, *Chewing Over the West* (2009), p. 136**, and an **Oman Observer** feature of 28 Aug 2020 — [Oman Observer](https://www.omanobserver.om/article/11056/Opinion/the-story-of-blue-eyed-woman-of-yamama). Neither is a specialist source on pre-Islamic Arabia. **The proverb associated with her, and any pre-Islamic poetic attestation, are unverified** — Wikipedia explicitly says it does not address either.
- **Shaddād ibn ʿĀd (شداد بن عاد)** — **I found no source for him.** The Iram article explicitly states it "does not discuss Shaddād ibn ʿĀd." He appears to be a figure of the later romance tradition (he is the builder of Iram in the *Nights*-type material) but **I cannot source that either.** The report should mark him as unverified and probably late.
- **ʿAmāliq, Umaym, Abīl, Jāsim, Wabār** as distinct peoples: essentially no information. The Extinct Arabs article does not cover Umaym, Abīl, Jāsim or Wabār at all. The EI and al-Masʿūdī/Ibn Khaldūn would be needed.

## Key Question 7: Legendary kings and figures — Luqmān, Sayf ibn Dhī Yazan, Bilqīs, the Maʾrib dam, the Azd dispersal

### Takeaway
This group splits cleanly. **Sayf ibn Dhī Yazan and the Maʾrib dam are documented history with legend attached** — the dam's breaches are dated by inscriptions, and Sayf's Sasanian intervention is a real 6th-century event. **Bilqīs, Luqmān and Dhū al-Qarnayn are Qurʾānic or para-Qurʾānic figures whose colourful details are all exegetical.** And **Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī is a real man (d. 578 CE) buried under a mountain of later romance.** The jinn-and-magic versions of all of them are medieval.

### Cited Findings

**The Maʾrib dam (سد مأرب) — inscriptions vs. legend**
- Construction: the infobox gives **1750–1700 BCE** but the text calls this only "tentative"; **the most reliable information dates the Great Dam to about the 8th century BCE**. It was about **4 m high at first, raised to 7 m around 500 BCE, and reached 14 m under Ḥimyarite rule** — [Marib Dam, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam)
- **Inscriptional record** (this is the hard evidence): earliest inscription attributed to **Yathaʿ Amar Watar I (r. 760–740 BCE)**; repairs under **Yadaʿ Il Bayin II (740–720 BCE)** and **Dhamar Il Zarīh I with Karab Il Bayin (700–680 BCE)**; **Samahʿ Il Yanūf II** carved his name on parts of it; **Sharhabīl Yaʿfar repaired it in 450 CE** and **Abraha in 543 CE**, the latter inscription **first published by Eduard Glaser in 1897**. **Major breaches are recorded for 449, 450, 542 and 548 CE, and the last recorded repairs were in 557 CE** — [Marib Dam, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam)
- The dam was **overtopped in 570 or 575 CE and left unrepaired** (source for the date not given) — [Marib Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam)
- Ancient South Arabian sources report a major breach **around 145 BCE** during the Raydān–Sabaʾ war; **scholars disagree whether this caused the "Flood of ʿArim"** — [Marib Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam)
- **Al-Iṣfahānī dates a breach to about 400 years before Islam; Yāqūt assigns one to Abyssinian rule** — i.e. the Arabic historians disagree — [Marib Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam)
- **Qurʾānic reference: Sūrat Sabaʾ 34:15–17**, alluding to the destruction and describing **"the flood of the dam" (سيل العرم, sayl al-ʿarim)** sent upon the people of Sabaʾ — [Marib Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam)
- **The legend, as Wikipedia reports it:** locals say **a soothsayer king, ʿImrān, predicted the dam's final breach, and later the king's wife did too**; and **rats gnawed at the dam with their teeth and scratched it with their nails** — [Marib Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam)
- ⚠️ The claim that the failure caused **up to 50,000 people to migrate** is on the page but **marked "citation needed"** — do not report it as fact — [Marib Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam)

**ʿAmr Muzayqiyāʾ (عمرو مزيقياء) and the Azd dispersal**
- His real name was **ʿAmr**; son of the patriarch **ʿĀmir Māʾ al-Samāʾ**; he succeeded his brother **ʿImrān** in leading **Kahlān**; his **reign is dated to the 2nd century CE** — [Muzayqiya, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzayqiya)
- Early years prosperous, especially for agriculture around the Maʾrib dam; **in his final years the dam was neglected and then collapsed, and several Qaḥṭānite tribes left Yemen** for other parts of Arabia or the Levant. He emigrated with family to the land of the **ʿAkk** tribe (now the Tihāma), where he fell ill and died — [Muzayqiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzayqiya)
- **The branches:** from his son **Jafna** → the **Ghassānids** (Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih says they were named for an abundant water source near where they settled); from **Thaʿlaba** → the **Aws and Khazraj** (i.e. the Medinan Anṣār); from **Ḥāritha** → the **Banū Khuzāʿa and Bāriq** — [Muzayqiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzayqiya)
- Sources the article cites: **al-Zirikli *al-Aʿlām*; Ibn Qutayba *Kitāb al-Maʿārif*; Ibn al-Kalbī *Kitāb al-Nisab*; Brian Ulrich *Arabs in the Early Islamic Empire* (2019) and *Constructing al-Azd* (2008); Iskandar Abkārius *Nihāyat al-Arab* (1852); Ibn Kathīr "The Story of Sabaʾ"; DASI inscription ZI 75; Norbert Nebes (2004) on a new Abraha inscription from the Great Dam of Maʾrib** — [Muzayqiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzayqiya)

**Sayf ibn Dhī Yazan (سيف بن ذي يزن) — history, then epic**
- **History:** a semi-legendary Ḥimyarite king of the 6th century CE, **born around 516 CE in Ṣanʿāʾ** to the **Dhū Yazan** family, a ruling family that had **converted to Judaism**. At his birth Yemen was ruled by **Maʿdīkarib Yaʿfur**, an Aksumite client king. **Abraha later exiled Sayf's father to marry Rayḥāna bint Dhī Jadan, producing Sayf's half-brother Masrūq ibn Abraha** — [Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayf_ibn_Dhi_Yazan)
- **The Byzantines refused to help because they shared a religion with Masrūq.** Sayf then went to the Lakhmid king **al-Nuʿmān III** and reached the Sasanian court of **Khosrow I**, who agreed on condition that **Yemen become a Sasanian vassal**. General **Wahrīz** sailed with him — **at least eight ships, two said to have sunk**. Masrūq was killed in battle and Sayf installed as a vassal king owing yearly tribute — [Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayf_ibn_Dhi_Yazan)
- **He was stabbed to death by one of his Abyssinian servants between 575 and 578 CE**; Wahrīz's troops then conquered Yemen, beginning Sasanian Yemen — [Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayf_ibn_Dhi_Yazan)
- **A chronological problem flagged on the page:** al-Nuʿmān III's reign is dated c. **580–583**, *after* Sayf's death and after Khosrow's, which sits awkwardly with his role as intermediary — [Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayf_ibn_Dhi_Yazan)
- **The epic, explicitly later:** ***Sīrat Sayf ibn Dhī Yazan*** is "extremely legendary," describing **extraordinary conquests including a journey into the realm of the jinn**, blending history and fiction. It **portrays the Aksumites as followers of South Arabian polytheism, whereas they were in fact Christians** — a clear marker of its distance from the events. **Wikipedia does not date its composition.** It inspired a king in Malaysian literature (Yusuf Dzu Yazin), and a 1982 Jordanian TV series — [Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayf_ibn_Dhi_Yazan)

**Bilqīs (بلقيس), the Queen of Sheba**
- **The Qurʾān never names her.** In **Sūrat al-Naml 27:22–44** she is "a woman ruling them." **A hoopoe (الهدهد) reports her rule to Solomon**, who sends a letter urging submission; she sends gifts, which he refuses; she travels to his palace — [Queen of Sheba, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_of_Sheba)
- **The throne:** Solomon asks who can bring her throne; **an ʿifrīt offers to move it before Solomon rises, but a man with knowledge of Scripture moves it almost instantly**. Solomon disguises the throne to test whether she recognises it — [Queen of Sheba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_of_Sheba)
- **The glass floor:** she mistakes it for water and lifts her clothing, **"uncovered her shins"**; Solomon tells her it is smooth glass; she acknowledges having wronged herself and submits — [Queen of Sheba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_of_Sheba)
- **The name and the jinn-parentage are exegetical, not Qurʾānic: al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī and al-Bayḍāwī expanded the story and gave her the name Bilqīs**; the name **may derive from a Greek or Hebrew word meaning "concubine."** Accounts differ on her fate: some say Solomon married her, others that he gave her to a king of **Hamdān**. **Al-Hamdānī names her father as a Sabaean king; another tale makes her the daughter of a jinnī (or *perī*) and a human** — [Queen of Sheba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_of_Sheba)
- Elsewhere she is described as **a jinn-hybrid linked to Solomon**, in later Islamic tradition — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- **The historical record:** **no inscription names the queen.** South Arabian inscriptions mention a queen (*mlkt*) — **though the same article also says southern inscriptions do not mention female rulers, so its sources conflict on this point and the report should say so.** Assyrian records mention **Arab queens in northern Arabia**. The **Awwām temple, called Maḥram Bilqīs in folklore, was excavated, and no evidence linking it to the queen was found** — [Queen of Sheba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_of_Sheba)

**Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī (حاتم الطائي)**
- **Died 578 CE**, buried at **Tuwārin, Ḥāʾil**; lived in the last half of the 6th century and the beginning of the 7th. A chieftain of **Ṭayyiʾ**, knight and poet; associated with the **Lakhmid court at Ḥīra**, especially under **al-Mundhir III ibn al-Nuʿmān** — [Hatim al-Tai, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)
- **The proverb: *akram min Ḥātim* (أكرم من حاتم), "more generous than Ḥātim,"** still in use. **Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih named him, Kaʿb ibn Māma and Harim ibn Sinān al-Murrī as the three most generous people of the pre-Islamic era.** It is said he gave away everything but his mount and weapons — [Hatim al-Tai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)
- **Kitāb al-Aghānī** is cited for the 578 death date. **Saʿdī praised him in the *Bustān* (1257) and *Gulistān* (1259)** — [Hatim al-Tai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)
- **The later romance:** the ***Arabian Nights*** include his story and describe his tomb (the collection "took its modern form around 800–900 CE"). The Persian ***Qiṣṣa-e Ḥātem Ṭāʾī*** is the most popular version in Persia and South Asia, built on **seven riddles posed by Ḥusn Bānū** which Ḥātim must answer to help a king win her. Films in 1956, 1971, 1990 and TV adaptations — [Hatim al-Tai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)
- **Historical vs legendary, as the source sorts it:** better attested — his 578 death, burial site, Lakhmid connection, Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih's notice, Saʿdī's praise. Legendary/uncertain — the seven-riddle quest, the *Nights*' tomb description, D'Herbelot's tomb location (Anwarz), and his supposed Middle East–South Asia fame (marked "citation needed") — [Hatim al-Tai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)
- His name **is said to mean "black crow"** — a linguistic claim, not a historical one — [Hatim al-Tai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)

**ʿAntara and Sīrat ʿAntar — the later epic**
- ***Sīrat ʿAntar* (The Romance of ʿAntar)** was **"long handed down by oral tradition only"** before publication; it **"has grown to immense proportions,"** published in **32 volumes at Cairo (1889)** and **10 volumes at Beirut (1871)**. Wikipedia calls it a "long and extravagant romance" but **does not describe its supernatural content and does not give a composition date**, though it mentions a 13th-century epic ʿAntar poem. The story of ʿAntar and ʿAbla was traditionally credited to **al-Aṣmaʿī**, a poet at Hārūn al-Rashīd's court — [Antarah ibn Shaddad, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)

### Inferences
- **The Maʾrib dam is the best example in this brief of how legend and record interlock.** The inscriptions give **breaches in 449, 450, 542 and 548 and last repairs in 557** — a visibly failing system across the exact century the VN covers. The rats and the soothsayer-king are the *explanation* a 6th-century person would reach for; the real cause was the end of the maintenance capacity of a collapsing polity. A VN can stage both at once with complete honesty.
- Note the chronological impossibility in the Azd legend: **ʿAmr Muzayqiyāʾ is dated to the 2nd century CE** but the dam collapse that supposedly scatters his sons' descendants is a 6th-century event — and his son's line produces the Ghassānids and the Medinan Aws and Khazraj, who are 6th–7th century. The genealogy is **doing etiological work**, explaining why Yemeni-origin tribes are in Syria, Mecca and Medina. Present it as that.
- **The *Sīrat Sayf* is the perfect case study for the user's central question.** The historical Sayf (d. 575–578) is inside the VN's window; the epic in which he journeys to the land of the jinn is centuries later and gets basic facts wrong (Christian Aksumites rendered as polytheists). It is the clean line between period-authentic and accretion.
- **Bilqīs's jinn mother is exegetical, and the hairy-legs version is not even in Wikipedia** — so the familiar "she had goat's legs / hairy legs, hence the glass floor" story is a *later folk elaboration on a later exegetical elaboration*. Two steps removed from the Qurʾān, which says only that she bared her shins.

### Gaps
- **Ṭarīfa / Turayfa the kāhina (طريفة الكاهنة):** the Muzayqiya article **does not mention her, or any prophecy, or a rat or mouse, or the meaning of the name "Muzayqiyāʾ"**. However, **Savran independently places her**: Saṭīḥ and Shiqq "appear as youths around the flood of the Maʾrib Dam and **were given soothsaying by the *kāhina* Turayfa, who predicted the dam's collapse**" — [Savran](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition). **So Ṭarīfa/Turayfa IS attested as the dam-prophetess and as the source of Saṭīḥ and Shiqq's gift — but I could not confirm her relation to ʿAmr Muzayqiyāʾ (sister? wife?).** This is a high-value loose end.
- **Al-jurdh (الجرذ):** Wikipedia says only "large rats," without the Arabic term. The specific word, and whether the legend involves **one** rat or many, is unverified.
- **Luqmān (لقمان) and the seven vultures (لبد, lubad): I could not retrieve a usable source.** The 1911 *Britannica* Wikisource page returned URL-not-found. Two promising leads were surfaced but not fetched: an open-access article, **"Doomed Power and Eternal Wisdom in Late Antiquity: Intertwining Representations of Luqmān in Light of the Qurʾānic Tradition," *Religions* 16:10 (2025), art. 1301 — [doi.org/10.3390/rel16101301](https://doi.org/10.3390/rel16101301)**; and **Kevin Blankinship, "Labīd, ʿAbīd, and Lubad: Lexical Excavation and the Reclamation of the Poetic Past in al-Maʿarrī's *Luzūmiyyāt*," ch. 6 of *The Cooing of the Dove and the Cawing of the Crow* (Brill) — [Brill page](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004499287/BP000009.xml?language=en); [academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/44502269/Lab%C4%ABd_%CA%BFAb%C4%ABd_and_Lubad_Lexical_Excavation_and_the_Reclamation_of_the_Poetic_Past_in_al_Ma%CA%BFarr%C4%ABs_Luz%C5%ABmiyy%C4%81t). The Blankinship chapter title confirms **Lubad is a real item of poetic vocabulary treated by al-Maʿarrī**, which supports the user's premise; but **I report no details of the seven-vultures story, because I verified none.** Also noted: a popular treatment at [Saudi Aramco World, "Aesop of the Arabs" (1974)](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/197402/aesop.of.the.arabs.htm).

The lead worth following is **Ṣaʿb Dhū Marāthid (صعب ذو مراثد)**, the **South Arabian / Ḥimyarite identification of Dhū al-Qarnayn** associated with Wahb ibn Munabbih and the *Kitāb al-Tījān* — [Wikipedia article exists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%27b_Dhu_Marathid) but returned URL-not-found on fetch. **This is a significant gap**, because a *Yemeni* Dhū al-Qarnayn is far more useful to an Arabian-set VN than the Alexander identification, and the user should know the tradition exists.
- **Sulaymān's command of the jinn specifically in *Arabian* lore** (as distinct from Qurʾānic and later Islamic magic literature): only the ʿifrīt-and-throne episode (Q 27) and the general note that "later folklore expands on this" — [Islam and magic, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_magic). **Q 2:102 links *siḥr* to Hārūt and Mārūt and to Solomon's time** — same source.

## Key Question 8: Soothsaying and prophecy — the kāhin, Saṭīḥ and Shiqq, dreams and omens

### Takeaway
The *kāhin* institution is securely pre-Islamic and its mechanism is clearly described: a jinn-familiar eavesdrops at heaven, and the *kāhin* delivers what it brings **in rhymed prose (*sajʿ*) or metre**. But **Saṭīḥ and Shiqq themselves are now read by specialists as early-ʿAbbāsid literary constructions**, not as remembered historical seers — which the user needs to know before building on them.

### Cited Findings

**The institution**
- Pre-Islamic Arabs had **seers** thought to have a special relationship with a jinnī, **sometimes said to be the offspring of a human–jinnī marriage**. In exchange for a sacrifice or a demand, the jinnī gave information gathered **by approaching Heaven and "spying."** The seer delivered it **in metered poetry** — [Mohamed](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA), citing El-Zein 2009
- **Sajʿ (سجع, rhymed prose) was a means for jinn to speak through soothsayers** — [Savran](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition)
- **Soothsayers (*kāhin*) were believed to be controlled by jinn that revealed hidden knowledge** — [Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)
- **Early *shuʿarāʾ* and *kuhhān* were not clearly distinguished** — [Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia)
- **The mechanism is shut down in the Qurʾān:** 72:8–9 — jinn and devils **once spied at heaven's gates to get news for soothsayers, but are now driven off by meteors**; and per Q 72:6–7 the seers "increased them in burden" — [Al-Jinn, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Jinn); [Mohamed](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA)
- **Two seer-legends with striking physical descriptions** (El-Zein 2009, p. 56): one seer was **a half-person with one leg, one arm and one eye**; another's **body rolled up like a mat when he received communication from his jinnī, and his only bone was his skull, with his face on his chest** — [Mohamed](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA)

**Saṭīḥ (سطيح) and Shiqq (شقّ)**
Principal source: **Scott Savran, "The Two Old Soothsayers in the Islamic Historiographical Tradition"** (10 pp.; the academia.edu page gives no journal or year) — [academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition)

- **Their bodies, and their names:** **Saṭīḥ had almost no bones and was compared to a doormat, which his companions folded up when travelling with him. Shiqq was a Cyclops with half a face, half a head and half a body. Their names allude to these deformities** (*saṭīḥ* ≈ "spread flat," *shiqq* ≈ "half") — [Savran](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition)
- **Origin:** they appear as youths **around the flood of the Maʾrib dam** and **were given soothsaying by the *kāhina* Turayfa, who predicted the dam's collapse.** ⚠️ **Savran does not describe their births and does not give them jinn parentage** — so the popular "Shiqq the half-jinn, born to a jinn mother" framing is **not supported by the one specialist source I reached.** (Compare the popular claim at [Ali A. Olomi on X](https://x.com/aaolomi/status/1405215323289161730), attributing the jinn-mother version to Ibn ʿAbbās — **unverified, social media, do not rely on it.**)
- **The dream of Rabīʿa ibn Naṣr (ربيعة بن نصر):** Rabīʿa, chieftain and **progenitor of the Lakhmids**, consulted them about an ominous dream. They predicted **the Abyssinian conquest of Yemen, its reconquest by a native Yemeni dynasty, and the rise of the Prophet and Islam.** Rabīʿa then **sent his sons and family to Iraq, who founded the Lakhmid kingdom** — [Savran](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition)
- **Other prophecies:** they told **ʿAbd al-Manāf** that a prophet would arise from his progeny; **Saṭīḥ described the mark of prophecy between the Prophet's shoulders**; **Shiqq outlined the caliphates after the Prophet's death** — [Savran](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition)
- **Saṭīḥ's deathbed prophecy — the great set-piece.** On the night Muhammad was born, in **Khusraw I Anūshirvān's** reign: **an earthquake in the throne room toppled pillars, the Iṣṭakhr sacred fire went out, Lake Sāwa sank, and the chief Zoroastrian priest dreamed of camels leading Arabian horses across the Tigris.** Khusraw sent for an Arab sage; the Lakhmid king sent the Christian **ʿAbd al-Masīḥ**, who travelled to Syria to consult Saṭīḥ. Saṭīḥ, **near death (Shiqq already dead)**, said an Arab prophet would arise, that **Persian rule would pass to the Arabs**, and that **the number of fallen pillars equalled the number of kings who would rule before then. He died on the spot.** — [Savran](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition)
- **Transmission:** the Rabīʿa dream is in **Ibn Isḥāq via Ibn Hishām's *Sīra* (I, 31–35)** and in **al-Ṭabarī (I, 910–914)**; the Sasanian deathbed report is **al-Ṭabarī (I, 981–984)** with **a spurious isnād tracing to Hāniʾ al-Makhzūmī**. **Al-Masʿūdī (II, 380–383)** gives Shiqq's ancestry and the Maʾrib passage. **Al-Masʿūdī and Ibn Durayd date the Sasanian events to Khusraw II Parvīz instead** — a direct source conflict. Others: ***Nihāyat al-irab*** (anonymous, for the ʿAbd al-Manāf report), **al-Yaʿqūbī** (ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib arbitration; **pillar count of thirteen**), **Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih, Ibn Durayd, *Kitāb al-Tījān*, al-Qazwīnī, al-Dīnawarī, Yāqūt** — [Savran](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition)
- **Savran's verdict, which the user must hear:** these are **Islamic-era historiographical memory**, not pre-Islamic report. In the early ʿAbbāsid period *akhbārīs* used Saṭīḥ and Shiqq as **"connectors"** linking ancient Arabian figures, the rise of Islam and Sasanian history into a **universal master narrative**, and as **legitimizing agents** presenting Jāhiliyya Arabs as carrying **a hidden monotheist current**. He cites **Uri Rubin** on similar projections in Medinan traditions and **Drory and Webb** on ʿAbbāsid constructions of the Jāhiliyya. He presents these as **later, ideologically motivated constructions**, while noting the figures' ancient narrative setting — [Savran](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition)

**Omen-taking and divination**
- **Ṭiyara (طِيَرة), bird/evil omens — denied in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2220**, which proves it was a live practice. The sunnah.com note observes that **the majority of scholars read these beliefs as baseless superstition** and that such things do not cause harm of themselves — [Sahih Muslim 2220a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2220a)
- **Arrow divination:** **Hubal's (هبل) rituals involved throwing divination arrows before his image in the Kaʿba, for questions of virginity, death and marriage** — [List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)
- **ʿIlm al-faʾl (علم الفأل, omens)** is described as an **Islamic-period** practice of reading visible random events to uncover hidden realities; **ʿilm al-raml (علم الرمل)**, geomancy, "the science of the sand," interprets figures traced in sand — [Divination, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination)
- **Al-Ghazālī (d. 1111)** objected to divination because he saw it as **akin to pagan invocation of spirits other than God** — [Divination, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination)

### Inferences
- **The jinn/kāhin/meteor complex gives the VN a dateable supernatural "event."** If a character's grandfather's generation had working oracles and this generation's oracles have gone silent because the sky is now guarded, that is a change the Qurʾān itself announces, and it is dramatically enormous: a world in which divination *used to work*.
- The physical deformity of the great soothsayers is a consistent motif — **Saṭīḥ boneless, Shiqq halved, El-Zein's seers one-limbed or face-on-chest** — and it rhymes exactly with the *shiqq*/*nasnās* monster (Q3). **The seer and the half-being are the same image.** The one who sees the unseen is himself not quite a whole man. That is a genuinely striking, well-sourced motif and I'd put it near the top of the report.
- Savran's reading has a sharp implication for authenticity: **using Saṭīḥ and Shiqq as 5th–6th-century characters reproduces an ʿAbbāsid ideological construction, not a 6th-century reality.** But that need not stop the user — it just means they are writing a *tradition* rather than a *fact*, and the VN could even make that its subject.
- The Khusraw portent-cluster (quake, extinguished fire, sunken lake, priest's dream) is a Sasanian-style omen-list grafted onto an Arabian prophecy. It is late and composite, and spectacular.

### Gaps
- **The *ḥāzī* (حازي): I found no source at all.** Unverified; do not report.
- **Zajr (زجر)** as a distinct technique of bird-omen reading: not attested in any source I reached. Only *ṭiyara* is.
- **Istiqsām bi'l-azlām (استقسام بالأزلام) and the Qurʾānic prohibition at Q 5:90:** the Wikipedia Divination article **explicitly contains nothing** on Arabian belomancy or Q 5:90, and the deities list mentions arrow-divination only in passing under Hubal. **The specific Qurʾānic prohibition is unverified in my sources**, though the Hubal arrow ritual is attested. EI "Istiksām" / "Azlām" needed.
- **The *ʿarrāf* (عرّاف)** as distinct from the *kāhin*: not attested in any source reached.
- **Toufic Fahd, *La divination arabe*** — reachable only at one remove: the Islam-and-magic article cites Fahd for the view that the Qurʾān's account of *siḥr* as taught by fallen angels suggests it is **"a fragment of a celestial knowledge,"** and **not a separate party among the Jāhiliyya** — [Islam and magic, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_magic). That is the only Fahd content I can offer, and it is second-hand.
- **Prophetic dreams** as a general pre-Islamic institution (beyond Rabīʿa's dream): no source reached treats this systematically.
- **Ibn Khaldūn's *Muqaddima* chapter on soothsaying and supernatural perception** surfaced in search at [muslimphilosophy.com](http://www.muslimphilosophy.com/ik/Muqaddimah/Chapter3/Ch_3_52.htm) and [superphysics.org](https://www.superphysics.org/research/ibn-khaldun/muqaddimah/chapter-1/part-6h/) but I did not fetch it. It is a **14th-century** theoretical account, useful but very late.

## Key Question 9: Folk belief and magic — evil eye, amulets, ruqya, siḥr, contagion, star lore

### Takeaway
This is the **weakest-sourced area of the entire brief**, and the report should say so rather than pad it. The one genuinely solid finding is again **Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2220**, whose chapter heading preserves a list of five specific pre-Islamic beliefs — including **ʿadwā (contagion)** and **ṣafar** — in the act of rejecting them. Beyond that, the accessible English-language material on pre-Islamic Arabian magic is thin and dominated by Islamic-era normative sources that describe what *should* be done rather than what pre-Islamic Arabs *did*.

### Cited Findings

**The hadith that names the beliefs (the core evidence)**
- Chapter heading, *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, Book of Greetings (Book 39): **باب لا عدوى ولا طِيَرَةَ ولا هَامَةَ ولا صَفَرَ** — "There Is No **ʿAdwā**, No **Ṭiyara** (evil omens), No **Hāma**, No **Ṣafar**, No **Nawāʾ**, And No **Ghūl**, And No Sick Camel Should Be Brought To A Healthy Camel" — [Sahih Muslim 2220a, sunnah.com](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2220a); [chapter listing, sounah.com](https://sounah.com/en/bab/4960/)
- Hadith 2220a (Abū Hurayra), Arabic: **لا عدوى ولا صفر ولا هامة** — "There is no infection, no *ṣafar*, no *hāma*." **A desert Arab objects: why do camels in the sand look as healthy as deer until a scabbed camel mixes with them? The Prophet replies, "Who infected the first one?"** — [Sahih Muslim 2220a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2220a)
- **Only *ṭiyara* is glossed on the page** ("evil omens"); **ʿadwā, hāma, ṣafar, nawāʾ and ghūl are not defined there.** Further hadiths 2220b–d exist at sunnah.com but their text was not on the page I fetched — [Sahih Muslim 2220b](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2220b), [2220d](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2220d)

**Siḥr, ruqya, amulets, the evil eye**
- **The Qurʾān describes *siḥr* (سحر) negatively but does not forbid it or prescribe a punishment.** **Q 2:102 links it to Hārūt and Mārūt (هاروت وماروت) and to Solomon's time** — [Islam and magic, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_magic)
- Citing **Toufic Fahd**: the Qurʾān's account of *siḥr* as taught by fallen angels suggests it is **"a fragment of a celestial knowledge"** rather than the mark of a separate party among the Jāhiliyya — [Islam and magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_magic)
- **Sūrat al-Falaq (113)** — hadith literature says it was revealed **to protect Muhammad from the *Jānn***; both al-Falaq and **al-Nās (114)** are used for protection and healing — [Islam and magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_magic)
- **The knot charm:** hadiths describe Muhammad falling ill from **a charm hidden in a well**, with differing accounts of how it was countered — [Islam and magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_magic)
- **Ruqya (رقية)**: recitation-based healing, described in **later** Islamic sources and modern practice — [Islam and magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_magic)
- **Evil eye:** shielding against it is described as permissible healing, with **later** ritual bathing and incantations — [Islam and magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_magic)
- **"Until the ninth century, talismans often reflect pre-Islamic artistry,"** before pre-Islamic designs gave way to other symbols — the one concrete material-culture datum — [Islam and magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_magic)
- **Āyat al-Kursī (Q 2:255)** functions as the counter-charm against ghouls in the Abū Ayyūb hadith, and the **adhān** against a ghūl attack on lonely travellers — [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf); [Ghoul, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul)
- **Possession:** *majnūn* = "one possessed by a jinnī" (Ashqar 2005, pp. 203–212, cited in Mohamed); exorcism is treated as an Islamic-era jurisprudential topic — [Mohamed](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA)
- **The qarīn in later folk practice** (post-period but illustrative of the amulet logic): Egyptian Copts and Muslims believe **the qarīn can turn into a cat or dog at night**; **amulets guard against a jealous qarīn, and rituals often involve the number seven**; pregnant women once consulted a *shaykha* before birth, and **brides in Upper Egypt wore amulets**; the qarīn may persist after death for holy men; Russian and Turkish Muslims believe it is present in the womb; it is distinguished from the ***Qarīna*, a female childbed demon** — [Qareen, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qareen)
- **The qarīn's own dating:** the **spirit-companion concept is absent from the Qurʾān** — the word appears at **43:36, 37:51, 4:38, 50:23, 50:27** without necessarily meaning a spiritual being, and at 43:36 the "satan" is a **human** tempter (Winkler reads it as an earthly companion pulling a Muslim away from the community). **The spiritual sense comes from hadith**: al-Tirmidhī (via Ibn Masʿūd) contrasts a devil's whispers with an angel's; *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* states each person has a qarīn "from the jinn kind," and Muhammad's qarīn submitted to Islam (*Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* no. 2814; Q 43:36 cited). Ibn Ḥanbal and al-Ṭabarī describe both a devil and an angel; al-Suyūṭī is unsure; al-ʿUthaymīn identifies it as an evil jinn; some read it as an inner "other self." Possible older Near Eastern background: **the Egyptian *ka* and a Babylonian personal deity** — [Qareen, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qareen); [Mohamed](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA)

**Star lore and the anwāʾ**
- The relevant doctoral study exists: **"Rain Stars Set, Lunar Stations Rise: Multivalent Textures of Pre-Islamic Arabian Astronomy and the Hegemonic Discourse of Order" (PhD thesis, 2018)** — [Harvard ADS record](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018PhDT........54A/abstract) (abstract **not retrievable**, robots-disallowed)
- Other leads surfaced but not fetched: [Ithraeyat, "Star Lore of the Past"](https://ithraeyat.ithra.com/editions/light/star-lore-of-the-past); [Springer, "Lunar Mansions in Islam"](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-94-007-7747-7_9024); [Springer, "Stars in Arabic–Islamic Science"](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-94-007-3934-5_9060-2); [Culture & Cosmos vol. 21 (Ayash)](https://www.cultureandcosmos.org/pdfs/21/CCv21_15Ayash.pdf); [Muslim Heritage, "Stellar and Lunar Keys to Medieval Muslim Agriculture"](https://muslimheritage.com/stellar-lunar-keys-medieval-mus-agriculture/)

### Inferences
- **The Muslim 2220 list is the report's best single tool for the user's authenticity question**, and it should be presented as a table: *ʿadwā* (contagion passes by contact), *ṭiyara* (omens, esp. from birds), *hāma* (the death-bird of the unavenged), *ṣafar*, *nawāʾ* (apparently star-related; see Gaps), *ghūl*. Six beliefs, all certified live in the 7th century, all repudiated in one sentence. That sentence is simultaneously the evidence for the old world and the announcement of its end.
- The *nawāʾ* in that list is almost certainly connected to the **anwāʾ** (rain-stars) — the denial would then be of the belief that particular stars *cause* rain. **I could not confirm this** and flag it below, but if confirmed it would mean the Muslim 2220 list contains the anwāʾ belief too, which would be a very tidy result for the report.
- The persistent counter-charms in this material are **recitation** (Āyat al-Kursī, al-Falaq, al-Nās, the adhān) rather than objects — note that the normative Islamic sources *approve* recitation and *disapprove* amulets, which is itself a clue that **amulets were the pre-Islamic default** and recitation the replacement.

### Gaps
- **The ṣafar worm (الصفر):** **not defined in any source I reached.** sunnah.com's page does not gloss it. The traditional gloss (a worm in the belly, or a shifted month-taboo) is **unverified here** and should not be asserted.
- **Nawāʾ (نواء):** likewise undefined in the source. Its probable link to the anwāʾ is **my inference only**.
- **ʿAdwā:** attested as a *term* in the hadith and clearly concerns contagion (the camel exchange makes that plain), but **no source explains the pre-Islamic theory behind it.**
- **Tamīma (تميمة) and taʿwīdh (تعويذ)** as specific amulet-terms, **nafth (نفث, ritual spitting)**, and **knot-magic** as a named Arabian practice: the Islam-and-magic article **explicitly does not cover** these.
- **The belief that a snake embodies a jinnī** — a specific item in the user's brief — **I found no source for it.** The jinn/serpent association is well known in the literature but **unverified in anything I reached.** Adjacent attested material: the ghūl combining **snake**, goat and ass traits (al-Rawi).
- **Transformation / shape-shifting** as a general magical capacity: attested only for the ghūl (takes the form of whom it last ate) and for the later qarīn (cat or dog at night). No general Arabian doctrine of transformation was located.
- **The anwāʾ and the lunar mansions (منازل القمر, manāzil al-qamar):** **I obtained no substantive content at all.** The dissertation abstract was robots-blocked and I did not have budget to work through the Springer and Culture-&-Cosmos leads. **This is a real, flagged hole in the research and the report should say the anwāʾ remain unresearched here.** EI "Anwāʾ" and the 2018 dissertation are the two things to get.

## Key Question 10: The later literary afterlife — what the user should know is LATER

### Takeaway
Everything in this section postdates the VN's setting by **200 to 1,100 years**, and several of these works are the direct source of the "Arabian Nights" register the user wants to avoid. The useful move for the report is to give the user a **dated ladder**, so they can see exactly how far each familiar image is from the 5th–7th century.

### Inferences
- **The most useful finding for the user in this whole section is that the two most "Arabian Nights" images in the popular imagination are provably European narrowings**: the ghoul-as-graveyard-corpse-eater is **Galland's**, and "genie" is an 18th-century French-English word. Neither is Arabian. Dropping them is not a loss of authenticity; it is a gain.
- **Al-Jāḥiẓ is the most valuable single later author** for this project, because he reports the folk material *and* argues against it, which gives the user both the belief and a period-appropriate way to doubt it. Note he is 9th century, so the doubt is not anachronistic for a story — but it is anachronistic for a 6th-century *character* to voice in his systematic form.
- **Nothing in this table should be read as evidence of what 6th-century Arabians believed** — only as evidence of what later Arab writers said they believed. The report should state that boundary explicitly, since it is precisely what the user asked for.
