# Poets and Poet-Heroes of Pre-Islamic Arabia (Jāhiliyya): Dossier, Chronology, Reliability

Scope note for the report-writer: this file covers named figures, dates, places, tribes, courts, and the attested/akhbār/legend distinction, plus editions and free online texts. **A general caution that applies to every entry below:** virtually all biographical detail for these poets comes from Abbasid-era compilations (2nd–4th centuries AH) — Ibn Sallām al-Jumaḥī's *Ṭabaqāt Fuḥūl al-Shuʿarāʾ*, Ibn Qutayba's *al-Shiʿr wa-l-Shuʿarāʾ*, and above all Abū al-Faraj al-Iṣfahānī's *Kitāb al-Aghānī* — written 200–400 years after the events. Dates in CE are almost always modern scholarly reconstructions, not transmitted dates, and should be treated as conjectural unless flagged otherwise.

## Imruʾ al-Qays (امرؤ القيس): dates, descent, vengeance quest, wanderings, Justinian, the poisoned robe

### Takeaway
Imruʾ al-Qays is securely attested as a mid-6th-century Kindite prince-poet whose father Ḥujr was killed by the Banū Asad; the vengeance quest, the Byzantine embassy, and the poisoned-robe death at Ankara form a narrative arc of decreasing reliability, with the poisoned robe being clearly legendary. Two rival dating schemes circulate (c. 496/501–544/565 vs. c. 526–565), and the sources do not resolve them.

### Cited Findings
- Full name given as Imruʾ al-Qays Ḥundūj (Junduḥ) ibn Ḥujr al-Kindī — Arabic: امْرُؤ ٱلْقَيْس حُنْدُج ٱبْن حَجْر ٱلْكِنْدِيّ — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **Competing birth datings:** one estimate c. 496 CE, another c. 526 CE; the source states explicitly that "historians are divided" — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **Death:** commonly given as 565 CE at Ankara, aged 63–64 on the early-birth reckoning; the embassy to Justinian and the Anatolian death are placed in the window 561–565 CE — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- Britannica places his floruit simply in the 6th century and treats him as the first great Arabic poet — [Britannica: Imruʾ al-Qays](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Imru-al-Qays-Arab-poet)
- **Father:** Ḥujr ibn al-Ḥārith (حجر ابن الحارث), Kindite regent over the Asad and Ghaṭafān; killed in the Asad revolt, probably c. 525 CE — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **Mother:** Fāṭima bint Rabīʿa al-Taghlibī (فاطمة بنت ربيعة التغلبي) — i.e. a Taghlib connection, the tribe of the Basūs war — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **Kindite line:** Ḥujr → al-Ḥārith → ʿAmr → Ḥujr Ākil al-Murār ("eater of bitter herbs," first king of Kinda) → Muʿāwiya → Thawr → ʿUfayr. Kinda originated in South Arabia and migrated north to Najd in the 4th–5th century CE — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **Grandfather al-Ḥārith:** converted to Mazdakism under Kavadh I, lost al-Ḥīra to al-Mundhir when Khosrow I restored the Lakhmids; reportedly captured and killed with two sons and 40+ kinsmen — the source flags this as "not certain" — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- Ḥujr's death is linked to the collapse of Kinda's sponsor after the **Axumite occupation of Yemen in 525 CE** — a useful hard anchor for the chronology — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **The vengeance quest:** Imruʾ al-Qays was the only son to pursue revenge. Asad offered three options — kill a noble equal in rank to his father, accept blood-money in sheep and camels, or war. He chose war. **Bakr and Taghlib joined him against Asad, then withdrew** once they judged the revenge sufficient, leaving him without allies — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- A Ḥimyarite ruler, **Marthadʾilān Yuʿnim, is said to have sent at least 500 soldiers** to help Imruʾ al-Qays reunite Taghlib and Bakr against Banū Asad — [Wikipedia: Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- **Epithet:** الملك الضليل, "the Lost/Errant King," because he never regained his father's throne — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **Wanderings:** after the revenge he travelled "across the Arabian peninsula and the Levant" seeking refuge and support. **No detailed itinerary is given in the accessible sources** — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **al-Samawʾal at al-Ablaq (Taymāʾ):** pursued and abandoned by his followers, Imruʾ al-Qays took refuge with al-Samawʾal ibn ʿĀdiyāʾ at the fortress of al-Ablaq near Taymāʾ, and left with him three deposits — his **daughter Hind, his cousin, and his armour** — when he departed for Constantinople. He never reclaimed them — [Wikipedia: Samaw'al ibn 'Adiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaw%27al_ibn_%27Adiya)
- **al-Ḥārith (ibn Abī Shamir), the Ghassanid:** the accessible source names al-Ḥārith ibn Abī Shamir specifically as **the Ghassanid prince to whom Imruʾ al-Qays carried a letter of introduction**; the Ghassanid prince al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala sponsored his appeal to Justinian I — [Wikipedia: Samaw'al ibn 'Adiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaw%27al_ibn_%27Adiya); [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **Byzantine embassy:** he reportedly received promises of support from Justinian, perhaps some troops. Justinian is said to have asked the Negus of Axum to back him; the Negus refused because of the Axumite feud with Kinda — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **Death:** he fell ill near Ankara and died there. **Legend:** Justinian, angered by an alleged affair at court, sent a poisoned jacket/robe. **Scholarly view: most historians favour death from a chronic skin disease, which he himself mentions in one of his poems** — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- A later claim says the Greeks erected a statue at his tomb, reportedly still visible in 1262 CE; the tomb is traditionally located at Hıdırlık, Ankara — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- The poisoned-robe motif is an international folklore type (the "poison dress"), not specific to this story — [Wikipedia: Poison dress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poison_dress)
- There is a dedicated scholarly article on the Greek/Byzantine death narrative: "The Greek Death of Imruʾ al-Qays" — [The Free Library listing](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/The+Greek+Death+of+Imru'+al-Qays.-a0630409908)
- **Items explicitly flagged as legend or uncertain** in the sources: his being the youngest son; composing poetry as a child; banishment by his father for drink and women (incl. the camel-herd episode and the affair with ʿUnayza); the famous "today drink, tomorrow serious matters" line (traced to Ibn al-Kalbī, d. 826); the poisoned jacket; whether he was present in his father's army at the Asad revolt; Mazdakite influence on him — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **Items with more support:** early poetic training under possible teachers Zuhayr ibn Janāb al-Kalbī, Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī, and ʿAmr ibn Qamīʾa; Ibn Sallām al-Jumaḥī's praise (d. 846) in *Ṭabaqāt Fuḥūl al-Shuʿarāʾ* (طبقات فحول الشعراء) for his innovations in imagery; his poems' references to Sasanian and Byzantine events — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **Religion contested:** traditionally pagan; Louis Shaykhū (c. 1898) argued for Christianity on the basis of Christian imagery and the word *Allāh*; others read these as frontier contact or as pagan high-god usage. al-Ṭāhir Aḥmad Makkī suggests he may have been a *ḥanīf* — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)
- **A separate "Imruʾ al-Qays" exists epigraphically**: the Namāra inscription (328 CE) names an Imruʾ al-Qays "king of all the Arabs" — this is a different, much earlier figure and is a genuine documentary inscription, not to be conflated with the poet — [Wikipedia: Namara inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namara_inscription)
- Key modern reference work on him: al-Ṭāhir Aḥmad Makkī, "Imruʾ al-Qays," *Dictionary of Literary Biography* 311 (2005), pp. 212–222; also Mazhar al-Samarrāʾī, *Imruʾ al-Qays: Poet and Lover* (Amman, 1993) — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)

### Inferences
- The two dating schemes are not independent: the c. 496–565 scheme requires a man in his late 60s making a diplomatic journey to Constantinople, while the c. 526–565 scheme makes him roughly the right age (late 30s) but then requires him to be an infant when his father died c. 525. **The c. 526 birth is hard to reconcile with an adult vengeance quest for a father killed in 525**; the report-writer should present this as an unresolved internal tension rather than pick a side.
- For a visual novel, the most dramatically usable *and* defensible version is: father killed c. 525, vengeance war in the 530s–540s with Bakr/Taghlib allies who then desert him, refuge at al-Ablaq with al-Samawʾal, journey north through Ghassanid territory with a letter of introduction, Constantinople c. 561–564, death near Ankara c. 565 of illness. The poisoned robe can be used as *in-world rumour* rather than fact.
- The Axumite conquest of Yemen (525) and Justinian's reign (527–565) are the only two externally datable anchors in his biography; everything between them is reconstructed from the poems and the akhbār.

### Gaps
- **No documented route of his wanderings** exists in the accessible sources. The sequence (Najd → Taymāʾ → Syria/Ghassanid territory → Constantinople → Ankara) is reconstructed from scattered episodes, not a transmitted itinerary. Named stopping-places beyond al-Ablaq/Taymāʾ were not found.
- **al-Ḥārith ibn Shamir vs. al-Ḥārith ibn Ẓālim:** the identity of the besieger of al-Ablaq who killed al-Samawʾal's son is confused in the sources. The accessible source names the Ghassanid introduction-letter recipient as al-Ḥārith ibn Abī Shamir but explicitly states it **does not** identify the besieger with him, and does not mention al-Ḥārith ibn Ẓālim at all. This is a genuine source confusion, not merely a gap in my research.
- **No dealings with the Banū Ṭayyiʾ were found** in any accessible source for Imruʾ al-Qays. If the user has a specific report of this, it likely comes from *al-Aghānī* and needs checking against the Arabic text directly.
- EI2/EI3 entries (Brill, paywalled) could not be consulted; they would give the authoritative dating discussion.

## ʿAntara ibn Shaddād (عنترة بن شداد): dates, ʿAbs, Zabība, ʿAbla, Dāḥis, and the epic

### Takeaway
A historical late-6th-century ʿAbsī warrior-poet, son of a free Arab father and an enslaved Ethiopian mother, with a small authentic-ish dīwān; the ʿAbla romance and nearly everything colourful about him belongs to the vastly later *Sīrat ʿAntara*, a multi-volume popular epic that grew by oral accretion from roughly the 12th century onward.

### Cited Findings
- Arabic: عنترة بن شدّاد العبسيّ; popular short form عنتر (ʿAntar) — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- **Dates as given:** born c. 525 CE at Qusayba in the Qaṣīm region of Najd; died 608 CE at Ḥāʾil, aged 82–83 — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad). Note: these are traditional/reconstructed, not documented.
- Nöldeke placed his productive period at **the end of the 6th century** — consistent with his Muʿallaqa alluding to the ʿAbs–Dhubyān feuds — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat)
- **Tribe:** Banū ʿAbs. Father Shaddād al-ʿAbsī, a warrior under the ʿAbsī chief **Zuhayr** — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- **Mother:** Zabība (زبيبة), described as an Ethiopian/Abyssinian woman. He grew up enslaved — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- He is counted one of the three *aghribat al-ʿArab* ("crows of the Arabs"), sons of Arab fathers and African enslaved mothers — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad); the category and its scholarship: Bernard Lewis, "The Crows of the Arabs" (1985) — [Wikipedia: Su'luk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su%27luk)
- **Half-brother Shaybūb**, on his mother's side; his father's wife **Sumayya** is cast as his enemy — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- **ʿAbla** (عبلة): his cousin, whom he loved; his servile status barred marriage. In the story he must obtain a special camel from the Lakhmid kingdom, then ruled by al-Nuʿmān III ibn al-Mundhir, to win permission — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad). **The al-Nuʿmān III reign (580–602) is chronologically awkward against a c. 525 birth** — flag as epic, not history.
- **Manumission story:** at the outbreak of the war, Ṭayyiʾ invaded ʿAbs lands; his father ordered him to defend the tribe, promising freedom; he fought, the attackers were defeated, and he gained his freedom — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad). Note: the accessible source attributes the attack to **Ṭayyiʾ**, whereas the Dāḥis wa-l-Ghabrāʾ war is properly ʿAbs vs. Dhubyān — a discrepancy worth flagging.
- His Muʿallaqa alludes to the ʿAbs–Dhubyān feuds; the tone is markedly warlike; he is said to have killed the grandfather of al-Aḥnaf ibn Qays — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat)
- **Death accounts conflict:** Ibn Durayd says he was slain by Wasr ibn Jābir, or in battle against Ṭayyiʾ; Abū ʿUbayda says he died naturally of old age. Sources disagree on both time and manner — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- **The epic:** *Sīrat ʿAntar(a) ibn Shaddād* "grew from oral tradition to enormous size." A poetic saga about ʿAntar and ʿAbla is *traditionally* (i.e. spuriously) credited to al-Aṣmaʿī at Hārūn al-Rashīd's court — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- **Scale of the epic in print:** 32 volumes (Cairo, 1889); 10 volumes (Beirut, 1871) — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- **Epic's reach:** a Turkish translation was ordered by Sultan Mehmed II in 1477; *Qıṣṣa-i ʿAntar* manuscripts are in the Topkapı Palace Museum Library — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- **Ahlwardt's authenticity work** on the six ancient poets: W. Ahlwardt, *Bemerkungen über die Aechtheit der alten arabischen Gedichte* (1872), pp. 50ff — cited in the ʿAntara article and the origin of the 19th-century scepticism later taken up by Margoliouth — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- **Modern critical translation:** James E. Montgomery, *War Songs* (Library of Arabic Literature vol. 11), with Richard Sieburth and Peter Cole; NYU Press — [NYU Press: War Songs](https://nyupress.org/9781479858798/war-songs/); DOI edition at [De Gruyter/Brill](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.18574/nyu/9781479806553.001.0001/html?lang=en); Montgomery's own interview on the volume, "When 'All Poets Were Warriors'" — [Library of Arabic Literature](https://www.libraryofarabicliterature.org/2018/war-songs-interview-part-1/)
- **Older translations of the epic:** Terrick Hamilton, *Antar, a Bedoueen Romance*, 4 vols. (London, 1820), partial; Étienne Dinet's 1898 translation of a 13th-century ʿAntar poem; Diana Richmond, *Antar and Abla: a Bedouin Romance* (1978) — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- **Dīwān editions:** Wilhelm Ahlwardt, *The Divans of the Six Ancient Arabic Poets* (London, 1870); separate Beirut printing 1888 — [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- An accessible narrative treatment with bibliography: "Episode 113: Antarah ibn Shaddad" — [Literature and History](https://literatureandhistory.com/episode-113-antarah-ibn-shaddad/)
- ʿUrwa ibn al-Ward is linked to ʿAntara in modern popular culture through their shared ʿAbs tribe; ʿAntara has been the subject of Arab films/TV in 1961, 1978 (as *ʿUrwa ibn al-Ward*), and 2007 — [Wikipedia: Urwa ibn al-Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urwa_ibn_al-Ward)

### Inferences
- The clean split the user needs is: **historical ʿAntara** = an ʿAbsī *muḥarrab* of slave birth, fighting in the ʿAbs–Dhubyān wars at the end of the 6th century, author of a Muʿallaqa and a short dīwān of boast-and-battle verse; **epic ʿAntara** = chivalric superhero, the ʿAbla romance, the quest-objects, conquests from Byzantium to the Sudan. The ʿAbla material has no attestation in the earliest layers.
- Montgomery's *War Songs* is the single best English entry point and is also the volume most likely to discuss the authenticity question for ʿAntara specifically.

### Gaps
- The exact date and process by which *Sīrat ʿAntara* took shape (commonly placed 12th century, growing through the Mamluk era) was not confirmed by a source I could cite; the accessible sources only say it "grew from oral tradition."
- Whether Montgomery's *War Songs* is open access could not be verified (libraryofarabicliterature.org blocks automated fetching — see the Editions section below).

## The Muʿallaqāt poets: who, when, at whose court

### Takeaway
The Muʿallaqāt are a late-8th-century anthology (probably by Ḥammād al-Rāwiya) of seven — in variant lists, up to ten — long odes, whose poets span roughly 530–630 CE; the latest of them, Labīd, lived decades into the Islamic era. The "hung in the Kaʿba" story is rejected by most scholars.

### Cited Findings

**Compilation**
- Arabic: المعلقات. **Ḥammād al-Rāwiya** (8th century), Persian by descent and a client of Bakr ibn Wāʾil, is the likely original compiler; al-Naḥḥās (d. 949) attributes the collection to him, explaining that Ḥammād saw how little people cared for poetry — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat)
- **Most scholars doubt the poems were physically hung in the Kaʿba.** Robson proposed the title meant the poems were "suspended" from their rightful place; Orfali suggests the unifying link is the multi-thematic *qaṣīda* form — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat)
- Ḥammād's patronage matters for bias: al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥilliza's ode **praises the Bakr ancestors of Ḥammād's own patrons** — a direct motive for inclusion — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat)
- The standard seven are enumerated by Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih (860–940) and al-Naḥḥās; Ibn Khaldūn spoke of nine, probably a misunderstanding — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat)

**Variant list members**
- **al-Nābigha al-Dhubyānī** (النابغة الذبياني), real name Ziyād ibn Muʿāwiya: c. 535 – c. 604 CE, Banū Dhubyān, born near Mecca. Patron sequence: Lakhmid al-Mundhir III ibn al-Ḥārith, his successor from 562, and **al-Nuʿmān III ibn al-Mundhir**. His verses about the queen forced his **flight to the Ghassanid court**; he returned c. 600, and withdrew to his tribe when al-Nuʿmān died five years later. **al-Mufaḍḍal (d. c. 790) and Abū ʿUbayda (d. 824) reportedly placed his ode among the Seven in place of ʿAntara and al-Ḥārith** — [Wikipedia: Al-Nabigha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nabigha); [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat); [Britannica: al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī](https://www.britannica.com/biography/al-Nabighah-al-Dhubyani); 1911 Britannica text free at [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/N%C4%81bigha_Dhuby%C4%81n%C4%AB)
- **al-Aʿshā** (الأعشى): sometimes added. His ode alludes to the **battle of Dhī Qār** (which he calls the "Battle of Hinw"); he composed a poem honouring Muḥammad and **died not long before 630 CE** — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat)
- **ʿAbīd ibn al-Abraṣ** (عبيد بن الأبرص): a contemporary of Imruʾ al-Qays, of the Asad tribe — i.e. the tribe that killed Ḥujr. **Ibn Qutayba counted one of his poems among the Seven**; its metre deviates from the schema, possibly through transmission error — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat)

**Ṭarafa's death — the sealed-letter story**
- Ṭarafa travelled with his uncle **al-Mutalammis** (المتلمس) to ʿAmr ibn Hind's court at al-Ḥīra and became a companion of the king's brother. After mocking the king in verse, the king gave both poets leave to go home, each carrying a **sealed letter to the governor of Baḥrayn**. Neither could read. Outside the city al-Mutalammis had a boy from al-Ḥīra read his letter — it ordered him buried alive — and threw it into a stream, urging Ṭarafa to do likewise; **Ṭarafa refused to break the royal seal**, continued to Baḥrayn, and was imprisoned and executed — [Wikipedia: Tarafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarafa); al-Mutalammis has his own entry — [Wikipedia: Al-Mutalammis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mutalammis)
- The burial-alive detail is presented as **one legend** among variants; Ṭarafa was about 26 at death on the c. 543/c. 569 reckoning — [Wikipedia: Tarafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarafa)
- Note a chronological snag in the sources: the Muʿallaqāt article states that the **battle of Dhī Qār (c. 610 CE)** involves a relative of a figure in Ṭarafa's poem — this is a relative, not the poet, and should not be read as placing Ṭarafa in the 7th century — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat)

**Labīd**
- Dates c. 560 – c. 661 CE; Banū ʿĀmir of Hawāzin. His ill uncle sent him to Muḥammad in Medina for a remedy; he was moved by Sūrat al-Kawthar and accepted Islam. He composed an elegy for **Nuʿmān (ibn) al-Mundhir**. Al-Walīd ibn ʿUqba, governor of Kūfa, sent him 100 camels so he could keep his vow to feed people whenever the east wind blew — the Kūfa connection in his later life. Cited in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* hadith 3841 — [Wikipedia: Labid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labid)

### Inferences
- **For a VN chronology, the Muʿallaqāt poets cluster into two generations:** (a) the mid-6th-century group around ʿAmr ibn Hind's al-Ḥīra (r. 554–568/9) — Ṭarafa, al-Mutalammis, al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥilliza, ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm, with Imruʾ al-Qays slightly earlier; and (b) the late-6th/early-7th-century group around al-Nuʿmān III's al-Ḥīra (r. 580–602) and the Ghassanids — al-Nābigha, al-Aʿshā, Zuhayr, ʿAntara, ʿAdī ibn Zayd, with Labīd and al-Khansāʾ crossing into Islam.
- A protagonist travelling in, say, the 560s could plausibly meet Imruʾ al-Qays (old, in exile, heading north), Ṭarafa and al-Mutalammis (young, at al-Ḥīra, doomed), ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm (about to kill the king), al-Samawʾal (at Taymāʾ), al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥilliza, and al-Nābigha as a young man. A protagonist in the 590s could meet al-Nābigha, al-Aʿshā, ʿAdī ibn Zayd (soon strangled), Zuhayr, ʿAntara (old), Labīd (young), ʿUrwa ibn al-Ward, Ḥātim's son ʿAdī, Durayd ibn al-Ṣimma (old), and al-Khansāʾ (young).
- Because the anthology is an 8th-century construct with a Bakr-friendly compiler, the *set* of "canonical" Jāhilī poets is itself an Abbasid artefact. This is worth saying plainly in the report.

### Gaps
- **al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥilliza's dates are nowhere given** in the sources consulted. His ode's address to ʿAmr ibn Hind puts him in the 554–568/9 window, which is the only anchor.
- **Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā's dates** are given only as "end of the 6th century (Nöldeke)." No birth/death figures were found; his tribe (conventionally Muzayna/Ghaṭafān) is not stated in the accessible sources.
- **ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm's own dates** were not found beyond the floruit implied by ʿAmr ibn Hind's reign.
- **al-Aʿshā's name, tribe and dates** beyond "died shortly before 630" were not retrieved; he is conventionally Maymūn ibn Qays of Bakr, but I could not source that here.
- The variant "ten Muʿallaqāt" list is referenced only via an Arabic poetry site, not a scholarly source.

## The ṣaʿālīk / brigand poets

### Takeaway
The *ṣaʿālīk* are outlaw poets, mostly men expelled from their tribes, whose verse celebrates poverty, endurance, hunger, speed, and kinship with wolves and hyenas; the four the user names are real literary figures but their dates are poorly fixed, and the most famous poems attributed to them are the ones most suspected of being later forgeries.

### Cited Findings
- **Term:** صعلوك *ṣuʿlūk*, pl. صعاليك *ṣaʿālīk*, glossed "brigand," "brigand-poet," "vagabond." Biographical sources usually mean "honourable brigand"; in the poetry itself it seems to mean simply "poor" — [Wikipedia: Su'luk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su%27luk)
- **Related vocabulary** (useful for in-world texture): *dhuʾbān* ("wolves"); *khulaʿāʾ* (sing. *khalīʿ*), a man formally cast out of the tribe, the word coming to mean a rebel who consciously chooses evil; *aghribat al-ʿArab*, "crows of the Arabs" — [Wikipedia: Su'luk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su%27luk)
- **Mechanics of exclusion:** *khalāʿ* sometimes followed a crime that dishonoured the tribe; excluded men might receive protection from another tribe or be banished to places such as **the mountain of Hadawdā**. They banded together to raid tribes, steal camels, and rob caravans and markets, selling the goods on to other tribes — [Wikipedia: Su'luk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su%27luk)
- **Themes (per Albert Arazi, EI2 "Ṣuʿlūk," 1997):** *apologetic* — poverty, courage, endurance, with praise of *ṣabr*; *lyrical* — desert journeys, flora and fauna, raids, kinship with wolves and hyenas; *therapeutic* — meditation on the imminence of death — [Wikipedia: Su'luk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su%27luk)
- **al-Shanfarā (الشنفرى):** author of the *Lāmiyyat al-ʿArab*, the most famous ṣuʿlūk poem; sometimes classed among the *aghribat al-ʿArab*, which **Bernard Lewis argues is a confusion in the early sources** — [Wikipedia: Su'luk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su%27luk); [Britannica: al-Shanfarā](https://www.britannica.com/biography/al-Shanfara). He was **a member of Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's band** and, like Taʾabbaṭa Sharran and ʿAmr ibn Barrāq, famous as a fast runner — [Wikipedia: Ta'abbata Sharran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27abbata_Sharran)
- **Taʾabbaṭa Sharran (تأبط شرا)**, real name **Thābit ibn Jābir al-Fahmī** (ثابت بن جابر الفهمي). Dates unknown; from personal names in the poems, **probably late 6th or early 7th century CE**. Tribe **Fahm**, in western Arabia (Tihāma/Ḥijāz) near Ṭāʾif and Mecca. Mother Umayma al-Fahmiyya of the Banū al-Qayn; after his father Jābir died she married his enemy **Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī** (أبو كبير الهذلي). He married a woman of Banū Kilāb. **Unlike most ṣuʿlūk poets his tribe did not disown him.** He led a band raiding Bajīla, Banū Hudhayl, Azd and Khathʿam, hiding in the Sarawāt mountains — [Wikipedia: Ta'abbata Sharran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27abbata_Sharran)
- **The nickname** ("he who had evil under his arm") has three competing explanations in *Kitāb al-Aghānī*: he carried a ram that turned out to be a *ghūl* (غول); he filled his mother's truffle-bag with snakes and carried it into the tent; his mother named him for always carrying his sword under his arm on raids. **Modern scholars say these are not to be taken at face value** and read the name as marking an unavoidable tendency to trouble — [Wikipedia: Ta'abbata Sharran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27abbata_Sharran)
- **His poems:** the *Qāfiyya* opens the *Mufaḍḍaliyyāt* and boasts of his speed in escaping the Bajīla at al-Raht — **Gabrieli suggested it may be a compilation by later editors**. The *Lāmiyya*, an elegy for an uncle killed by Banū Hudhayl and the revenge taken, is called by Suzanne Stetkevych the most famous Arabic poem of blood vengeance, but **its authenticity is doubtful: al-Tibrīzī credited Khalaf al-Aḥmar, Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih credited a nephew.** He also has *ghūl* poems, including a parody of the desert love-poem — [Wikipedia: Ta'abbata Sharran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27abbata_Sharran)
- **Correction to a common attribution:** the famous anonymous elegy for him in the *Ḥamāsa* is **not** by his nephew; it is variously attributed to his mother or to the mother of **al-Sulayk ibn al-Sulaka**, and its author is unknown — [Wikipedia: Ta'abbata Sharran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27abbata_Sharran)
- **His death:** killed during a raid on Banū Hudhayl; his body thrown into a cave called al-Rakhmān (الرخمان). His dīwān contains **238 verses in 32 poems and fragments**, ed. ʿAlī Dhū al-Faqār Shākir (1984) — [Wikipedia: Ta'abbata Sharran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27abbata_Sharran)
- **Afterlife:** Qays ibn ʿAzara of Banū Hudhayl lampooned him as *Shaʿl* ("firebrand"), naming his wife Umm Jundab (أم جندب). In al-Maʿarrī's *Risālat al-Ghufrān* (c. 1033) he and al-Shanfarā are both placed in hell, and he is asked whether he married a *ghūl*. **Adūnīs** later praised both as exemplars of "the literature of rejection." In Europe, scholarly interest began with al-Shanfarā and spread to Taʾabbaṭa Sharran; **Goethe put a German version of the Lāmiyya in *West-östlicher Divan* (1819)**, working from Latin translations by Freytag and Michaelis — [Wikipedia: Ta'abbata Sharran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27abbata_Sharran)
- **ʿUrwa ibn al-Ward (عروة بن الورد):** dates given as **520–600 CE**; tribe **Banū ʿAbs** (hence the modern association with ʿAntara). Celebrated as a heroic outlaw, skilled archer and swordsman, and **supporter of the poor who stole to feed them**; reputed "the most generous of the ancient Arabs." Some of his poetry concerns his estranged wife **Salmā**, whom he divorced while drunk and then despaired over when sober — [Wikipedia: Urwa ibn al-Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urwa_ibn_al-Ward)
- **ʿUrwa's editions/scholarship:** A. Arazi, "ʿUrwa b. al-Ward," EI2 vol. 10 (2000); Theodor Nöldeke, *Die Gedichte des ʿUrwa ibn al-Ward al-ʿAbsī* (1863; dīwān 1864); René Basset, *Le Dîwân de ʿOrwa ben el Ward* (Geuthner, 1928); Albert Socin in *ZDMG* (1877) — [Wikipedia: Urwa ibn al-Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urwa_ibn_al-Ward)
- **al-Sulayk ibn al-Sulaka (السليك بن السلكة):** named by Arazi as **one of only three ṣaʿālīk who were also *aghribat al-ʿArab***, with Khufāf ibn Nudba and al-Ḥārith ibn Sharīd — i.e. his mother was an African enslaved woman — [Wikipedia: Su'luk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su%27luk)
- **Later history of the type:** ṣuʿlūk poetry began before Islam, continued under the Umayyads, and largely disappeared under the Abbasids (only Bakr ibn al-Naṭṭāḥ is attested then). Muḥammad offered to spare ṣaʿālīk who converted and let them keep their booty. They served as mercenaries in the First Fitna, notably at the Battle of the Camel, and fought for Yazīd ibn ʿUmar al-Fazārī at the siege of Wāsiṭ (749–750) — [Wikipedia: Su'luk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su%27luk)
- **Key scholarship:** Suzanne Pinckney Stetkevych on al-Shanfarā and the *Lāmiyyat al-ʿArab* (1986) and on Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's *rithāʾ* (1986), both developed in *The Mute Immortals Speak* (1993); Roger Allen, *The Arabic Literary Heritage* (2005) on *ṣabr* and the genres; Pierre Larcher's French translation (2012); Alan Jones, *Early Arabic Poetry* (2011); and an Arabic-language overview by Muḥammad Shaʿbān Ayyūb (al-Jazeera, 2017) — [Wikipedia: Su'luk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su%27luk); [Wikipedia: Ta'abbata Sharran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27abbata_Sharran)

### Inferences
- The ṣaʿālīk are the single best-suited group for a travelling-protagonist VN: they are mobile, outside tribal protection, defined by encounter rather than court, and their world (the Sarawāt mountains, Hadawdā, caravan routes near Ṭāʾif and Mecca) is geographically concrete.
- Note the authenticity irony the report should flag: the two poems that make these figures famous — the *Lāmiyyat al-ʿArab* and Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's *Lāmiyya* — are precisely the ones with the strongest forgery attributions (Khalaf al-Aḥmar is a known 8th-century forger of "ancient" verse). Their *personae* are more securely medieval-literary than their biographies are historical.

### Gaps
- **No dates whatsoever for al-Shanfarā or al-Sulayk ibn al-Sulaka** were found; al-Shanfarā's tribe (conventionally Azd/Banū Salāmān) is likewise unsourced here. Conventional handbooks place al-Shanfarā in the mid-to-late 6th century, but I could not cite that.
- Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's dates remain explicitly "unknown" in the sources, only inferred as late 6th/early 7th century.
- The name is spelled inconsistently in the accessible sources (Sulayk ibn al-Salaka / al-Sulaka); flag for the user when searching Arabic.

## Other key figures

### Takeaway
This group splits into (a) reasonably well-attested late-Jāhilī tribal figures (al-Khansāʾ, Durayd, ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib, ʿAdī ibn Zayd, ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim), (b) proverb-figures whose historicity is plausible but whose stories are moral exempla (Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī, al-Samawʾal), and (c) frankly legendary or semi-legendary figures (Zarqāʾ al-Yamāma, Quss ibn Sāʿida).

### Cited Findings

**al-Samawʾal ibn ʿĀdiyāʾ (السموأل بن عادياء) — the paragon of fidelity**
- Arabic: السموأل بن عادياء بن رفاعة بن الحارث بن كعب; Hebrew: שמואל בן עדיה. Born early 6th century at Taymāʾ, northern Arabia; **died c. 560** — [Wikipedia: Samaw'al ibn 'Adiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaw%27al_ibn_%27Adiya)
- **Jewish:** his clan converted to Judaism in southern Arabia, then moved north and settled at Taymāʾ. Genealogy disputed — Ibn Durayd calls him son of a priest, Hārūn ibn ʿImrān; al-Jawālīqī says his mother was of Ghassān and his father of Azd — [Wikipedia: Samaw'al ibn 'Adiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaw%27al_ibn_%27Adiya)
- **al-Ablaq (الأبلق):** built by his grandfather ʿĀdiyāʾ on a high hill near Taymāʾ, named for its mixed/piebald colouring; **a stopping place for travellers to and from Syria** — i.e. on the main northern caravan route, ideal for a VN waypoint. Al-Aʿshā's description of it confuses it with Solomon's Temple — [Wikipedia: Samaw'al ibn 'Adiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaw%27al_ibn_%27Adiya)
- **The fidelity story:** after Imruʾ al-Qays left his daughter Hind, his cousin, and his armour in trust and never returned, "Prince al-Mundhir" sent al-Ḥārith to demand the deposits. Al-Samawʾal refused; al-Ḥārith besieged al-Ablaq, captured al-Samawʾal's son returning from a hunt, and offered the father the choice of surrendering the property or watching his son die. **Al-Samawʾal refused and the son was killed in front of him**; al-Ḥārith then withdrew. This gave rise to the proverb أوفى من السموأل, "more loyal than al-Samawʾal" — [Wikipedia: Samaw'al ibn 'Adiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaw%27al_ibn_%27Adiya)
- **Reliability:** the son's capture while hunting is attributed to *Kitāb al-Aghānī*; the refusal and killing are "the reported story, supported by a few verses." **It is unknown whether Imruʾ al-Qays died before or after the siege.** His fame rests on the Imruʾ al-Qays connection more than on his poetry. A poem attributed to him was examined by D. S. Margoliouth, "A poem attributed to al-Samawʾal," *JRAS* (1906) — [Wikipedia: Samaw'al ibn 'Adiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaw%27al_ibn_%27Adiya)
- Editions/scholarship: J. W. Hirschberg, *Der Diwan des as-Samauʾal ibn ʿAdiāʾ* (1931); *Dīwān al-Samawʾal*, ed. Wadīʿ al-Ṣamad (Beirut, 1996); Franz Delitzsch, *Jüdisch-Arabische Poesien aus Vormuhammedischer Zeit* (1874); Nöldeke, *Beiträge zur Kenntniss der Poesie der Alten Araber* (1864); G. Levi Della Vida, "A proposito di as-Samawʾal," *RSO* XIII (1931); *Jewish Encyclopedia* (1901–06), s.v. "Samuel bin ʿAdiya" — [Wikipedia: Samaw'al ibn 'Adiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaw%27al_ibn_%27Adiya)

**Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī (حاتم بن عبد الله بن سعد الطائي) — the paragon of generosity**
- **Died 578 CE** per the article's citation to *Kitāb al-Aghānī*; lived late 6th century; buried at Tuwārin, Ḥāʾil (though d'Herbelot reports the tomb elsewhere). Tribe **Ṭayyiʾ** (الطائي) — [Wikipedia: Hatim al-Tai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)
- Proverb أكرم من حاتم, "more generous than Ḥātim," still current. **Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih counted him among three peak-generosity figures of the Jāhiliyya, with Kaʿb ibn Māma and Harim ibn Sinān al-Murrī** — a useful triad for the user. Reportedly gave away everything but his mount and weapons — [Wikipedia: Hatim al-Tai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)
- **Court connection:** associated with the Lakhmid court at al-Ḥīra, especially under al-Mundhir III — [Wikipedia: Hatim al-Tai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)
- **Legend layer:** the extreme-generosity stories that made him an icon; the seven-riddle quest of the Persian/Urdu *Qiṣṣa-e Ḥātim Ṭāʾī*; appearances in the *Arabian Nights*; a tradition (Rawḍat al-Ṣafāʾ) dating his death to the year of Muḥammad's birth, reported without endorsement — [Wikipedia: Hatim al-Tai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)
- **His son ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim (عدي بن حاتم الطائي):** has a substantial independent biography as a companion of Muḥammad and later partisan of ʿAlī — [Wikipedia: Adi ibn Hatim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_ibn_Hatim); [WikiShia: Adi b. Hatim al-Ta'i](https://en.wikishia.net/view/Adi_b._Hatim_al-Ta%27i)

**al-Khansāʾ (الخنساء) — Tumāḍir bint ʿAmr**
- Full name تماضر بنت عمرو بن الحارث بن الشريد السُلمية; epithet الخنساء, "snub-nosed." Tribe **Banū Sulaym**; daughter of the head of the al-Sharīd clan. **No birth or death date is given** in the accessible source; she is a contemporary of Muḥammad — [Wikipedia: Al-Khansa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khansa)
- **612 CE:** her brother **Muʿāwiya** killed by members of another tribe. Her brother **Ṣakhr** avenged him and died of his wounds about a year later. Both deaths came in Banū Sulaym's skirmishes with **Banū Murra and Banū Asad** — [Wikipedia: Al-Khansa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khansa)
- **629 CE:** she went to Medina with a deputation from her clan and embraced Islam after meeting Muḥammad. He asked her to recite and encouraged her to continue ("Go on, Khunās!"); he is also said to have rated her above Imruʾ al-Qays as a poet — [Wikipedia: Al-Khansa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khansa)
- **Over a hundred elegies for her two brothers alone; nearly a thousand lines survive.** Four of her sons — Yazīd, Muʿāwiya, ʿAmr and ʿAmra — were killed at the **Battle of Qādisiyya (636)**. Her elegies are noted for short, despairing lines and for raising the *marthiya* to the level of *qarīḍ* — [Wikipedia: Al-Khansa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khansa)
- **Transmission:** **Ibn al-Sikkīt (802–858) collected her elegies** — i.e. two centuries later. Also Ibn Qutayba, *al-Shiʿr wa-l-shuʿarāʾ*; Buṭrus al-Bustānī, *Udabāʾ al-ʿArab fī al-Jāhiliyya wa-ṣadr al-Islām* (1968) — [Wikipedia: Al-Khansa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khansa)
- **Translations:** Geert Jan van Gelder, trans., *Classical Arabic Literature: A Library of Arabic Literature Anthology* (2013); **James E. Montgomery, *Loss Sings*, The Cahiers Series 32 (2019)** — a short, high-quality rendering specifically of al-Khansāʾ; **Yasmine Seale was announced in 2021 as translator of al-Khansāʾ for the Library of Arabic Literature** — [Wikipedia: Al-Khansa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khansa)

**Durayd ibn al-Ṣimma (دريد بن الصمة)**
- Born 6th century; **died January 630 CE**. Tribe **Hawāzin**; chief of **Banū Jusham ibn Muʿāwiya**. Father Muʿāwiya ibn al-Ḥārith al-Khuzaʿī al-Jushamī; mother **Rayḥāna bint Maʿdīkarib** — i.e. kin to ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib — [Wikipedia: Durayd ibn al-Simma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durayd_ibn_al-Simma)
- Already an old man at the rise of Islam and **remained a pagan**. He **took part in the Battle of Ḥunayn** (the accessible source gives the January 630 death date but does not itself state he died at Ḥunayn — though Ibn Hishām has a chapter titled "Maqtal Durayd ibn al-Ṣimma," مقتل دريد بن الصمة) — [Wikipedia: Durayd ibn al-Simma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durayd_ibn_al-Simma)
- **Fought in about a hundred battles and, per Abū ʿUbayda, lost none.** Muḥammad ibn Sallām al-Jumaḥī called him **the first poet of the knights** (*fāris*-poets) — [Wikipedia: Durayd ibn al-Simma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durayd_ibn_al-Simma)
- Sources: Abū Ḥātim al-Sijistānī, *al-Muʿammarūn wa-l-Waṣāyā*; Ibn Ḥazm, *Jamharat Ansāb al-ʿArab*; *Kitāb al-Aghānī*; Alan Jones, *Early Arabic Poetry: Select Poems* (2011) — [Wikipedia: Durayd ibn al-Simma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durayd_ibn_al-Simma)

**al-Muhalhil / ʿAdī ibn Rabīʿa and the Basūs war**
- **Dating:** the Basūs war (حرب البسوس) is dated **c. 494–540 CE**, traditionally said to have lasted forty years, but **modern scholars see it as a composite narrative, not a single prolonged war**; by the 530s the fighting had ended — [Wikipedia: Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- Participants: **Kulayb ibn Rabīʿa** of Taghlib, who killed the she-camel of **al-Basūs**, a woman related to Jassās; **Jassās ibn Murra** of Shaybān (Banū Bakr), who killed Kulayb after al-Basūs's lament; **Abū Layla al-Muhalhil**, the leading Taghlib commander (the article does **not** use the name "ʿAdī ibn Rabīʿa"); **ʿUday ibn Murra**, a Bakrī chief — [Wikipedia: Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War); [Wikipedia: Al-Muhalhil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Muhalhil); [Wikipedia: Kulaib ibn Rabiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kulaib_ibn_Rabiah); [Wikipedia: Jassas ibn Murrah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jassas_ibn_Murrah); [Wikipedia: Al-Basus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Basus)
- Narrative beats: camel killed → lament → Kulayb killed → Taghlib wins Yawm al-Ḥaẓr, casualties falling mostly on Shaybān → Bakr gradually joins → Bakr withdraws and returns ~forty years later with a younger generation → **al-Muhalhil is captured in South Arabia and forced to marry a woman of the Madhḥaj tribe** → Taghlib disperses into Iraq. The war is cited as an aphorism against grudges and vendetta — [Wikipedia: Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)
- **Flagged as legendary:** the camel-killing as cause; the lament as trigger; the forty-year duration and the "breed a new army" explanation; the moral framing. **Early sources disagree on sequence, participants and motives** — [Wikipedia: Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War)

**Quss ibn Sāʿida al-Iyādī (قس بن ساعدة الإيادي)**
- **Died c. 610 CE**; lived in the 6th and early 7th centuries; buried in north Syria, probably at Mount Simon. Tribe **Iyād** (إياد), North Arabian. **Sources give conflicting lifespans of 380, 600, or 700 years** — a clear marker of legendary accretion — [Wikipedia: Quss Ibn Sa'ida al-Iyadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quss_Ibn_Sa%27ida_al-Iyadi)
- **Bishop of Najrān** (نجران); renowned for poetry, rhymed prose (*sajʿ*) and sermons; **preached in the marketplace of ʿUkāẓ (عكاظ) near Mecca**, hoping to win people to Christianity — [Wikipedia: Quss Ibn Sa'ida al-Iyadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quss_Ibn_Sa%27ida_al-Iyadi)
- Islamic tradition says the young Muḥammad heard him at ʿUkāẓ; sources date this either before the first revelation (610) or before the Hijra (622). **Irfan Shahîd argues this exposure would have given Muḥammad a natural route into Christian tradition and stylistic influence.** Muḥammad reportedly praised him and recited one of his speeches on learning of his death — [Wikipedia: Quss Ibn Sa'ida al-Iyadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quss_Ibn_Sa%27ida_al-Iyadi)
- **Abū Hilāl al-ʿAskarī credits him as the first to believe in the one God at Mecca before Islam and in the Resurrection.** Al-Shahrastānī names **Waraqa ibn Nawfal (ورقة بن نوفل)** and **Zayd ibn ʿAmr (زيد بن عمرو)** as the other early Arab monotheists — [Wikipedia: Quss Ibn Sa'ida al-Iyadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quss_Ibn_Sa%27ida_al-Iyadi)
- **Status: "considered legendary or semi-legendary and known only from Muslim sources."** This is the sharpest reliability verdict in any of the sources I consulted and should be reported as such — [Wikipedia: Quss Ibn Sa'ida al-Iyadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quss_Ibn_Sa%27ida_al-Iyadi)

**Others (minimal findings — see Gaps)**
- **Waraqa ibn Nawfal (ورقة بن نوفل):** named in al-Shahrastānī's list of pre-Islamic Arab monotheists; the standard tradition makes him Khadīja's cousin who recognised Muḥammad's first revelation — [Wikipedia: Waraqah ibn Nawfal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waraqah_ibn_Nawfal); [Wikipedia: Quss Ibn Sa'ida al-Iyadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quss_Ibn_Sa%27ida_al-Iyadi)
- **Umayya ibn Abī al-Ṣalt (أمية بن أبي الصلت):** Ṭāʾif poet, traditionally a monotheist with Qurʾān-adjacent verse — [Wikipedia: Umayya ibn Abi as-Salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayya_ibn_Abi_as-Salt)
- **ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib (عمرو بن معدي كرب):** Yemeni warrior-poet of Zubayd, kin to Durayd's mother Rayḥāna bint Maʿdīkarib — [Wikipedia: Amr ibn Ma'adi Yakrib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amr_ibn_Ma%27adi_Yakrib); [Wikipedia: Durayd ibn al-Simma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durayd_ibn_al-Simma)
- **Zarqāʾ al-Yamāma (زرقاء اليمامة):** the blue-eyed seeress of al-Yamāma, a legendary figure; there is a comparative study of her reception in modern Arabic poetry — [Wikipedia: Zarqa al Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarqa_al_Yamama); [ResearchGate: Zarqa' al-Yamama in Modern Arabic Poetry](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240582077_Zarqa'_al-Yamama_in_the_Modern_Arabic_Poetry_A_Comparative_Reading)
- **Hind bint al-Nuʿmān (هند بنت النعمان):** a **granddaughter** of al-Nuʿmān III, Christian, described as exceptionally beautiful, married to **ʿAdī ibn Zayd**. ʿAdī and she appear in an *Arabian Nights* tale, "ʿAdī ibn Zayd and the Princess Hind" (the source marks this citation-needed) — [Wikipedia: Adi ibn Zayd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_ibn_Zayd). Related: the "Monastery of Hind the Younger" at al-Ḥīra — [Wikipedia: Monastery of Hind the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastery_of_Hind_the_Younger); and al-Ḥurqa, another Lakhmid princess — [Wikipedia: Al-Hurqah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hurqah)
- **Note a naming confusion to avoid:** the sources call Hind both *daughter* and *granddaughter* of al-Nuʿmān; the Lakhmid women called Hind (Hind the Elder, Hind the Younger, al-Ḥurqa) are repeatedly conflated in the tradition.

### Inferences
- Ḥātim's death (578) and al-Samawʾal's (c. 560) place both squarely in the generation a protagonist travelling in the 550s–570s would meet; ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, Durayd, al-Khansāʾ and Labīd belong to the next generation and carry the story into Islam, making them the natural "bridge" characters for a narrative that spans the transition.
- The proverb-pairs (أوفى من السموأل / أكرم من حاتم) are the most efficient dramatic hooks in the whole corpus, because the moral claim and the test-scene are built into the figure.

### Gaps
- **al-Khansāʾ has no birth or death date** in the accessible sources — only the 612 and 629 event anchors, and her sons' deaths at Qādisiyya (636) implying she lived past that.
- **ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib, Umayya ibn Abī al-Ṣalt, Waraqa ibn Nawfal, and Zarqāʾ al-Yamāma were not researched in depth** — I identified their pages but did not have the tool budget to fetch and extract them. The report-writer should treat these four as needing follow-up.
- **al-Muhalhil's identification with ʿAdī ibn Rabīʿa was not confirmed**: the Basūs War article names him only as "Abū Layla al-Muhalhil" and does not use "ʿAdī ibn Rabīʿa." He has a separate article I did not fetch.
- **No "notable women poets" survey** beyond al-Khansāʾ and Hind was found; the Jāhilī women's elegiac tradition (e.g. the mothers credited with the *Ḥamāsa* elegy) is attested only in fragments in what I consulted.
- No source was found that gives dates for Ḥātim's birth.

## Patrons and courts: al-Ḥīra, the Ghassanids, Kinda, and the fairs

### Takeaway
Three courts and one fair-circuit structure the whole poetic economy: the Lakhmids at al-Ḥīra (Sasanian clients, by far the richest attested patronage), the Ghassanids in Syria (Byzantine clients), the Kindite kings in Najd (the earlier, collapsed power), and the ʿUkāẓ/Majanna/Dhū al-Majāz fair circuit near Mecca.

### Cited Findings

**al-Ḥīra under the Lakhmids**
- **ʿAmr ibn Hind (عمرو بن هند)**, i.e. ʿAmr III ibn al-Mundhir, **reigned 554–568/9**. He is characterised as having a violent, implacable temper. He was the patron — and executioner — of Ṭarafa, the addressee of al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥilliza's ode, and **was killed by ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm** of Taghlib — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat); [Wikipedia: Tarafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarafa); [Wikipedia: Amr ibn Hind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amr_ibn_Hind); [Britannica: ʿAmr ibn Hind](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Amr-ibn-Hind)
- **al-Nuʿmān III ibn al-Mundhir (النعمان الثالث بن المنذر)**, **r. 580–602** (one passage in the sources gives 580–595 — a discrepancy to flag). Patron of al-Nābigha and, disastrously, of ʿAdī ibn Zayd — [Wikipedia: Al-Nu'man III ibn al-Mundhir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nu%27man_III_ibn_al-Mundhir); [Wikipedia: Adi ibn Zayd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_ibn_Zayd)
- **al-Mundhir III ibn al-Ḥārith**: earlier Lakhmid patron, of al-Nābigha and (per the Ḥātim article) of Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī; succeeded from 562 — [Wikipedia: Al-Nabigha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nabigha); [Wikipedia: Hatim al-Tai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatim_al-Tai)
- **ʿAdī ibn Zayd (عَدِيُّ بْنُ زَيْدٍ العِبَادِيُّ التَمِيمِيُّ)**: born c. 550 at al-Ḥīra, died c. 600. A 6th-century **Arab Christian (Nestorian) poet of an ʿIbādī family of al-Ḥīra**, of Tamīm descent, his family having migrated from Yemen. **His grandfather and father both served the Lakhmids.** Educated at the Sasanian court at al-Madāʾin; spoke Arabic and Persian, probably Syriac — [Wikipedia: Adi ibn Zayd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_ibn_Zayd)
- ʿAdī served as *dabīr* (secretary) for Arab affairs under **Hormizd IV (r. 579–590)** and **Khosrow II (r. 590–628)**. **In 579 he was sent to Constantinople on the Sasanian king's orders and brought back several books.** He married a granddaughter of al-Nuʿmān III and is said to have helped al-Nuʿmān accede to power. His influence drew court jealousy; rivals accused him of favouring Persian over Arab interests — [Wikipedia: Adi ibn Zayd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_ibn_Zayd)
- **al-Nuʿmān had him imprisoned and then strangled, c. 600.** His death is believed to have worsened Sasanian–Lakhmid relations, contributing to the dynasty's decline — [Wikipedia: Adi ibn Zayd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_ibn_Zayd)
- Scholarship on ʿAdī: Isabel Toral-Niehoff (2008) on his pre-Islamic creation/fall-of-man poem; Tilman Seidensticker, "ʿAdī b. Zayd," **EI3** (Brill, doi:10.1163/1573-3912_ei3_COM_22804); Francesco Gabrieli, "ʿAdī ibn Zaid, il poeta di al-Ḥīrah," *Rendiconti dell'Accademia Nazionale dei Lincei* ser. VIII vol. I (1946), 81–96; Hashem Rajabzadeh, "Dabīr," **Encyclopaedia Iranica** VI/5, 534–539 — [Wikipedia: Adi ibn Zayd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_ibn_Zayd)
- Other al-Ḥīra court figures with their own entries: **al-Mutalammis** (Ṭarafa's uncle) and **al-Munakhkhal** — [Wikipedia: Al-Mutalammis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mutalammis); [Wikipedia: Al-Munakhal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Munakhal)

**The Ghassanids**
- Al-Nābigha "spent time at the Ghassanid court," fleeing there after offending al-Nuʿmān III, and returned c. 600 — the clearest attested instance of a poet shuttling between the two client courts — [Wikipedia: Al-Nabigha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nabigha)
- **al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala**, the Ghassanid prince, sponsored Imruʾ al-Qays's appeal to Justinian I; **al-Ḥārith ibn Abī Shamir** is named as the recipient of Imruʾ al-Qays's letter of introduction — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais); [Wikipedia: Samaw'al ibn 'Adiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaw%27al_ibn_%27Adiya)
- Dynasty overview — [Britannica: Ghassanid dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ghassanid-dynasty); on al-Jābiya — [HistoryAtlas: Al Jabiyah](https://www.historyatlas.com/locations/al-jabiyah-syria/)

**Kinda**
- Kinda originated in South Arabia and migrated north to Najd in the 4th–5th century; **Ḥujr Ākil al-Murār was its first king**; the dynasty's position collapsed after its Ḥimyarite sponsor fell to the Axumites in 525, and al-Ḥārith lost al-Ḥīra to al-Mundhir when Khosrow I restored the Lakhmids — [Wikipedia: Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)

**ʿUkāẓ and the fairs**
- **ʿUkāẓ (عكاظ)** was a marketplace near Mecca where **Quss ibn Sāʿida preached**, and where tradition places the young Muḥammad in the audience either before 610 or before 622 — [Wikipedia: Quss Ibn Sa'ida al-Iyadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quss_Ibn_Sa%27ida_al-Iyadi)

### Inferences
- **The patronage map a VN can use:** al-Ḥīra is the one court with attested salaried positions, Persian-language administration, Christian clergy, and a documented route to Constantinople (ʿAdī's 579 embassy). It is also the most lethal — Ṭarafa executed, ʿAdī strangled, the king himself murdered by a poet. That cluster of violence around a single court within fifty years is the strongest dramatic material in the whole dossier and is better attested than anything about the ṣaʿālīk.
- ʿAdī ibn Zayd is the single most useful "insider" NPC: trilingual, court-raised, Christian, with a documented journey to Constantinople in 579 that parallels Imruʾ al-Qays's legendary one, and a death that can be dated to within a few years.
- The Lakhmid/Ghassanid rivalry gives a built-in reason for a travelling protagonist to carry messages or poems between Najd, al-Ḥīra and Syria — exactly what al-Nābigha is attested as doing.

### Gaps
- **The ʿUkāẓ fair itself is badly under-documented in what I could reach.** I found no source giving its location (conventionally between Ṭāʾif and Nakhla), the dates in the sacred months on which it was held, its poetry-competition function, or when it ceased (conventionally 726 CE). **Majanna (مجنة) and Dhū al-Majāz (ذو المجاز) were not found at all.** The "poets competed at ʿUkāẓ and the winner's ode was hung in the Kaʿba" story is a standard claim that the Muʿallaqāt article's scepticism about the Kaʿba undercuts, but I could not source the fair-competition claim directly either way. **This is the largest single gap in this file.**
- **The Ghassanid court at al-Jābiya/Jalliq**: no source I reached names a specific poet-patron pairing there with a date, beyond al-Nābigha's unspecified sojourn. Ḥassān ibn Thābit's Ghassanid panegyrics (the standard example) were not covered.
- The reign dates of al-Nuʿmān III are given inconsistently (580–602 vs. 580–595) within a single source.

## Reliability: the authenticity controversy and the status of the akhbār

### Takeaway
The radical forgery thesis of Margoliouth (1925) and Ṭāhā Ḥusayn (1925/1927) is no longer held by specialists, but the moderate conclusion that replaced it is still significant for a novelist: the *poems* are broadly accepted as genuinely pre-Islamic products of an oral tradition, while the *biographies* are Abbasid-era *akhbār* substantially constructed to explain the verses. The user should treat poem and life-story as having very different evidentiary status.

### Cited Findings
- **D. S. Margoliouth**, "The Origins of Arabic Poetry," *JRAS* (1925): argued the poems labelled pre-Islamic were later forgeries modelled on Qurʾānic style, partly on his reading of Qurʾān 26:224–227. He built on earlier doubts by **W. Ahlwardt (1872)** and **Sir Charles Lyall** (though Lyall rated the transmitters more highly) — [Islamic Awareness: On Pre-Islamic Poetry & The Qur'an](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/polemics/poetry.html)
- **Ṭāhā Ḥusayn**, *Fī al-shiʿr al-jāhilī* (1925), **withdrawn after protests**, reissued in modified form as *Fī al-adab al-jāhilī* (1927) with the core argument retained and the offending passages dropped. He argued pre-Islamic literature was a later forgery produced by a conspiracy with political, religious and exegetical motives — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/polemics/poetry.html); [Wikipedia: On Pre-Islamic Poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Pre-Islamic_Poetry); an accessible account of the controversy's impact on Arab literary criticism — [Abra Mundi: "On Pre-Islamic Poetry" by Ṭāhā Ḥusayn](https://www.abramundi.org/post/on-pre-islamic-poetry-by-taha-husayn-a-shock-that-awakened-arab-literary-criticism)
- **A. J. Arberry**, *The Seven Odes* (1957): called the sophistry of Margoliouth's arguments "only too apparent," while hesitating to allege dishonesty; judged that **the case against authenticity looks strong in aggregate but weakens when each argument is examined separately**. He catalogued the Arab and European rebuttals — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/polemics/poetry.html)
- **Irfan Shahîd (1965)** judged Margoliouth's reading of Qurʾān 26:224–227 a failed effort that undermined his wider case — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/polemics/poetry.html)
- **H. A. R. Gibb**'s methodological objection: reconstructing Jāhilī poetry out of Umayyad poetry would be as impossible as reconstructing Elizabethan drama out of Caroline drama — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/polemics/poetry.html)
- **Michael Zwettler**, *The Oral Tradition of Classical Arabic Poetry* (1978): traced doubts about the transmitters from Abū ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAlāʾ and Ibn Sallām through Ahlwardt, Margoliouth and Ḥusayn; **held that the extreme forms of these doubts were later laid to rest**; and reframed the corpus as **oral-traditional poetry preserved and performed by *ruwāt*** — which relocates the question from "forged or genuine" to "what kind of fidelity an oral tradition has" — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/polemics/poetry.html)
- **Régis Blachère** is named among the European scholars who refuted Margoliouth and Ḥusayn, with no arguments summarised — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/polemics/poetry.html)
- **Internal evidence of forgery exists within the corpus itself:** Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's *Lāmiyya*, "the most famous Arabic poem of blood vengeance" (Stetkevych), is of doubtful authenticity — **al-Tibrīzī credited it to Khalaf al-Aḥmar** and Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih to a nephew. His *Qāfiyya*, which opens the *Mufaḍḍaliyyāt*, may per Gabrieli be a compilation by later editors — [Wikipedia: Ta'abbata Sharran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27abbata_Sharran)
- **Evidence that the anthology itself is a construct with motives:** the Muʿallaqāt were probably assembled by **Ḥammād al-Rāwiya** in the 8th century, a client of Bakr ibn Wāʾil, and the ode of al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥilliza included in the set **praises the Bakr ancestors of Ḥammād's patrons**. Variant lists differ on membership (al-Mufaḍḍal and Abū ʿUbayda substituting al-Nābigha for ʿAntara and al-Ḥārith; Ibn Qutayba admitting ʿAbīd ibn al-Abraṣ), and ʿAbīd's poem's metre deviates from the schema — [Wikipedia: Mu'allaqat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27allaqat)
- **Evidence that akhbār were built around verses:** the nickname of Taʾabbaṭa Sharran has **three mutually exclusive explanatory stories in *Kitāb al-Aghānī*** alone, and "modern scholars say these should not be taken at face value." The Basūs War is "a composite narrative, not a single prolonged war," with early sources disagreeing on sequence, participants and motives. Al-Samawʾal's fidelity story is "the reported story, supported by a few verses" — the verses being the evidence from which the story was grown — [Wikipedia: Ta'abbata Sharran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27abbata_Sharran); [Wikipedia: Basus War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basus_War); [Wikipedia: Samaw'al ibn 'Adiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaw%27al_ibn_%27Adiya)
- **Gap between poet and compiler:** al-Khansāʾ's elegies were collected by **Ibn al-Sikkīt (802–858)**, roughly two centuries after her; the ʿAntar romance is "traditionally credited to al-Aṣmaʿī" at Hārūn al-Rashīd's court, a spurious attribution of the same type — [Wikipedia: Al-Khansa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khansa); [Wikipedia: Antarah ibn Shaddad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarah_ibn_Shaddad)
- An Arabic-literature-adjacent discussion of the translation problem, including remarks on rendering the Muʿallaqāt — [ArabLit: On Bringing the Muʿallaqāt into English](https://arablit.org/2017/11/27/on-bringing-the-muallaqat-into-english-theres-such-a-divide-that-needs-to-be-crossed-by-the-translator/)

### Inferences
- A practical three-tier rubric for the user's VN, derived from the above: **(1) Attested** — a poem's existence in the early anthologies; a poet's tribe; a court's reign dates; externally datable events (Axumite Yemen 525, Justinian 527–565, Hormizd IV 579–590, Khosrow II 590–628, Ḥunayn 630, Qādisiyya 636). **(2) Akhbār** — nearly all named episodes: the sealed letters, the three deposits at al-Ablaq, the camel of al-Basūs, the nickname stories, the meeting with Justinian. Usable as in-world *story*, i.e. what people in 6th-century Arabia would themselves be telling. **(3) Later accretion** — *Sīrat ʿAntara*, the poisoned robe, the Kaʿba suspension, Quss's 700-year lifespan, the *Qiṣṣa-e Ḥātim Ṭāʾī* riddles.
- **This tiering is a gift to the VN's design rather than a constraint.** Because the akhbār were themselves circulating stories, a protagonist who hears contradictory versions of how Taʾabbaṭa Sharran got his name, or whether Justinian really sent a robe, is behaving exactly as a traveller in that world would. The unreliability is period-accurate.
- The scholarly consensus is best stated as: the corpus is genuinely old oral poetry, imperfectly transmitted and with a real admixture of 8th-century forgery (Khalaf al-Aḥmar being the named culprit); the biographies are largely 9th–10th-century narrative scholarship.

### Gaps
- **The main source I could reach on this debate (islamic-awareness.org) is an apologetic site**, and I have flagged it as such; its characterisation of opponents is one-sided and it was unable to cover **Nāṣir al-Dīn al-Asad, James Montgomery, Gregor Schoeler, or Alan Jones at all.** The report-writer should not rely on it for the current state of the field.
- **Nāṣir al-Dīn al-Asad's *Maṣādir al-shiʿr al-jāhilī* (the standard Arabic rebuttal, arguing for early written transmission) was not reached**, nor was any account of his arguments.
- **Gregor Schoeler's work on orality/writing in early Arabic transmission was not reached.** His position (that the tradition was neither purely oral nor purely written, but used writing as a memory aid alongside oral performance) is the modern refinement of Zwettler and is important, but I cannot cite it from this research.
- **James Montgomery's *The Vagaries of the Qaṣīdah* (1997) was not reached**; only his translation work surfaced.
- **Suzanne Stetkevych's *The Mute Immortals Speak* (1993)** is cited repeatedly at second hand but I could not access it directly to characterise its ritual/rite-of-passage reading of the qaṣīda.
- No source was found quantifying how much of *Kitāb al-Aghānī*'s biographical material scholars consider verse-derived; the inference above is drawn from multiple individual cases, not a scholarly estimate.

### Inferences
- The user's practical shopping list: **Ithra parallel-text PDF (free)** for the Muʿallaqāt in both languages; **Montgomery's *War Songs*** for ʿAntara, borrowable free through OverDrive/Libby; **van Gelder's LAL anthology** for breadth; **Arberry** or **Sells** for the odes in good English; **al-Ghalāyīnī's *Rijāl al-Muʿallaqāt al-ʿAshr*** and the **al-Dulaymī *Aghānī*** for Arabic-language depth. Sells is the better choice if the user wants prose rhythm he can learn from as a writer; Arberry if he wants completeness.
- Because the user writes in English but reads Arabic natively, the parallel-text and Arabic-primary resources are disproportionately valuable to him relative to a monolingual researcher.
