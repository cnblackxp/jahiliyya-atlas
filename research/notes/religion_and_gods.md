# Religion, Gods, Cults, Shrines and Ritual Practice of Pre-Islamic Arabia (5th–7th c. CE)

> **Evidence-tier convention used throughout.** Each claim is tagged:
> **[EPI]** = inscription / archaeology (contemporary witness);
> **[QUR]** = the Qurʾān itself;
> **[TRAD]** = later Islamic literary tradition (Ibn al-Kalbī, Ibn Isḥāq, al-Azraqī, Yāqūt, ḥadīth, tafsīr — 8th–13th c. CE, i.e. 150–600 years after the fact);
> **[CLASS]** = Greek/Latin/Syriac outsider sources;
> **[MOD]** = modern scholarly reconstruction or dispute.
> This separation is the single most important thing for world-building: almost everything colourful and narratable about "the idols of the Kaʿba" is **[TRAD]**, and almost everything securely dated is **[EPI]**.

---

## The major deities: cult centres, sanctuaries, custodian tribes, iconography, sources

### Takeaway
Only a small number of the deities named in the later Arabic literary tradition are confirmed by contemporary inscriptions — essentially Allāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt/Manawat, Dushara/Dhū al-Sharā, Ruḍaw, Wadd, and a handful of others — while the great bulk of Ibn al-Kalbī's catalogue (Dhū al-Khalaṣa, Isāf and Nāʾila, Nuhm, Saʿd, Dhū al-Kaffayn, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, Nasr) is attested **only** in Islamic-era texts. In the epigraphic record of the nomads, Allāt is the most-invoked deity and Ruḍaw second; Hubal is essentially absent, appearing in exactly one Nabataean tomb inscription.

### Cited Findings

**Allāh / الله (al-ilāh)**
- [EPI] The Zabad (Zebed) inscription of 512 CE, a Christian martyrion text in Arabic and Aramaic, opens "By the help of al-ilāh," written ʾ-l-ʾ-l-h — i.e. *al-ʾilāh*, "the god," without the long-ā — [Wikipedia, Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah)
- [EPI] Inscriptions from a church ruin at Umm al-Jimāl (northern Jordan) by Arab Christians were read by Enno Littmann as naming Allāh as a proper name; a later reading by Bellamy et al. (1985–88) rejected that interpretation — [Wikipedia, Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah)
- [EPI] The epithet ʾlʾlh (al-ilāh) occurs once in the Ḥimà Paleo-Arabic corpus (Ḥimà-Sud PalAr 8), alongside the epithet mrʾ-lqyš twice — [Wikipedia, Hima Paleo-Arabic inscriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hima_Paleo-Arabic_inscriptions)
- [EPI] Al-Jallad records at least one Safaitic prayer addressed "O Allāh" (inscription zega 1), but it is not analysed in the section surveyed — [Al-Jallad 2022, OA PDF](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [TRAD/MOD] The name's currency is argued from Muḥammad's father's name ʿAbd-Allāh, and from Arab Christian use (Irfan Shahîd, citing *Kitāb al-Aghānī*, reports the battle cry *Yā la-ʿibād Allāh*; al-Marzubānī reports Ghassanid and Tanūkhid Christian poets naming Allāh) — [Wikipedia, Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah)
- [MOD] Etymology: majority view derives Allāh from *al-* + *ilāh*, exactly parallel to Allāt from *al-ʾilāt*; a minority holds it a loan from Syriac *Alāhā*. No iconic representation of Allāh is known — [Wikipedia, Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah)
- [MOD] Al-Azmeh's *The Emergence of Islam in Late Antiquity: Allāh and His People* addresses how the Muslim faith arose and the Arabs' role in it, directing historians toward Hellenism, Late Antiquity and the anthropology of religion — [Al-Usur al-Wusta review](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/alusur/article/view/7046)

**Allāt / اللات**
- [EPI] The most widely invoked deity in the Safaitic corpus. Prayers address her as *f h lt* "O Allāt," asking for security (*slm*), reunion (*qbll*), relief (*rwḥ*), booty (*ġnmt*) and abundance; she is paired with Dushara in several sacrifice texts at Tell al-Rāḥib. Al-Jallad argues *hlt* is a variant (or dittography) of *lt* — the same goddess — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [EPI] Also attested in Nabataean, Palmyrene, Dadanitic, Thamudic and Sabaean inscriptions; cult strongest in north Arabia and the Ḥijāz, weak in South Arabia, reaching eastern Arabia (Gerrha) and Syria — [Wikipedia, Al-Lat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Lat)
- [EPI] A Hegra tomb curse names "[the goddess] Allāt of Amnad" with Dushara and Manotu — [Livius, Nabataean inscriptions](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/nabataean-inscriptions/)
- [EPI] An inscription calls her "the goddess who is in Iram"; at Palmyra she is "Lady of the temple," and her temple held a basin for divination lots. The Palmyrene Lion of al-Lāt pairs a lion with a gazelle — [Wikipedia, Al-Lat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Lat)
- [CLASS] Herodotus (5th c. BCE) names "Alilat," equating her with Aphrodite Urania — [Wikipedia, Al-Lat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Lat)
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī: her sanctuary stood at al-Ṭāʾif, on the site of the later mosque's left minaret; she was **a cubic rock with a building over it**; the custodians were the Banū ʿAttāb ibn Mālik of the Thaqīf; she was venerated by Quraysh and other Arabs; children were named after her; al-Mughīra ibn Shuʿba destroyed her after the Thaqīf converted — [Faris trans., *Book of Idols*](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [TRAD] Thaqīf called her *ar-Rabba*; her precinct was sacred ground where trees, game and human blood were off-limits; Muḥammad ordered the Ṭāʾif shrine demolished c. 630 as a condition of reconciliation — [Wikipedia, Al-Lat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Lat)
- [QUR] Named with al-ʿUzzā and Manāt at Q 53:19–22 — [Wikipedia, Al-Lat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Lat)
- [MOD] Nabataean inscriptions call her Dushara's consort in some places and his mother in others; Healey argues Allāt and al-ʿUzzā began as one goddess, others that al-ʿUzzā was originally an epithet of Allāt — [Wikipedia, Al-Lat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Lat); Alpass treats the composite "Allāt–al-ʿUzzā" as a scholarly construct — [Alpass, *The Religious Life of Nabataea*](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Alpass-P-%E2%80%93-The-Religious-Life-of-Nabataea-.pdf)

**al-ʿUzzā / العزى**
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī: her shrine was in the valley of Ḥurāḍ in Nakhlat al-Shāʾmiyya, near the Mecca–Iraq road; introduced by Ẓālim ibn Asʿad; her custodians were the Banū Shaybān of the Banū Sulaym, the last being Dubayya al-Sulamī; a house called **Buss** covered her, where people received oracular communications; she was the chief goddess of Quraysh; she had a sacred ravine, **Suqām**, and a sacrifice site, **al-Ghabghab**; Khālid ibn al-Walīd cut down her (three) trees and killed Dubayya — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt); [Wikipedia, Al-Uzza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Uzza)
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī's destruction narrative has a wild-haired woman appear at the last tree and be struck down. The page notes some scholars consider this account unreliable, and the only other mention of the destruction is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī — [Wikipedia, Al-Uzza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Uzza)
- [EPI] Cult centre at Petra; her name is taken to mean "the mightiest one"; her betyls at Petra had star-like "eyes"; linked with Aphrodite — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)
- [EPI] Nabataean inscriptions call al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā "brides of Dushara" — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)
- [TRAD] *Kitāb al-Aṣnām* has the longest entry of the whole book on al-ʿUzzā — [Wikipedia, Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam)

**Manāt / Manawat / مناة**
- [EPI] Attested in Nabataean (and Latin) inscriptions. Hegra tomb curses pair her with Dushara: a tomb inscription dated to the ninth year of Aretas (Tebeth = Dec. 1 BCE / Jan. 1 CE) invokes against anyone who sells, buys, pledges or gives away the tomb, and names "[the goddess] Manotu and her Qaysha" alongside Dushara; the penalty clause makes violators liable "to Dushara and Hubalu and to Manotu in the sum of five shamads," plus a fine to the exorcist priest of 1,000 Aretite selaʿs. The mason signs: Wahballāhī son of ʿAbdobodat — [Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/nabataean-inscriptions/)
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī: her sanctuary was on the coast at **al-Mushallal** in Qudayd, between Medina and Mecca; the Aws and Khazraj were her most devoted worshippers (also Hudhayl and Khuzāʿa); sacrifices were offered; pilgrims **shaved their heads** there and did not consider the pilgrimage complete without visiting her. ʿAlī destroyed her on the Prophet's orders (Ibn al-Kalbī); other reports name Saʿd b. Zayd al-Ashhalī with 20 horsemen, which William Muir preferred — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt); [Wikipedia, Manat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manat_(goddess))
- [MOD] Name derived either from *manā* "to mete out, apportion" or from *maniyya* "fate" — goddess of fate/destiny; identified with Nemesis — [Wikipedia, Manat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manat_(goddess)); [Wikipedia, List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)
- [MOD] Invoked for rain and victory in the Nabataean sphere; believed to be the same deity as the Manāt of the Kaʿba — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)
- [TRAD/MOD] Persian court writers claimed Mahmud of Ghazni's 1024 attack on Somnath was motivated by a belief an idol of Manāt had been moved there ("Somnat" garbling *su-manāt*) — a late claim, not established fact — [Wikipedia, Manat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manat_(goddess))

**Hubal / هبل**
- [EPI] Hubal appears in **one** Nabataean inscription — the Hegra tomb-penalty formula quoted above ("to Dushara and Hubalu and to Manotu"). Healey reads it as an injunction to placate Hubal and the others for violating a tomb — [Wikipedia, Hubal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubal); [Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/nabataean-inscriptions/)
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī: Hubal's image stood **inside** the Kaʿba, of **red agate (ʿaqīq)** in human form, with the right hand broken off and replaced by a gold hand by Quraysh; set up by Khuzayma ibn Mudrika; Quraysh kept it; **seven divination arrows** stood before it. Abū Sufyān invoked it after Uḥud — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [TRAD] al-Azraqī calls the material "cornelian pearl," says the image came from Hīt in Mesopotamia, and mentions a sacrificial vault and an offering of a hundred camels. Ibn Isḥāq credits ʿAmr ibn Luḥayy of Khuzāʿa with setting it up — [Wikipedia, Hubal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubal); [Wikipedia, Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba)
- [TRAD] The ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib story: Hubal's arrows selected his son ʿAbdallāh for sacrifice, and 100 camels were offered in his place — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [MOD] Role disputed: Wellhausen made Hubal a son of al-Lāt; Winckler proposed a lunar deity; Eliade and Adams a rain/warrior god; Healey suggests a possible link with Dushara. Name possibly from Baal, or Aramaic for "spirit" (Hitti) — [Wikipedia, Hubal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubal)
- [MOD] Rubin and Robin hypothesise the Kaʿba was first dedicated to Allāh and the Quraysh pantheon installed after they took Mecca c. a century before Muḥammad; Robin argues the Kaʿba rose in prominence only in the late 6th c., after the Quraysh defeat of Abraha; Webb argues it was never a major pan-Arabian pilgrimage site but regional — [Wikipedia, Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba)

**Wadd / ود**
- [EPI] Genuinely well attested. The formula *Wd ʾb*, read "Wadd is father," appears on amulets and buildings in Minaean contexts; a Minaean inscription and a Greek inscription on an altar from Delos are dedicated to Wadd; Minaean inscriptions attest **Levites** in Wadd's temple at Dedan — [Wikipedia, Wadd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadd)
- [EPI] A Temple of Wadd — rectangular, limestone, in the city centre near the palace and a fortified castle — has been excavated at Qaryat al-Fāw in central Arabia — [Wikipedia, Qaryat al-Faw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw)
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī places Wadd at **Dūmat al-Jandal**, adopted by the Kalb — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [TRAD] Khālid ibn al-Walīd's second expedition (April 631) was sent to demolish an idol called Wadd worshipped by the Banū Kalb — [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal)
- [MOD] Depicted as a man; associated with snakes; "love/kindness/affection" are associations of the symbol rather than a stated etymology; Himyar is said to have worshipped Wadd along with ʿAthtar, ʿAmm and Almaqah — [Wikipedia, Wadd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadd); [Wikipedia, Himyarite Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyarite_Kingdom)

**Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, Nasr (the Q 71:23 group)**
- [QUR] Named at Q 71:23 as the gods of Noah's people — [Wikipedia, Wadd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadd)
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī: **Suwāʿ** at Ruhāṭ near Yanbuʿ, adopted by Hudhayl, custodians the Banū Lihyān; **Yaghūth** adopted by Madhḥij and the people of Jurash, cited in poetry; **Yaʿūq** at Khaywān, a village two nights' journey toward Mecca, adopted by the Khaywān, little mentioned in poetry; **Nasr** at Balkhaʿ, worshipped by Ḥimyar, who later embraced Judaism under a Tubbaʿ — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [EPI] **None of Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq or Nasr is epigraphically attested** — the Wikipedia survey states no inscriptions are known for them, they appear only in the Qurʾānic list and its elaborations — [Wikipedia, Wadd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadd)
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī frames all five as the idols of Noah's people inherited by Ishmael's dispersing descendants, who "exchanged the religion of Abraham and Ishmael for another" — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)

**Dushara / Dhū al-Sharā / ذو الشرى (Dusares, dśr)**
- [EPI] Patron deity of the Nabataeans at Petra and Hegra; glossed "Lord of the Mountain"; invoked as "Dushara and all the gods"; protector of the royal house; appears as both betyl and statue; the eagle is his symbol, carved above doorways. Epithets: Dushara-Aʿrā at Bostra, "Lord of the Temple" (possibly at Jabal Ithlib) — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)
- [EPI] A shrine ("Dusari sacrum") in the harbour of Puteoli (Pozzuoli, Italy) is the only known Nabataean temple outside the homeland — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)
- [EPI] At Hegra, Jabal Ithlib is the religious quarter, believed originally dedicated to Dushara; a ~40 m corridor reminiscent of Petra's Sīq leads to the hall of the Dīwān; small rock-cut sanctuaries with inscriptions were cut near Jabal Ithlib — [Wikipedia, Mada'in Saleh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mada%27in_Saleh)
- [EPI] Safaitic inscriptions mention animal sacrifices to Dushara (*dśr*), call him a son of Allāt, and describe him assembling in the heavens with other deities; he is invoked with Allāt in sacrifice texts at Tell al-Rāḥib and with Gadd-ʿAwīdh in aws 279 — [Wikipedia, Dushara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dushara); [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [EPI] Two Dushara-Aʿrā inscriptions dated to Nisan may indicate a spring festival — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī: an idol called **Dhū al-Sharā** belonging to the Banū al-Ḥārith ibn Yashkur of the Azd; a footnote in Faris describes it as a large black unhewn quadrangular stone and links it to Nabataean Dusares with a major sanctuary at "Ettra" — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt); [Wikipedia, Dushara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dushara)
- [CLASS/MOD] Greek Δουσάρης, Latin Dusares; associated with Zeus, Dionysus and Helios. The etymology (Dhū l-Sharā, "the one of the Sharā," i.e. the al-Sharāt range southeast of the Dead Sea) is disputed; if right, Dushara is a title, not a name — [Wikipedia, Dushara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dushara)
- [MOD] Alpass notes Dushara was linked to "Arabia" in sources from the 3rd c. CE onward, and that later Greek and Christian writers gave him Dionysiac mythic parentage — late sources with their own agendas; he mentions Dushara's black unworked block in the *Suda* and idol blocks at Petra — [Alpass](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Alpass-P-%E2%80%93-The-Religious-Life-of-Nabataea-.pdf)

**Dhū al-Khalaṣa / ذو الخلصة**
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī: at **Tabāla**, between Mecca and Ṣanʿāʾ, about seven nights from Mecca; a **carved block of white quartz with a crown-like top**; custodians the Banū Umāma of the Bāhila ibn Aʿṣur; venerated by Khathʿam, Bajīla, Azd and subtribes of Hawāzin; sacrifices offered; sortilege used to decide on blood-revenge, with **three arrows** — "the enjoiner," "the forbidder," "the vigilant." Imruʾ al-Qays drew "the forbidder" three times, broke the arrows and attacked the idol — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [TRAD] Called the "Southern Kaʿba" (*al-kaʿba al-yamāniyya*) to distinguish it from Mecca's; the Bajīla were custodians, the Khathʿam defended it; Jarīr ibn ʿAbdallāh demolished and burned it on Muḥammad's orders, April–May 632 (10 AH) — [Wikipedia, Dhu al-Khalasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhu_al-Khalasa)
- [EPI] **No epigraphic evidence.** The cult is attested only in Islamic sources — ḥadīth and Ibn al-Kalbī — [Wikipedia, Dhu al-Khalasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhu_al-Khalasa)

**Isāf and Nāʾila / إساف ونائلة**
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī (p. 34) and al-Azraqī (pp. 49–50) are the sources. Two stones, called *miskhs* (metamorphosed beings), said to be petrified lovers who committed adultery inside the Kaʿba while on pilgrimage. Originally inside the Kaʿba; later one by the Kaʿba and one by Zamzam. Al-Azraqī credits ʿAmr ibn Luḥayy with promoting their worship and says Quṣayy ibn Kilāb moved them to Zamzam; he also reports that under Jurhum rule they were placed on Ṣafā and Marwa as a warning and then became objects of veneration. Worshipped by Khuzāʿa, Quraysh and pilgrims; sacrificed to together; destroyed after 630 — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt); [Wikipedia, Isaf and Na'ila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaf_and_Na%27ila)
- [EPI] No inscriptions or archaeology attested — [Wikipedia, Isaf and Na'ila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaf_and_Na%27ila)
- [MOD] Aziz al-Azmeh suggests they were original Quraysh deities brought from their earlier territories and worshipped alongside Mecca's other deities — [Wikipedia, Isaf and Na'ila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaf_and_Na%27ila)

**Ruḍā / Ruḍaw / Ruldaiu (رضى)**
- [EPI] Earliest attestation is Neo-Assyrian: the Esarhaddon Prism lists an idol *Ru-ul-da-a-a-u*, read as Ruḍaw. He appears in **Dumaitic, Thamudic B and Safaitic**, and is the **second-most attested deity in Safaitic after Allāt** — [Wikipedia, Ruda (deity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruda_(deity))
- [EPI] In Safaitic he is invoked for booty (a captured she-camel as spoil, ma.1) and in prayers for vengeance — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [EPI/MOD] A recently published Dumaitic inscription invokes him as **"Ruḍaw from Chaldea" (mkśd)**. Al-Jallad (JRAS 31.3, 2021, pp. 559–571) proposes either a pilgrimage link to an Arab community near Nippur or a symbolic association with Mesopotamian power; he argues the title bears on Ruḍaw's standing relative to Allāt, concludes his cult likely died out by the Islamic era, and reads the stories of his cult site's destruction as an instance of the "smashing idols" topos marking the shift from pre-Islamic belief to Islam — [Al-Jallad, JRAS (abstract; not OA)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/on-the-origins-of-the-god-rudaw-and-some-remarks-on-the-preislamic-north-arabian-pantheon/8ED8F88CB6A525C33696175D6E0EFE51)
- [MOD] Earlier scholarship treated Ruḍaw as **female**; recent analysis makes him a masculine, paternal deity. In the "settled oasis astral triad" scheme he is identified with the Moon (not the sun) — [Wikipedia, Ruda (deity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruda_(deity))
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī: Ruḍā had a temple of the Banū Rabīʿa ibn Saʿd ibn Zayd ibn Manāt, destroyed by al-Mustawghir in early Islam — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)

**Nuhā / Nuhay (نهى) and ʿAttarsamain / ʿAttarsamē**
- [EPI] Nuhay and ʿAttarsamē occur together in the Dumaitic inscription **wti 23**; they are not part of the Safaitic repertoire — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [MOD] In the oasis astral triad reconstruction: Nuhay = sun, Ruḍaw = moon (senior), ʿAttarsamē = Venus. Nuhā is described as a sun goddess of the Najd associated with emotions, the North Arabian counterpart of South Arabian Shams. Scholars debate whether ʿAttarsamē and Allāt are distinct regional deities or dialect names for the same astral entity — [Wikipedia, Ruda (deity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruda_(deity)); [Wikipedia, List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)
- [CLASS] Teʾelḫunu, one of five known Qedarite Arab queens in Neo-Assyrian records (with Zabībē, Šamšī, Tabūʿa), held the title of **high priestess of Atarsamain**, a deity of fertility, love and war associated with Ishtar; Dūmah/Adummatu (from 845 BCE) had an important temple to Ishtar — [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal)

**Shams / شمس**
- [EPI/MOD] South Arabian **sun goddess** (Shamsum, Dhāt-Baʿdhānum), patron goddess of the **Himyarite Kingdom**, described as especially favoured as a national and possibly ancestral deity; identified with Mesopotamian Shamash; horses offered as votives; a stele shows her as protective goddess, and a sculpture (probably 1st c.) shows a Sabaean priestess interceding with the sun goddess for a donor; Peter Stein links the "Hymn of Qāniya" to her — [Wikipedia, Shams (deity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shams_(deity)); [Wikipedia, Himyarite Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyarite_Kingdom)
- [EPI] A **Temple of Shams** at Qaryat al-Fāw contains a *mrzḥ* (ritual banqueting institution) — the easternmost epigraphically attested *mrzḥ*, showing Hellenised cult practice reaching central Arabia. Its frescoes (c. 1st c. CE, per Juan de Lara) carry regeneration/afterlife symbolism and the inscription *zky*, probably a benediction ("pious, blessed") — [Wikipedia, Qaryat al-Faw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw)
- [MOD] The Wikipedia Shams article is internally inconsistent (pairing her with Almaqah as moon god in Sabaʾ and with Sayīn in Ḥaḍramawt) and is flagged for reliability — treat its triad framing with caution — [Wikipedia, Shams (deity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shams_(deity))

**Other named cults in Ibn al-Kalbī (all [TRAD]-only)**
- **Nuhm**: in Muzayna territory; custodians the Khuzāʿī ibn ʿAbd-Nuhm of the Banū ʿIdā of Muzayna; sacrifices offered; the custodian destroyed it on hearing of the Prophet and converted — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- **Saʿd**: on the coast near Jedda; Mālik and Milkān, sons of Kināna; **a long rock stained with blood**; camels shied from it and a man threw a stone at it in anger — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- **Dhū al-Kaffayn** ("he of the two palms"): in Daws territory; Banū Munhib ibn Daws; al-Ṭufayl ibn ʿAmr al-Dawsī burned it on the Prophet's orders — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- The Arabic text's further names include **al-Uqayṣir, ʿĀʾim, Saʿīr, Riʾām, al-Fals, ʿUmyānis**, plus an appendix on **al-Yaʿbūb** and **Bājir** — [Arabic text, Wikisource](https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/كتاب_الأصنام)
- **Quzaḥ**: rainbow/weather/mountain god associated with Muzdalifa, with a bow and arrows of hailstones — [Wikipedia, List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)

**Minor deities genuinely in the inscriptions (Safaitic)**
- [EPI] **Baʿal-Samīn (bʿlsmn)**, the storm god: prayers following the narrative formula *t(n)ẓr h-smy* "he awaited the rains" are overwhelmingly addressed to him, asking for rain, relief or abundance; a sacrifice to him is recorded in c 4410 — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [EPI] **The Gadds** (tutelary/fortune deities of groups and places): **Gadd-Ḍayf (gdḍf)** is the most frequent sacrifice recipient at Tell al-Rāḥib (four sacrifice texts, five prayer-only mentions), invoked for protection from misfortune (abws 8) and relief in captivity (ahs 10); **Gadd-ʿAwīdh (gdʿwḏ)** in c 860, aws 279, rq.a 9 — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [EPI] **Shayʿ-ha-Qawm (śʿhqm)**, protector of caravans, "who drinks no wine and builds no home": invoked for protection of camels and provisions (krs 756), safe reunion (absws 11), and in a sacrifice text (al-namārah.m 5814) — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf); [Wikipedia, List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)
- [EPI] **Yayṯeʿ (yṯʿ)**: invoked for security, deliverance from illness, and protection of writings (c 527, krs 818) — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [EPI] **"The Goddess of Dṯn" (ʾlt dṯn)**: recipient of a cult stone in rq.a 9 — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [EPI] **Malkom, Kemosh, Qaws** occur in Thamudic B htham 1; **Ṣalm** and **Dhū Ghaybat** belong to Taymanitic and Dadanitic material — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [EPI] **Ṣalm of Maḥram**: the Tayma stele (found 1883, now in the Louvre) lists the gods worshipped at Taymāʾ in the 6th c. BCE, including Ṣalm of Maḥram and a deity linked to Asherah (reading debated) — [Wikipedia, Tayma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tayma)
- [EPI] **Aʿrā** at Bostra: a cult image stained with blood from sacrifices; equated with Dushara — [Wikipedia, List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)
- [EPI] **Kahl** at Qaryat al-Fāw: patron god of Kinda; the town was called **Qaryat Dhāt Kāhil**; attested in inscriptions, rock engravings and incense burners; five temples found at the site, dedicated to different deities in different periods, including Shams and Wadd; a sacred area of temples and tombs separate from the residential sector; a bust of Artemis was found in the burial area. The city emerged in the 4th c. BCE and flourished 3rd c. BCE – 3rd c. CE; Kinda abandoned it c. 300 CE for Ḥaḍramawt — [Wikipedia, Qaryat al-Faw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw); [Wikipedia, List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)

### Inferences
- The asymmetry is sharp and usable: the deities with real inscriptional lives (Allāt, Ruḍaw, Dushara, Wadd, Baʿal-Samīn, the Gadds, Shayʿ-ha-Qawm) are mostly *not* the ones the Islamic tradition dramatises, and the ones it dramatises most (Hubal, Isāf and Nāʾila, Dhū al-Khalaṣa) are mostly absent from inscriptions. A world-building choice between "the gods people actually wrote prayers to" and "the gods later writers remembered" is therefore a real historiographical fork, not a stylistic one.
- Ibn al-Kalbī's entries follow a recurring template — place, tribal custodian, material of the image, and a destruction episode under Muḥammad — which looks structural rather than archival. Al-Jallad explicitly calls the Akhbār material "largely folkloric," with only some *names* reliably preserving older knowledge.
- The three "daughters" of Q 53 are the only deity cluster where [EPI], [QUR] and [TRAD] genuinely converge; they are the safest ground for a narrative that needs to be defensible.

## South Arabian religion: ʿAthtar, Almaqah, Wadd, ʿAmm, Sayīn, the triad hypothesis, and temple practice

### Takeaway
The "astral triad" (moon-father / sun-mother / Venus-son) proposed by Ditlef Nielsen was the dominant 20th-century framework and has been largely abandoned: Garbini, Pirenne, Beeston, Ryckmans and Robin all attacked it, and the specific claim that Almaqah was a moon god has no epigraphic support. South Arabian religion is better described as a set of national/dynastic patron gods with concrete functions (irrigation, war, the hunt) served by large institutional temples, of which the Awām temple at Maʾrib is the best-documented in the entire peninsula.

### Cited Findings

**The triad hypothesis and its collapse**
- [MOD] Alexander Sima: the lunar nature of Almaqah/Ilmaqah is "speculative" and lacks "any epigraphic evidence." The moon-god label is an artefact of Nielsen's triad hypothesis, which later scholars rejected; Almaqah's inscriptions show no explicit link to sun or moon — [Islamic Awareness, moon-god page](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/quran/sources/allah/moongod)
- [MOD] **Garbini and Pirenne** (1970s): Almaqah's bull's-head, vine and lion's-skin motifs are **solar and Dionysiac**, so Almaqah was a sun god, not a moon god — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/quran/sources/allah/moongod)
- [MOD] **Ryckmans**: called Nielsen's triad "widely contested," noted Garbini and Pirenne's solar reading of the bull and vine, and pointed out that **the sun is feminine in Arabic**, which undercuts the triad's gender logic — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/quran/sources/allah/moongod)
- [MOD] **Beeston**: called Nielsen's reductionism "excessive"; Ilmqh shows "warrior-god" and "Dionysiac vegetation deity" traits with "solar rather than lunar associations"; nothing in the evidence points to lunar qualities for the South Arabian gods — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/quran/sources/allah/moongod)
- [MOD] **Robin**: proposed reading the Ḥaḍramitic deity as **Sayīn** rather than *Sin*, a reading now widely accepted — removing the basis for equating him with the Mesopotamian moon god — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/quran/sources/allah/moongod)

**ʿAthtar / ʿAṯtar (ʿṯtr; Ḥaḍramitic ʿśtrm)**
- [EPI] Masculine. Deity of the planet **Venus as Morning Star**; also presided over thunderstorms and provided rain. Supreme deity who replaced the older Semitic high god ʾIl; always appears **first in deity lists**; had multiple manifestations with their own epithets; rulers held ritual banquets in his honour funded by a tithe — [Wikipedia, ʿAthtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BFA%E1%B9%AFtar)
- [EPI] Epithet **ʿAṯtar-Shāriqān** ("ʿAṯtar of the East"), invoked especially in **curses** as avenger against enemies. *Kirrūm* is a rain-related hypostasis; the Babylonians identified South Arabian Kirrūm (Kinruma) with Ishtar — [Wikipedia, ʿAthtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BFA%E1%B9%AFtar)
- [EPI] A **hunter god**: ritual hunts were held in his honour as fertility rites to bring rain, probably targeting gazelles, which were sacred to him; the Sabaic phrase for the hunt day names *ʿṯtr* directly. A South Arabian stela shows a reclining Nubian ibex and three Arabian oryx heads associated with him — [Wikipedia, ʿAthtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BFA%E1%B9%AFtar)
- [EPI] A southern temple at **Barāqish** (ancient Yathill) with 16 columns and beams in the Minaean style is linked by archaeologists to ʿAthtar; a necropolis with many grave stelae lies nearby — [Wikipedia, Baraqish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baraqish)

**Almaqah (ʾlmqh)**
- [EPI] National god of Sabaʾ; inscriptions call him "Lord of ʾAwwām." Attributes: bull's head, vine, lion's skin; warrior; Britannica describes him as "protector of artificial irrigation"; Breton makes him god of agriculture and irrigation, and a masculine sun god; Ryckmans and Breton make him the male counterpart of the sun goddess Shams. Also attested in Dʿmt and Aksum — [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/quran/sources/allah/moongod); [Wikipedia, List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)

**ʿAmm and Sayīn**
- [EPI] **ʿAmm** was the patron of Qatabān, whose people were "Banū ʿAmm"; associated with lightning bolts; conventionally called a moon god, and described as closer to the people than the more distant ʿAṯtar. **Sayīn (Syn)** was the god of Ḥaḍramawt, with the **eagle** as emblem (read as a solar symbol); possibly moon-linked — [Wikipedia, ʿAthtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BFA%E1%B9%AFtar); [Wikipedia, List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)
- [EPI] **Taʿlab** was a god of the Sumʿay confederation (Hashid, Humlān, Yarsum), with a temple at Riyām, conventionally a moon god, associated with pastures — [Wikipedia, List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)

**Maʾrib: the Awām temple (Maḥram Bilqīs)**
- [EPI] The main Sabaean temple of Almaqah, the **largest known temple complex in South Arabia**, c. 7 km southeast of ancient Maʾrib. Operated from the early 1st millennium BCE **until the 4th century CE**; potsherds date the site back to c. 1500–1200 BCE — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)
- [EPI] **Nearly a thousand inscriptions** found there, described as "the single most important collection of pre-Islamic Arabian inscriptions," key to reconstructing Yemen's political history in the first three centuries CE. The oldest, mid-7th c. BCE, records that Mukarrib Yadaʿil Dharih I walled Awwām — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)
- [EPI] **Pilgrimage**: an annual communal pilgrimage lasting **eight to ten days** in the month **Dhū-Abhay** (around February/March). Rites began at the **Hārūnum temple** and continued along a processional road. Sacrifices to Almaqah took place on the **seventh of Dhū-Abhay**. A **Maʿlaṣ festival**, possibly fertility- or harvest-related, was part of the ceremonies. Scholars have compared the communal rite to the ḥajj and ordinary visits to the ʿumra — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)
- [EPI] **Purity rules**: animals were banned from the temple, with fines paid to the king for violations; pilgrims had to be ritually pure, involving ablutions, temporary abstinence from sex and certain foods, and no weapons — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)
- [EPI] **Oracle by incubation**: the temple served as an oracle, where pilgrims slept in rooms to receive dreams — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)
- [EPI] **Architecture**: oriented to the rising sun (northeast). An eight-pillar propylaeum led to a peristyle hall of c. 42 × 19 m with 32 pillars and 64 false windows. An **oval enclosure c. 757 m long and 13 m high** (original height uncertain) surrounded it. Three gates: main entrance, a northwestern gate for priests, and a cemetery gate. An **alabaster conduit fed water through the hall into a bronze basin for purification**; bronze statues of bulls, horses and humans were attached to the entrance gates — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)
- [EPI] A 7th-c. BCE **cemetery** adjoins the oval sanctuary, with an estimated **20,000 burials in multi-storey tombs of up to four levels** — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)
- [EPI] Partly excavated by the American Foundation for the Study of Man (AFSM) in 1951–52 under Wendell Phillips; discoveries included bronze statues and evidence of ablution rituals at the southern entrance. UNESCO-listed in 2023 as part of "Landmarks of the Ancient Kingdom of Saba, Marib," and listed as endangered — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)

**Sirwāḥ**
- [EPI] An Almaqah sanctuary associated with King Yadaʿil Dharih I, who built a temple to Almaqah c. 700 BCE; a later temple dated to the 7th c. BCE has a monumental pillared entrance and several rooms of wood and stone, a layout called unique for Yemen in that period. One of the longest inscriptions there has 20 lines and describes the conquests of **Karibʾil Watar**. In 2005 the German Archaeological Institute (DAI) salvaged a block c. 7 m long and over 7 tonnes that had fallen from its plinth in the Almaqah sanctuary in an earthquake; it recounts a Sabaean's battles, and **Norbert Nebes called it the most important known inscription from the 1st century BCE** (attributed to Yiṯaʿamar Watar bin Yakrubmalik). Since 2015 the site has been a front line, making excavation nearly impossible — [Wikipedia, Sirwah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirwah)

**Barāqish (Yathill)**
- [EPI] Minaean capital c. 400 BCE, remaining an important religious centre after the capital moved to Qarnawu. Occupied from at least the 10th c. BCE. A southern temple (16 columns) linked to ʿAthtar; a central temple with four columns still standing; a **"Nakraḥ" temple** excavated and studied. Italian archaeologists excavated a temple with its roof intact in 1989–90 and 2003–07, finding **stone tables/altars with bulls' heads at each end**, believed to belong to a healing god's sanctuary. A Sabaean inscription records the rebuilding of the city wall c. 450 BCE. The site was badly damaged by bombing in 2015 — [Wikipedia, Baraqish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baraqish)

**The end of South Arabian polytheism**
- [EPI] The **last dated polytheistic inscription in Maʾrib's Great Temple is from 379–380 CE**. Robin notes that most temples were already deserted in the 3rd–4th centuries — [Robin, in *Jews in Arabia*, Open Book Publishers, ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [MOD] Robin's account of the motives for the religious reform: restore the match between political groups and rites, resist Byzantine pressure, replace temples as tax recipients, and seize temple treasures — [Robin, ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)

### Inferences
- The triad was a *framework* imposed on the data, and its collapse matters for world-building: South Arabian gods should be written as dynastic/territorial patrons with practical portfolios (dams, irrigation, war, hunting), not as a neat astral family.
- The Awām temple's purity rules, incubation oracle, water conduit and processional road give an institutional, priest-mediated, calendar-bound religion that is qualitatively different from the nomadic religion of the Safaitic inscriptions (no priest, sacrifice performed by the worshipper himself). These are two religious worlds in one peninsula.
- The 379/380 CE terminus in the Great Temple is a hard, dateable hinge: South Arabian polytheism is essentially *over* before the 5th–7th c. window of interest, replaced by Judaic monotheism and then Christianity. A 6th-c. story set in Yemen should not feature a functioning Almaqah cult.

## Nabataean religion in the north: Dushara, Allāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manawat; Hegra, Petra, the betyl tradition

### Takeaway
Nabataean religion is best documented by dedications and tomb-curse formulas written by worshippers themselves; its signature material form is the **betyl** (a stone block, often unworked, housing or representing a god) set in rock-cut niches and open-air "high places." The older scholarly habit of reducing the Nabataean pantheon to "a god and a goddess" is now contested: Alpass argues for regionally diverse, lively polytheism rather than one system drifting toward monotheism.

### Cited Findings

**Betyls and aniconism**
- [EPI/MOD] Betyls are stone blocks representing the gods; the term comes from a Greek myth about stones falling from heaven. Gustaf Dalman's classification lists **plain betyls, slabs, rounded and dome-shaped forms, eye betyls, and face stelae**. Scholars debate whether betyls were containers for gods or representations of them; grooves and holes suggest they were stored and transported to shrines. **Niches served as miniature shrines holding betyls** — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)
- [MOD] Alpass notes the aniconic tradition, including Dushara's black unworked block reported in the *Suda*, and idol blocks at Petra; he also notes a relief showing a deity in **both anthropomorphic and aniconic form** — [Alpass](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Alpass-P-%E2%80%93-The-Religious-Life-of-Nabataea-.pdf)
- [EPI/MOD] In Safaitic, the parallel object is the **nṣb** ("cult stone"), erected for a deity, often immediately before a sacrifice (*nṣb w ḏbḥ*), with variants *mṣbt*, *mṣbn*. Al-Jallad reads it as "a channel for the presence of deities during ritual acts" — a **vessel for divine presence** — and argues the deity was more likely represented by an **unworked** stone than by a carved idol. Cognates: Biblical *maṣṣēbâ*, Nabataean *mṣbʾ*, Classical/modern Arabic *manṣib* — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)

**High places and temples**
- [EPI] High places were **open-air shrines on mountaintops**. The Petra high place had a water pool, two altars and a large courtyard. Jabal al-Tannūr's isolated temple was reached by a single steep staircase. Religious regions: the Negev and Ḥijāz, Hawrān, central Jordan, southern Jordan, northwestern Saudi Arabia — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)
- [MOD] Alpass reads the elevated cult sites as part of a long Near Eastern hilltop-worship tradition, **not** as evidence of a solar cult, and treats Strabo's "sun altars" cautiously. Petra's surrounding peaks were topped with high places linked to the town centre by processional ways — [Alpass](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Alpass-P-%E2%80%93-The-Religious-Life-of-Nabataea-.pdf)

**Hegra / Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ**
- [EPI] **131 monumental rock-cut tombs**, many with Nabataean inscriptions on their façades; plates on tomb entrances give information about owners and the religious system. Jabal Ithlib is the religious quarter, with a Sīq-like corridor (~40 m) leading to the Dīwān hall and small rock-cut sanctuaries nearby — [Wikipedia, Mada'in Saleh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mada%27in_Saleh)
- [EPI] Tomb inscriptions name the owners, prohibit selling or mortgaging the tomb, and state fines and curses for violators; curses were formulaic and invoked Dushara and Manotu — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)
- [EPI] A worked example, dated to the ninth year of Aretas, month Tebeth (Dec. 1 BCE / Jan. 1 CE): invokes "Manotu and her Qaysha" with Dushara against anyone who sells, buys, pledges or gives away the tomb or buries a non-family member; penalty "to Dushara and Hubalu and to Manotu in the sum of five shamads," plus a fine of 1,000 Aretite selaʿs to the exorcist priest. "Allāt of Amnad" appears in a Hegra curse — [Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/nabataean-inscriptions/)
- [EPI] A Petra religious regulation on marble, dated 4 Ab, year 37 of Aretas (= 28/29 CE), appears to govern the priest's income (silver, gold, offerings) and to penalise breach of its terms — translation tentative — [Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/nabataean-inscriptions/)
- [EPI] A 1st-c. CE votive to the **deified Obodas** blesses whoever recites it "before the god Obodas"; it contains a short poem in Arabic, described as the oldest known evidence for the language — [Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/nabataean-inscriptions/)

**Sacrifice, priesthood, festivals**
- [EPI/CLASS] Libations (likely wine) and frankincense are the only definite offerings. **Strabo** reports daily offerings to the sun (Dushara). Animal sacrifice was common; camels were sacrificed especially to Dushara, and remains of raptors, goats, rams and dogs have been found. Human sacrifice is not directly attested; Porphyry's account (*De Abstinentia*, 3rd c. CE) of a boy sacrificed annually at Dūmat al-Jandal and buried under an altar is the only reference, and is treated as unsupported — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion); [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal)
- [EPI] A Nabataean **open-air banqueting hall** at Dūmat al-Jandal, used from the late 1st c. BCE to the early 2nd c. CE, was excavated in 2011 — [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal)
- [EPI] Greek/Roman identifications: Dushara → Dionysus, Zeus, Helios; al-ʿUzzā → Aphrodite; al-Lāt → Athena; Baalshamin → Zeus; Qos → Apollo. Isis, Tyche, Eros and Helios also appear in Nabataean contexts — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)
- [EPI] **Qos/Qaws**, an Edomite weather or lightning god associated with Apollo, worshipped at Khirbet al-Tannūr; a stele at Petra is dedicated to him, and a Tannūr inscription names him god of Hurawa — [Wikipedia, Nabataean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_religion)

**Alpass's method and critique**
- [MOD] Alpass organises his study **geographically** — Petra, Hegra, the Negev, the Hawrān, and central sanctuaries (Khirbet al-Tannūr, Khirbet al-Dharīḥ, Dhāt Rās) — defining "Nabataea" politically as the territory under the Nabataean king, roughly late 4th c. BCE to 106 CE — [Alpass](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Alpass-P-%E2%80%93-The-Religious-Life-of-Nabataea-.pdf)
- [MOD] Starcky and Healey organised religion **by deity** and argued for a coherent system, even a drift toward monotheism; Healey proposed the evidence reflects essentially two deities, a "Nabataean God" and a "Nabataean Goddess." **Alpass rejects this**, arguing that merging Dushara, Baalshamin and Qos imposes modern categories, since worshippers dedicated to each name separately, and that the diversity of evidence points to lively polytheism — [Alpass](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Alpass-P-%E2%80%93-The-Religious-Life-of-Nabataea-.pdf)
- [MOD] Alpass holds that inscriptions are the most valuable evidence because they come from worshippers themselves, while literary sources (Diodorus, Strabo, later Christian writers) are limited and shaped by their authors' purposes. On theophoric names (e.g. "servant of Manotu"), he argues family tradition shaped name choice more than live cult did — [Alpass](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Alpass-P-%E2%80%93-The-Religious-Life-of-Nabataea-.pdf)

### Inferences
- Nabataean religion is the one Arabian religious world where contemporary, first-person, **dated** religious texts survive in quantity. For world-building, this is the material to use when a scene needs a document: a tomb curse, a priest's income regulation, a betyl dedication, all with a regnal date.
- The Nabataean kingdom ends in 106 CE, so strictly the Nabataean *state* cult is outside the 5th–7th c. window. But the betyl/high-place tradition, the Dushara and Allāt cults, and the Hegra tombs remained physically present in the landscape, and Dushara and Allāt are both still being invoked in Safaitic and remembered in Ibn al-Kalbī — a continuity worth exploiting.
- Note the Islamic tradition's "Thamūd" at al-Ḥijr: the Qurʾān describes the Thamūd as idol-worshippers punished for rejecting the prophet Ṣāliḥ, which is how the Hegra tombs were read in Islamic times. That re-reading of a visible Nabataean necropolis as a monument to divine punishment is itself an excellent world-building detail — [Wikipedia, Mada'in Saleh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mada%27in_Saleh)

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve a full account of the Hegra tomb-inscription corpus by siglum (the standard reference is Healey's *The Nabataean Tomb Inscriptions of Mada'in Salih*, not available free in this pass). The Hegra conservation project site appeared in search results and may carry per-tomb records: [hegraconservation.com](https://hegraconservation.com/tombs/ign64/).
- The Petra "eye betyls" of al-ʿUzzā and the Dushara-Aʿrā identification at Bostra are reported but I did not find a primary publication reference for either.

## Ritual and practice: ḥajj, ʿumra, ṭawāf, iḥrām, sacred months, nasīʾ, sacrifice, divination, ḥimā, ḥaram

### Takeaway
Virtually everything we are told about pre-Islamic Meccan ritual is **[TRAD]** — reconstructed backwards from Islamic practice by writers explaining what the Prophet reformed. By contrast, the Safaitic inscriptions give us a nomadic ritual vocabulary that is *independent* of that tradition: animal sacrifice (*ḏbḥ*), high places (*ṣmd*), cult stones (*nṣb*), funerary monuments (*nfs*), cairns (*rgm*), and a fixed prayer grammar. Where [EPI] and [TRAD] touch — stone altars and sacrifice — the Qurʾān itself provides a bridge (Q 5:3).

### Cited Findings

**Safaitic rites [EPI]** (all from [Al-Jallad 2022, OA](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf))
- **ḏbḥ, "animal sacrifice"**: about **91 inscriptions** use this verb. **Camels are the only animals named.** **No priest is attested** — the worshipper appears to perform the rite directly. Sacrifice is tied to dangerous activity (raiding, travel, seasonal migration) and to seasonal transitions; the purpose was to get the gods to respond to worshippers.
- **ṣmd, "high place"** (cognate with Classical Arabic *ṣamdun*): the clearest example is **Tell al-Rāḥib**, where 23 of 53 inscriptions record sacrifices to a range of deities. krs 818 names a *ṣmd* where a camel was sacrificed.
- **nṣb, "cult stone"**: erected for a deity, often in the sequence *nṣb w ḏbḥ* ("he erected a cult stone and sacrificed"); variants *mṣbt*, *mṣbn*. rq.a 9 names the "cult stone of the Goddess of Dṯn." It also appears in funerary contexts (brcm 14, krs 929) and "could be a component of at least some mortuary complexes," though "there is yet no direct evidence that sacrifice and feasting were part of Safaitic funerary rituals."
- **nfs(t) / ʾfs, "funerary monument"** (nepheš-stele): **built** (*bny*) for the dead, and formally distinct from the *nṣb*, which is erected and is not used this way. Associated with the Jabal al-Muqallah cairn.
- **rgm, "funerary cairn"**: attested in hch 2 and krs 1307 contexts; the verb *ʾḫḏ* ("took") seems to mean interring someone in an installation.
- **Grief formulas**: *wgm ʿl-* "he grieved for," *bny ʿl-* "he built upon" (brcm 37.3).
- **Prayer grammar**: typically "O [deity], [grant] security (*slm*), reunion (*qbll*), relief (*rwḥ*), booty (*ġnmt*), or abundance." Prayers are **semantically bound to the narrative that precedes them** — after a "longing" formula, the prayer mostly asks for reunion; after *t(n)ẓr h-smy* "he awaited the rains," it mostly asks for rain or relief, addressed to Baʿal-Samīn.
- **Curses**: formulaic, e.g. "blind whoever effaces this writing" (*ʿwr … ʿwr*).
- The book's chapter structure confirms sections on **the Ritual Shelter (incl. Statues), the Pilgrimage (time period; pilgrimage sites), Ritual Purity, Offerings (unspecified; burnt offerings and libations; images), Vows and Oaths, and Sacred Water** — i.e. all of these are attested categories in the Safaitic record, though I could not retrieve their contents (see Gaps).
- **ḥggw** ("to make pilgrimage") appears in **Dadanitic**, not Safaitic; the *ẓll*-rite is Dadanitic, not Safaitic.

**The pre-Islamic ḥajj and ʿumra [TRAD]**
- Stations: **Mount ʿArafāt, Muzdalifa, Minā**, and central Mecca including Ṣafā and Marwa and the Kaʿba. Pilgrims stood in adoration (**wuqūf**) at ʿArafāt and Muzdalifa; animals were sacrificed at Minā — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- **The Ḥilla performed the ḥajj in autumn and the Ṭuls performed the ʿumra in spring.** The **Ḥums** (Quraysh, Kināna, Khuzāʿa and ʿĀmir) did **not** make pilgrimage outside Mecca's ḥaram, which excluded ʿArafāt — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- Ibn al-Kalbī names the ḥajj, the lesser pilgrimage (*al-ʿumra*), the *wuqūf* at ʿArafa and Muzdalifa, sacrifice of she-camels, the *talbiya*, the **tashrīq days** after the tenth of Dhū al-Ḥijja, and the Aws/Khazraj shaving their heads at Manāt only at the end of their pilgrimage — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- **Talbiya** formulas differed by tribe: the Nizār's begins "Here we are, O Lord!" and includes a line to the effect that God has no associate "save one who is thine" — i.e. the tradition itself presents the pagan talbiya as declaring God's unity while attaching other gods to Him. The ʿAkk were led by two black men calling themselves "the two ravens of the ʿAkk," and the ʿAkk responded that they humbled themselves before God as His Yemenite servants, coming for another pilgrimage. The Rabīʿa are characterised by leaving Minā with the first returning group rather than waiting out the tashrīq days — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- **Ṭawāf and dawār**: Arabs circumambulated the Kaʿba; those without a temple set up a stone and circled it the same way, and this was called ***dawār***. Pilgrims carried stones from the Sacred House and erected them elsewhere to circle them. Stones called ***anṣāb*** (betyls) were called ***aṣnām*** or ***awthān*** when they resembled living forms. Sacrifices before these stones were ***ʿatāʾir***, and the sacrificial platform an ***ʿitr***. A traveller might choose the finest of four stones as a god and use the other three to prop his cooking pot — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- Near the Kaʿba stood a betyl later called **Maqām Ibrāhīm**, the **al-Ḥijr** area (possibly reserved for consecrated animals), and the well of **Zamzam** — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- Tradition: the Kaʿba held **360 idols**, possibly one per day of the year, including statues and paintings — images of angels, of Ibrāhīm with divination arrows, and of ʿĪsā and Maryam; Muḥammad reportedly spared the Maryam and Ibrāhīm images when clearing the idols — [Wikipedia, Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba)
- [CLASS] Marinus of Tyre (70–130 CE) described Arabs venerating a "quadrangular stone," likely the Black Stone, suggesting the veneration predated Islam — [Wikipedia, Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba)
- [TRAD] ʿAmr ibn Luḥayy, of the Azd and father of Khuzāʿa, took custody of the Kaʿba by expelling the Jurhumites; falling ill, he was told to bathe at a hot spring at al-Balqāʾ in Syria, saw people worshipping idols there, asked for some, and brought them back to set around the Kaʿba. He is also credited with introducing the *sāʾiba*, *waṣīla*, *baḥīra* and *ḥāmiya* practices — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [MOD] Robin argues Ibn al-Kalbī **exaggerates the spread of ritual stones and statues**; the genealogies in the text are broadly unreliable and do not match epigraphic evidence; only the eight deities named in the Qurʾān are attested in pre-Islamic inscriptions — [Wikipedia, Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam)

**The sacred months (al-ashhur al-ḥurum) and nasīʾ / نسيء**
- [QUR] Q 9:36–37 bans *nasīʾ* ("postponement"); Muḥammad repeated the ban in the Farewell Sermon. The sacred months are **Dhū al-Qaʿda, Dhū al-Ḥijja, Muḥarram** (consecutive) plus **Rajab** alone — [Wikipedia, Nasi'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasi%27)
- [TRAD] The **Banū Kināna** administered *nasīʾ* through a hereditary officer called the **Qalammas** (pl. *qalāmisa*) — [Wikipedia, Nasi'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasi%27)
- [MOD] Two rival interpretations: (a) **purely lunar** — central Arabia used a lunar calendar and *nasīʾ* meant rearranging which months were forbidden within a year, mainly to move the ḥajj and its fairs to a suitable season, not to manipulate the calendar (Ibn Hishām, Ibn Manẓūr, Maḥmūd Effendi 1858; supported by the *Encyclopaedia of Islam* and an early Sabaean inscription); (b) **lunisolar** — c. 200 years before the Hijra the calendar became lunisolar with intercalary months added to keep the pilgrimage in a profitable season, making *nasīʾ* a synonym for *kabīsa* (Abū Maʿshar al-Balkhī first, then al-Masʿūdī, al-Bīrūnī, ʿAbdullāh Yūsuf ʿAlī, and some Western scholars); one explanation holds the Arabs borrowed the idea from the Jewish calendar — [Wikipedia, Nasi'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasi%27)
- [MOD] If *nasīʾ* meant intercalation, dates for early Islamic events (Hijra, Badr, Uḥud, the Trench) could be off by one to three lunar months — [Wikipedia, Nasi'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasi%27)
- [TRAD] Pilgrim fairs in central and northern Arabia were held in months designated violence-free; the **Basl** recognised at least eight calendar months as holy — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)

**Sacrifice and the dedicated animals**
- [TRAD] Animal sacrifice (camels, sheep, cattle) was common, especially among pastoralists; **blood was poured on the altar stone**, and communal feasts often followed. Camel-herding Arabs dedicated beasts to deities by **slitting their ears** and letting them pasture unherded until they died. Human sacrifice, usually of prisoners of war, is described as occasional — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [QUR] Q 5:103 names four categories God never instituted: **baḥīra / بحيرة** (glossed "slit-eared she-camel"), **sāʾiba / سائبة** ("freed animal"), **waṣīla / وصيلة** ("animal born of twins"), **ḥām / حام** ("stallion camel reserved for idols"). Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī describe them as economically harmful and religiously baseless — [Tafsir of Q 5:103](https://quranworld.net/surah/al-maidah/103/tafsir)
- [QUR] Q 5:3 forbids eating animals slaughtered "upon the stone altars" (*al-nuṣub*); Hoyland links this to sacrifice before stone idols. Ibn al-Kalbī describes the *naṣb* as erecting an idol or stone representation; Al-Jallad disputes that translation and favours an unworked stone — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [MOD] Al-Jallad notes that pre-Islamic poems mention **blood at altar stones mainly as a stereotyped topos tied to oath-making**, not as a feast shared with the gods — a caution against reading the poetry as ritual description — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [EPI] At the Awām temple, bronze statues of bulls, horses and humans were attached to the entrance gates; horses were votive offerings to Shams — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam); [Wikipedia, Shams (deity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shams_(deity))

**Divination by arrows (istiqsām bi-l-azlām)**
- [QUR] *al-azlām* (divining arrows) are condemned at Q 5:90 alongside wine and idols (cited in the literature as the Qurʾānic basis; see Gaps re. my inability to fetch the verse page directly).
- [TRAD] Widely attested: a simple form before Dhū al-Khalaṣa and an elaborate form before Hubal. The practice is also attested at **Palmyra** — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [TRAD] **Hubal's seven arrows**: two inscribed *ṣarīḥ* ("pure") and *mulṣaq* ("consociated alien") settled disputed lineage; the third and fourth concerned the dead and marriage. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib used them to pick which son to sacrifice — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [TRAD] **Dhū al-Khalaṣa's three arrows**: "the enjoiner," "the forbidder," "the vigilant" — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [EPI] Allāt's temple at Palmyra held **a basin for divination lots** — [Wikipedia, Al-Lat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Lat)

**The ḥimā and the ḥaram**
- [TRAD/MOD] ***ḥimā***, ***ḥaram*** and (South Arabian) ***maḥram*** were places where living things were inviolable and violence forbidden. Most were **open-air sanctuaries marked by springs or forests**; cities enclosed temples with walls. Stones were taken from the Ḥaram as tokens of reverence; the Quraysh dedicated **Suqām**, a ravine near al-ʿUzzā's shrine, rivalling the Kaʿba's sacred territory — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia); [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [MOD] *Ḥaram* ("sanctuary," from Semitic Ḥ-R-M) denotes an inviolate zone where contending parties could settle disputes peacefully. Some modern scholars distinguish *ḥaram* (chosen by the community) from *ḥimā* (chosen on natural features) — the article notes this may not reflect actual historical practice. Ibn Taymiyya held that only Mecca, Medina and probably the **Wajj valley at Ṭāʾif** could legitimately be called *ḥaram* — [Wikipedia, Haram (site)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haram_(site))
- [TRAD] Allāt's precinct at Ṭāʾif was sacred ground where trees, game and human blood were off-limits — [Wikipedia, Al-Lat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Lat)
- [EPI] South Arabian temple purity practice is the best-documented analogue: animals banned on pain of fines to the king, pilgrims required to perform ablutions, abstain temporarily from sex and certain foods, and carry no weapons — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)

### Inferences
- The "pagan talbiya" that already affirms God's unity is a textbook case of [TRAD] shaped by apologetic need: it lets the tradition present pre-Islamic ritual as a corrupted monotheism rather than a genuinely alien system. Hawting's reading makes this structural, not incidental.
- For a story, the *dawār* detail — any traveller can set up a stone, circle it, and have a god — is the single most evocative ritual item in the corpus, and it is corroborated in substance by the Safaitic *nṣb* practice: a worshipper erects a stone and sacrifices, with no priest. The nomadic religion really was portable and un-institutional.
- The Safaitic prayer grammar (narrative formula + matched petition) is effectively a template for writing authentic-sounding invocations: longing → reunion; awaiting rain → rain or relief; raiding → booty and security; illness → cure; writing → protection of the writing, with the standard blinding-curse against effacers.

### Gaps
- **Iḥrām** as a pre-Islamic institution: I found no source in this pass that attests *iḥrām* (or its garments and prohibitions) for the pre-Islamic period. The Wikipedia ḥajj article defines it only in Islamic terms and does not describe it as pre-Islamic. This should be flagged as weakly evidenced.
- **Naked ṭawāf** by the Ḥilla, and the Ḥums/Ḥilla system in detail (Kister's and Hawting's treatments): not retrieved; the relevant Wikipedia page did not resolve.
- Q 5:90 and Q 22:30 texts not fetched directly; the *azlām* prohibition is reported second-hand.
- Al-Jallad's sections on the Safaitic **pilgrimage** (§2.4), **ritual purity** (§2.5), **ritual shelter and statues** (§2.3), **offerings** (§2.6), **vows and oaths** (§2.7) and **sacred water** (§2.8) exist and are listed in the TOC but could not be retrieved — this is the largest single gap in the ritual section.
- I found **no evidence of divination treated in Al-Jallad's Safaitic material**; the only reference was a passing comparison to South Arabian sacrifice producing a "divine response" through an oracle. Arrow divination appears to be an essentially [TRAD] datum, corroborated only indirectly by the Palmyrene lot-basin.

## Religious specialists and figures: kuhhān, the sādin, the ḥanīfs

### Takeaway
The Islamic tradition describes a rich cast of religious specialists — ecstatic soothsayers, shrine custodians, hereditary intercalators — but there is almost no contemporary corroboration, and the Safaitic record positively suggests the *absence* of a priesthood among the nomads. The ḥanīfs in particular are a literary category: Britannica states flatly that there is "no evidence" of a ḥanīf cult, and the individual ḥanīfs are known only from Muslim sources, some of them visibly legendary.

### Cited Findings

**The kāhin / kāhina (soothsayer) and related offices**
- [TRAD/MOD] The *kāhin* was **not a priest** but a soothsayer whose authority came from ecstatic inspiration by a spirit or jinn; utterances were often in ***sajʿ*** (rhymed prose); consulted about travel, marriage, theft, illness and more — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [TRAD/MOD] Titles of sacred custodians varied by region: ***afkal*** (Ḥijāz), ***kāhin*** (Sinai–Negev), ***kumrā*** (Aramaic areas), with South Arabian ***rs²w***, ***ʾfkl***, ***qyn*** — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [TRAD] Named kāhins: **Shiqq** and **Saṭīḥ**, plus "Ḥāzī Juhayna" (the diviner of Juhayna) and ʿUzzā Salama. Kāhins claimed knowledge of the unseen through jinn, sometimes gained through asceticism or incantations; they resolved disputes and located stolen property — [WikiShia, Soothsaying](https://en.wikishia.net/view/Soothsaying)
- [TRAD] **ʿirāfa vs kahāna**: Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī distinguishes them — the *kāhin* reports on the past, the ***ʿarrāf*** predicts the future; many sources instead define *kahāna* as reporting the future. **Ibn Manẓūr lists divination (*ʿarāfa*) and astrology (*tanjīm*) as branches of *kahāna*** — [WikiShia, Soothsaying](https://en.wikishia.net/view/Soothsaying)
- [QUR/TRAD] Q 52:29 and Q 69:42 address the accusation that Muḥammad was a *kāhin*; some scholars link the charge to the Qurʾān's rhythmic cadence resembling the style soothsayers used. Ḥadīth equates consulting soothsayers with disbelief; jurists unanimously consider it ḥarām — [WikiShia, Soothsaying](https://en.wikishia.net/view/Soothsaying)
- [TRAD] An exorcist-priest figure appears in the **Nabataean** record too: the Hegra tomb-penalty formula assigns a fine of 1,000 Aretite selaʿs "to the exorcist priest" — [Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/nabataean-inscriptions/)
- [EPI] **No priest is attested in the Safaitic sacrifice texts**: the worshipper appears to perform the rite himself — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [EPI] A **Petra marble regulation of 28/29 CE** appears to govern a priest's income in silver, gold and offerings, and to penalise breaches — the clearest contemporary trace of an institutional Arabian priesthood — [Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/nabataean-inscriptions/)
- [EPI] The Awām temple had **a northwestern gate reserved for priests** — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)

**The sādin (shrine custodian)**
- [TRAD] Ibn al-Kalbī's standard entry names the custodian lineage for each shrine: Banū ʿAttāb ibn Mālik of Thaqīf for Allāt; Banū Shaybān of Sulaym (last: Dubayya al-Sulamī) for al-ʿUzzā; Banū Lihyān for Suwāʿ; Banū Umāma of Bāhila for Dhū al-Khalaṣa; the Khuzāʿī ibn ʿAbd-Nuhm of Muzayna for Nuhm; Banū Munhib ibn Daws for Dhū al-Kaffayn; Banū al-Ḥārith ibn Yashkur of Azd for Dhū al-Sharā — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [TRAD] Several Ibn al-Kalbī entries turn on the custodian's own conversion and destruction of his idol (Nuhm's custodian destroyed it after hearing of the Prophet and converted) — a recurring narrative move — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- [TRAD] The **Qalammas** of Kināna held the hereditary office of calendar intercalation — [Wikipedia, Nasi'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasi%27)

**The ḥanīfs / حنفاء**
- [QUR] *ḥanīf* appears about **10 times** and *ḥunafāʾ* **twice**. **Abraham is the only person explicitly identified with the term**; Michael Cook notes it is associated with Abraham but never with Moses or Jesus. The root ḥ-n-f is glossed "to incline, decline" (Lane) or "to turn or bend sideways" (Wehr); Wehr defines *ḥanīf* as "true believer, orthodox." A Syriac root means "to deceive, to turn pagan," and Watt suggests Jews and Christians may have used the term to taunt early Muslims — [Wikipedia, Hanif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanif)
- [MOD] **Britannica states there is "no evidence" of a true ḥanīf cult** in pre-Islamic Arabia, though some individuals rejected the old gods without embracing Judaism or Christianity. Cook says the term's "exact sense is obscure." Ibn Rawandi cites the 5th-c. Greek historian **Sozomen** as a reliable witness that Arabs in northwest Arabia knew of pre-Islamic Abrahamic monotheists, but says it is impossible to know whether this held across the peninsula. Nevo treats the ḥanīf movement as part of a broader pre-Islamic monotheistic trend — [Wikipedia, Hanif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanif)
- [TRAD] **Zayd ibn ʿAmr ibn Nufayl**: one of four friends in Mecca in Ibn Isḥāq's account who pledged to seek *al-ḥanafiyya*; rejected both Judaism and Christianity after questioning Jews and Christians in Syria, where a monk told him a prophet would soon revive Abraham's religion. He rebuked the Quraysh outside the Kaʿba, declaring none of them followed Abraham's religion but himself, and renounced al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā and Hubal in a poem. He avoided carrion, blood and anything slaughtered for an idol. He **rescued baby girls about to be buried alive** and raised them, later offering their fathers the choice of taking them back or leaving them in his care. His half-brother/uncle al-Khaṭṭāb ibn Nufayl reproached him and drove him from Mecca. Bukhārī has two versions of an encounter with Muḥammad at Baldah over meat sacrificed to idols (Vol. 7, Bk 67, no. 407 and Vol. 5, Bk 58, no. 169); **ʿAlī al-Mīlānī questions the authenticity** of the story, and Kister and Guillaume discuss its significance. Before dying he prayed that his son not be prevented from attaining "this good"; his son Saʿīd ibn Zayd became an early convert — [Wikipedia, Zayd ibn Amr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zayd_ibn_Amr)
- [TRAD] **Waraqa ibn Nawfal**: described as a Nestorian priest and a relative of Muḥammad — a third cousin (Peters) or a cousin of Khadīja (Britannica); he died before Muḥammad's prophethood was declared — [Wikipedia, Hanif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanif)
- [TRAD/MOD] **Umayya ibn Abī al-Ṣalt**: b. c. 536, d. c. 626 aged about 90, born at Ṭāʾif, of the **Banū Thaqīf** paternally and (through his mother Ruqayya) descended from Quraysh via Quṣayy ibn Kilāb. Said to have rejected idol worship and visited Jews and Christians, learning a prophet was expected among the Arabs; later tradition says he rejected Muḥammad out of jealousy, and some exegetes make him the referent of Q 7:175. The *Kitāb al-Aghānī* says he claimed prophethood; Ibn Ḥajar says he converted late in life. His Dīwān treats biblical stories, a flood narrative using the word *tannūr*, **Thamūd and the she-camel**, and the Annunciation. **Authenticity**: the earliest compilation dates from the 9th century, c. 250 years after his death; since Nöldeke most scholars have judged the religious poetry forged under Qurʾānic influence, though some recent scholars are more open (Sinai argues for genuine material in the Thamūd verses; Hawting is cautious). Historians consider the biographical material of little historiographical value — [Wikipedia, Umayya ibn Abi as-Salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayya_ibn_Abi_as-Salt)
- [TRAD/MOD] **Quss ibn Sāʿida al-Iyādī**: presented as a pre-Islamic **bishop of Najrān** who lived in the 6th–early 7th c., a preacher, sage and judge; said to have died c. 610 and probably buried at Mount Simon in north Syria. Believed to be the first pre-Islamic figure to believe in one God and in the Resurrection; **al-Shahrastānī lists him with Waraqa ibn Nawfal and Zayd ibn ʿAmr among the first Arab monotheists**. He preached at the market of **ʿUkāẓ** near Mecca, and Islamic tradition says the young Muḥammad heard him. A composer of ***sajʿ*** rather than poetry; one sermon survives, opening "O People! Listen and retain!", and Michael Sells argues its style resembles the short, rhythmic, rhyme-heavy early suras. **He is considered legendary or semi-legendary and is known only from Muslim sources**; accounts of his lifespan conflict wildly (380, 600, 700 years). Some modern historians have speculated he was an Arian — [Wikipedia, Quss Ibn Sa'ida al-Iyadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quss_Ibn_Sa%27ida_al-Iyadi)

**Jinn and lesser spirits**
- [TRAD] Jinn were seen as inspiring soothsayers, poets and philosophers, but also as causing diseases and mental illness; **ghouls** are recorded in poetry and later Muslim collections — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [EPI] **Ginnāyē** in Palmyrene inscriptions were benevolent, comparable to Roman *genii* — the one epigraphic anchor for the jinn concept — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [EPI] Al-Jallad's Chapter 3 includes a section on **"Malignant Magic"** (and on "Sin, Obedience, and Repentance?" with "Forsaken by the Gods") — confirming these are categories in the Safaitic material, though I could not retrieve the contents — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [TRAD] Other prophetic claimants besides the ḥanīfs appear in the tradition: Khālid ibn Sinān, Musaylima, Ṣāf ibn Ṣayyād — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)

### Inferences
- The clean division is: **institutional priesthoods existed where there were institutional temples** (South Arabia, Nabataea, Petra's income regulation, Awām's priests' gate) and **did not among the nomads** (Safaitic: no priest, worshipper sacrifices directly). The *kāhin* belongs to a third category — a freelance ecstatic, not a temple officer — and is the figure most useful dramatically and least secure historically.
- The ḥanīf stories are structurally identical to one another (reject idols → test Judaism and Christianity → find both wanting → learn from a monk that a prophet is coming), which is strong internal evidence that they are a genre rather than a set of independent biographies. For fiction that is not a problem; for a claim about history it is.
- Quss ibn Sāʿida is a double-edged figure: bishop of Najrān in some accounts, pagan sage in others, lifespan of several centuries in yet others. He is best treated as a legend *about* the pre-Islamic religious imagination rather than a person.

### Gaps
- No contemporary (epigraphic) attestation of the term *sādin* or of any Ḥijāzī shrine custodianship. The *afkal* title is reported for the Ḥijāz but I did not retrieve an inscription attesting it.
- Could not verify the Saṭīḥ/Shiqq narratives (their prophecies to the Yemeni kings) in a scholarly source; WikiShia is a confessional encyclopedia and should be used with care.
- No free full text of Toufic Fahd's *Le panthéon de l'Arabie centrale à la veille de l'Hégire* or *La divination arabe* (the standard works on Arabian divination and on the central-Arabian pantheon) was found. Only a bookseller listing surfaced: [Folios Ltd listing](https://foliosltd.com/products/le-pantheon-de-l-arabie-centrale-a-la-veille-de-l-hegire). Likewise I found no free text or summary of **Joseph Henninger**'s work.

## Judaism and Christianity in Arabia

### Takeaway
By the 5th–7th centuries, monotheism was not a fringe phenomenon but the dominant political religion over large parts of Arabia: Ḥimyar was officially Judaic from c. 380, with c. 140–150 inscriptions documenting it; Najrān, al-Ḥīra, the Ghassanid frontier and the whole Gulf littoral were Christian, with bishoprics attested in synod records from 410 onward and monasteries excavated from Sir Bani Yas to Siniyah Island. The Ḥijāz is the least documented region, which is exactly why the "pagan Arabia" picture survives there.

### Cited Findings

**Ḥimyar: the royal conversion and "Raḥmānism"**
- [EPI] **A religious reform occurred c. 380–384 CE.** Royal inscriptions from **January 384** invoke a new deity, **"the Lord of the Sky,"** instead of the ancestral gods. The **last dated polytheistic inscription in Maʾrib's Great Temple is 379–380 CE**. Robin keeps the 380–384 interval but allows an earlier start — [Robin, *Jews in Arabia* ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [EPI] The corpus is **c. 140–150 texts, 380–530 CE**. Royal: Garb Bayt al-Ashwal 2 and RES 3383 (January 384); YM 327; **CIH 540 (456 CE, Maʾrib Dam)**; ẒM 1 (462 CE) — **none states a dogmatic creed**. Officials: Garb Bayt al-Ashwal 1, by the Jew Yehuda Yakkuf, with a **Hebrew graffito**; Ry 508, Ja 1028, Ry 507, Ry 515 (June–July 523, the Najrān period). Princes: Ry 534 + Rayda 1 (433 CE, a *mikrāb*) and Ry 520 (465 CE). Private: ẒM 5+8+10 (432 CE), ẒM 2000 (470 CE), and CIH 543 = ẒM 772 A+B, which invokes **"Lord of the Jews"** — [Robin ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [EPI] **Raḥmānān** (RḤMNN) is first attested c. **420 CE**, alternating with other divine names including **Īlān** and the plural **Aʾlāhān** (Ry 508), paralleling Hebrew *ʾĕlōhîm*. The Qurʾān later uses the same name as **al-Raḥmān** — [Robin ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [EPI] Judaic markers: the word ***mikrāb*** (a Judaic place of worship); the Ḥaṣī cemetery *mikrāb* named **Ṣūrīʾel**; the "Israel" commune; Hebrew and Aramaic loanwords; the **Yoseh son of Awfà stele**; and a Ḥimyarite tomb at **Bet Sheʿarim** — [Robin ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [MOD] Robin: the inscriptions show a **Judaic religion, though not a rabbinic one**. They **never mention resurrection**, though five end with petitions about the authors' lives; Robin cites the Mishnah and Tosefta (which exclude deniers of resurrection from the world to come) to argue the Ḥimyarite form was not rabbinic. His doctrinal interest is in possible **binitarianism** in CIH 543, where Raḥmānān, Israel and the "Lord of the Jews" appear together, and in the title "Lord of the Jews," which he thinks Judaean Jews would not have used — [Robin ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [MOD] **Robin vs Gajda**: Robin argues the Ḥimyarite Judaism was more priestly than rabbinic; **Gajda reads the evidence as rabbinic** — an explicit live dispute — [Wikipedia, Himyarite Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyarite_Kingdom)
- [MOD] Robin notes the shift from von Wissmann's 1964 article to Gajda's 2009 book was driven by inscriptions found after the 1970s; he challenges the Arab-Muslim tradition's portrayal of pre-Islamic Arabia as isolated and polytheistic, and notes it was written in Muslim circles two centuries later — [Robin ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [CLASS] **Constantius II** reportedly sent an embassy in the early 340s to convert the king; **Theophilus the Indian** said Jews at court blocked it — [Robin ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [EPI/MOD] Judaism became the de facto state religion from at least the reign of **Malkīkarib Yuhaʾmin (c. 375–400)**; legend credits **Abū Karib Asʿad (c. 390–420)** as the first Jewish convert, but Judaism was historically introduced under his father Malkīkarib. Sources link the conversion to a wish to distance Ḥimyar from Rome, which had pressed it to adopt Christianity — [Wikipedia, Himyarite Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyarite_Kingdom)

**The Najrān crisis and the Aksumite conquest**
- [EPI/CLASS] **Joseph (Yūsuf Dhū Nuwās)** repressed the Najrān revolt in **523**; he was killed after the Aksumite invasion c. **525**, and Christian Aksum completed the conquest by c. **530** — [Robin ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [MOD] **Robin's key caution**: Christian accounts present the conflict as a religious war and are often quoted uncritically, whereas **the contemporary 523 inscriptions are political and do not clearly mention religion** — [Robin ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [CLASS] Sources on Najrān: the letters of **Simeon of Beth Arsham** (a letter of 524 names two bishops, both called Paul, who died in the massacre, consecrated by **Philoxenus of Mabbug** — implying Miaphysite, non-Chalcedonian Christianity), **Jacob of Serugh's Letter to the Himyarites**, the **Book of the Ḥimyarites**, and the **Martyrdom of Arethas** — [Wikipedia, Christian community of Najran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_community_of_Najran)
- [CLASS/MOD] **Death tolls diverge wildly**: 200 in early accounts to 70,000 in later sources; one tradition gives 12,500 executed despite a promise of safety; another gives about 340 men plus their leader. The event is widely described by modern scholars as political repression rather than purely religious persecution — [Wikipedia, Christian community of Najran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_community_of_Najran); [Wikipedia, Christianity in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia); [Wikipedia, Jewish tribes of Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_tribes_of_Arabia)
- [TRAD] The **Ḥārith ibn Kaʿb** clan built a martyrium known as the **"Kaʿba of Najrān,"** which drew pilgrims for about 40 years; the **Khathʿam** tribe is singled out as making the pilgrimage. Najrān had the Church of the Ascension, the Church of the Holy Martyrs and Arethas, and the Church of the Holy Mother of God; Syriac inscriptions near Najrān show ties to Syriac liturgy — [Wikipedia, Christianity in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia); [Wikipedia, Christian community of Najran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_community_of_Najran)
- [QUR] **Sūra 85** (*aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd*, "the men of the pit") is commonly read as referring to the Najrān martyrs — [Wikipedia, Christian community of Najran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_community_of_Najran)
- [TRAD] A delegation of about 60 Najrānī Christians, including 45 scholars, visited Muḥammad at Medina; he let them pray in his mosque, and after debate the two sides agreed a treaty guaranteeing their lives, religion and property in exchange for jizya — [Wikipedia, Christian community of Najran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_community_of_Najran)

**Ḥimà Paleo-Arabic inscriptions (the best dated Christian epigraphy of the region)**
- [EPI] **25 Paleo-Arabic inscriptions** from **Ḥimà, c. 90 km north of Najrān**, published in 2014 alongside 14 Sabaic inscriptions — the first Paleo-Arabic inscriptions found in Saudi Arabia (the script was previously known only from Syria). Sigla: **Ḥimà-Sud PalAr 1–12, Ḥimà-Idhbāḥ PalAr 1–7, Ḥimà-al-Musammāt PalAr 1–6** — [Wikipedia, Hima Paleo-Arabic inscriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hima_Paleo-Arabic_inscriptions)
- [EPI] **Ḥimà-Sud PalAr 1, dated 470 CE, is the earliest precisely dated Paleo-Arabic inscription**; another group is dated **513 CE**; the dates use the **Bostran era** (from 106 CE). Several are explicitly Christian, with large ornate crosses; **Ḥimà-al-Musammāt PalAr 5 includes "servant of Christ" (ʿbd-ʾlmšyḥ)**. Names include Himyarite names and names from Old Testament figures (Isaac, Moses). Recurring epithets: *ʾlʾlh* (once, Ḥimà-Sud PalAr 8) and *mrʾ-lqyš* (twice, Ḥimà-Sud PalAr 5 and 8). Many follow "X son of Y … wrote" patterns. Published by **Christian Robin, ʿAlī Ibrāhīm al-Ghabbān and Saʿīd Fāyiz al-Saʿīd** (2014), with **French-only translations** — [Wikipedia, Hima Paleo-Arabic inscriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hima_Paleo-Arabic_inscriptions)
- [MOD] The choice of Paleo-Arabic script may have been a deliberate move to link Najrān-area Christians with co-religionists in the north, in contrast to the Himyarite script; the shared script across southern Arabia, northern Arabia and Arabic-speaking southern Syria, while Aramaic declined, suggests cultural unification among Arabs in the 5th–6th centuries — [Wikipedia, Hima Paleo-Arabic inscriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hima_Paleo-Arabic_inscriptions)

**Aksumite/Ethiopian Christianity in Yemen**
- [EPI/MOD] After Aksum conquered Ḥimyar in the 520s, Kaleb built churches, and his successor **Abraha** built and consecrated many more, including a church at Maʾrib; **Abraha celebrated the Maʾrib dam's completion with a mass**. Later Islamic sources credit him with a church at Ṣanʿāʾ. Abraha broke from Ethiopian church ties and **adopted Syriac terminology**; his inscriptions reference the Messiah and take a relatively **low Christological line**, possibly to placate the Jewish population. Ḥimyar was annexed by the Sasanians in 575 or 578 — [Wikipedia, Christianity in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia); [Wikipedia, Himyarite Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyarite_Kingdom)

**Al-Ḥīra and the ʿIbād**
- [TRAD/MOD] Christianity reached al-Ḥīra in the 4th century; **the city had a bishop by about 410 CE** and many churches and convents; it was a base for missions into both the Sasanian world and the Arabian desert. **Hishām ibn al-Kalbī wrote about its monasteries** — the most famous being the **Monasteries of Hind the Elder and Hind the Younger**, both built in the second half of the 6th century. **Hind**, a Kindite princess who married **al-Mundhir III**, converted and sponsored a monastery in the capital, though her husband did not convert. **Imruʾ al-Qays I** was the first Lakhmid king to convert; the last king, **al-Nuʿmān III**, converted late in the 6th century, with **Simeon Jabara, bishop of al-Ḥīra**, instrumental — the Persians disapproved and tried to make him revert — [Wikipedia, Al-Hirah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hirah); [Wikipedia, Christianity in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [MOD] Al-Ḥīra was a centre of literacy: children from nearby villages came to learn reading and writing; the **local Hīrī script, derived from Aramaic, is considered a precursor to the Arabic script**; poets such as al-Muraqqish al-Akbar studied there, and al-Nābigha and Ḥassān ibn Thābit appeared at the Lakhmid court — [Wikipedia, Al-Hirah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hirah)
- [MOD] The Lakhmids were Sasanian clients and adopted **Nestorian** (Church of the East) Christianity; the Ghassanids were Roman clients and adopted **Miaphysitism** — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)

**The Ghassanids**
- [MOD] Rome's most powerful Arab client kingdom in the 6th century, Miaphysite in doctrine; they sponsored churches and monasteries in Syria and **the cult of St Sergius**, and helped appoint bishops. A conference they convened in **569–570 produced the Letter of the Archimandrites, signed by representatives of 137 monasteries** — [Wikipedia, Christianity in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)

**Monks, hermits and holy men**
- [CLASS/MOD] Christian holy men — **Saint Moses, Hilarion, Euthymius the Great, Simeon Stylites** — were credited with converting Arab groups. **Hagiographies follow a recurring pattern: an Arab community meets a holy man, renounces its idols, and builds a church** — [Wikipedia, Christianity in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [TRAD] Islamic sources describe monks in Midian and Wādī al-Qurā, converts in Mecca and Medina, and an icon of Mary in the Kaʿba — [Wikipedia, Christianity in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)

**The Gulf and eastern Arabia**
- [EPI/MOD] The Church of the East organised dioceses in **Beth Qaṭrāyē** (parts of modern Qatar, Bahrain and the UAE), subordinate to Persian metropolitan authority. **Synods from 410 CE onward record bishops** at **Mashmahig, Dārayn (Tarout Island), Hajar, and Haṭṭā**. Oman had the diocese of **Beth Mazūnāyē**. Archaeological sites: **churches at Jubail and Failaka; monasteries at Sir Bani Yas and Siniyah Island, and on Kharg Island** — [Wikipedia, Christianity in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)

**Jewish communities of the Ḥijāz and north**
- [TRAD] Three main Jewish tribes at **Yathrib/Medina**: **Banū Qaynuqāʿ, Banū al-Naḍīr, Banū Qurayẓa** (the Qurayẓa and the Naḍīr both sub-clans of al-Kāhinān). The Constitution of Medina (622) gave Jews parity with Muslims in exchange for political loyalty and allowed them their own religion. **Rabbi Mukhayrīq** of the Naḍīr fought alongside Muslims at Uḥud and bequeathed his wealth to Muḥammad, who reportedly called him "the best of the Jews." Relations later broke down: the Qaynuqāʿ were exiled to Derʿa in Syria, the Naḍīr evicted, and the adult males of the Qurayẓa executed — [Wikipedia, Jewish tribes of Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_tribes_of_Arabia)
- [TRAD/MOD] Jewish tribes were based along the western coast; Jewish communities also existed at **Khaybar, Taymāʾ, Fadak, al-ʿUlā and Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ**; some sources count twenty Jewish tribes in the peninsula. In al-ʿUlā the shift to Jewish settlement appears to have occurred in the **5th century**. Arab tribes sometimes adopted Judaism in order to settle in Jewish towns, and Jewish converts were treated as Jews — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [TRAD/MOD] **The Banū Qurayẓa and Banū al-Naḍīr are said to have collected tribute from the Aws and Khazraj on behalf of the Sasanians** — [Wikipedia, Pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [EPI/TRAD] At **Taymāʾ**: a **3rd-c. CE inscription names a governor with a Jewish name**; pre-Islamic poetry calls Taymāʾ a city of the Jews; Islamic tradition says the Jewish population was exiled under ʿUmar; Benjamin of Tudela reported a Jewish prince governing the city c. 1170. The Jewish poet **al-Samawʾal ibn ʿĀdiyāʾ** is famously linked with Taymāʾ, and **Qaṣr al-Ablaq** is attributed to him and his grandfather — [Wikipedia, Tayma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tayma)
- [MOD] Origins are debated: one account traces Jewish presence to the early 6th c. BCE after the Babylonian conquest of Judah, in six waves (post-587 BCE, the Roman conquest, the 66–70 CE revolt, the Bar Kokhba revolt); Yemeni Jewish traditions claim ancestry from settlers who arrived before the destruction of the First or Second Temple. Moshe Gil and Norman Stillman are the cited authorities — [Wikipedia, Jewish tribes of Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_tribes_of_Arabia)

**Christianity in the Ḥijāz and Dūmat al-Jandal**
- [EPI] Western Arabia is poorly represented in standard Christian sources, but **inscriptions near Mecca, Medina and Hegra use biblical language**, and some **Greek Christian inscriptions** have been found near Hegra. Irfan Shahid and Gabriel Said Reynolds argue the Qurʾān reflects a significant Christian presence in the Ḥijāz — [Wikipedia, Christianity in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [EPI] **A Paleo-Arabic Christian graffito dated AD 548/9 was found near al-Jawf** (Dūmat al-Jandal); Khālid's expedition there targeted the city's "Christian prince," and the ʿUmar Mosque's construction appears to date from an earlier period as a church — [Wikipedia, Dumat al-Jandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumat_al-Jandal)

### Inferences
- For a 5th–7th c. setting, the demographically and politically realistic religious map is: Judaic Ḥimyar (to 525) then Christian Ḥimyar; Christian Najrān, al-Ḥīra, Ghassanid Syria and the whole Gulf; Jewish oases in the northern Ḥijāz; and the inland Ḥijāz as the one region where traditional cult is best attested — partly because it is the one region whose religious history was written by the people who replaced it.
- The Ḥimà inscriptions are extraordinarily useful: dated 470 and 513 CE, Christian, in the ancestor of the Arabic script, from 90 km north of Najrān. A story needing a *literate, Arabic-writing, Christian* Arabian within living memory of Muḥammad has hard evidence for one.
- Robin's observation that the contemporary 523 inscriptions are *political* while the Syriac accounts are *religious* is a model for handling all of this material: the surviving monotheist narratives are martyrologies, and martyrologies have a shape.

### Gaps
- No archaeology of the Najrān churches at al-Ukhdūd retrieved; the Wikipedia article explicitly does not cover it.
- The "desert monastery lamp" image in pre-Islamic poetry (the lamp of a hermit's cell as a simile for a beloved's face or a distant fire, e.g. in Imruʾ al-Qays and the Muʿallaqāt tradition) — **I found no source for this in this pass.** It is a well-known topos but I will not assert it without a citation. The report-writer should source it from a study of jāhilī simile or from the Muʿallaqāt themselves.
- Could not retrieve the Syriac *Book of the Ḥimyarites* text or a scholarly edition.

## Afterlife, death beliefs and fate

### Takeaway
The nomadic poetry and the Safaitic inscriptions show an elaborate concern with grief, commemoration, burial and fate, but very little developed eschatology — Robin notes the Ḥimyarite Judaic inscriptions never mention resurrection either, and the Qurʾān represents its opponents as denying bodily resurrection. The colourful items (the hāma bird of the unavenged dead, the balīya camel tethered at the grave) come from late lexicographers and from the ḥadīth that denies them, not from contemporary evidence.

### Cited Findings

**Safaitic funerary practice [EPI]** (all [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf))
- ***nfs(t) / ʾfs***, the funerary monument (nepheš-stele), is **built** (*bny*) for the dead — distinct from the *nṣb*, which is erected for deities and is not used funerarily. Al-Jallad stops short of calling the *nṣb* a funerary stele, though it "could be a component of at least some mortuary complexes" (brcm 14, krs 929).
- ***rgm***, the funerary cairn (hch 2, krs 1307 contexts); ***ʾḫḏ*** "took" appears to mean interring someone in an installation. The **Jabal al-Muqallah cairn** is associated with the funerary ritual.
- **Grief formulas**: *wgm ʿl-* "he grieved for," and *bny ʿl-* "he built upon" (brcm 37.3). Allāt is invoked in a funerary curse (krs 941).
- "**There is yet no direct evidence that sacrifice and feasting were part of Safaitic funerary rituals.**"
- The book has a dedicated **Chapter 4: Fate** (p. 73) and **Chapter 5: Afterlife** (p. 78), with sections "Burial Installations" and "**Invoking the Names of the Dead**" — confirming both are attested categories; contents not retrievable (see Gaps).

**The hāma / هامة and the ṣadā**
- [TRAD/MOD] Some held that a bird named **hāma** formed from the blood near a dead person's brain and "once in a hundred years visited the sepulchre"; others that the bird was the **soul of someone unjustly killed**, crying *isqūnī* ("give me to drink") until the killer's blood was shed in vengeance. George Sale notes the Qurʾān expressly forbade this belief — [George Sale, *Preliminary Discourse*, via quran-archive.org](https://quran-archive.org/explorer/george-sale/1734?page=40)
- [TRAD/MOD] The cry is rendered "Iskoonee" = "give me to drink" (Hulme 1886); Lane (1843) gives both the blood-origin and the soul-origin versions. Al-Rawi (2009) describes a **worm** leaving the skull of an unavenged man, circling the tomb and asking to be watered, and notes that Jews in Arabia thought it circled the tomb for seven days. Pre-Islamic Arabs read a predatory bird or owl dying near a house as an omen of a resident's death. A Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim ḥadīth has martyrs' souls living in green birds in Paradise, which the page notes sits oddly with Muḥammad's reported rejection of the hāma belief — [EsoterX summary of Lane, Hulme, Al-Rawi, Roque](https://esoterx.com/2013/05/05/the-theriomorphic-arabian-hamah-an-angry-bird-before-it-was-cool/)
- [TRAD] The belief is denied in a tradition in Sunan Abī Dāwūd's *Kitāb al-Kahāna wa-l-Taṭayyur* (hadith 3915) — [Sunan Abi Dawud 3915](https://parallelquran.com/hadith/sunan-abi-dawud-3915/)

**The balīya camel at the grave**
- [TRAD] Some pagan Arabs **tied a dead person's camel by the sepulchre and left it to die**, so the deceased would not have to "go on foot" at the resurrection — [George Sale, via quran-archive.org](https://quran-archive.org/explorer/george-sale/1734?page=40)

**Fate**
- [TRAD/MOD] Nomadic belief is reported not to have addressed the afterlife in depth. **Fatalism (*qadar*) combined with astral interpretation** shaped many lives, and the goddess **Manāt** was associated with destiny — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)
- [MOD] Manāt's name derives from *manā* "to apportion" or *maniyya* "fate" — the lexical core of the fate vocabulary — [Wikipedia, Manat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manat_(goddess))
- [QUR] Chapter 1 of Crone's collected studies states that the *mushrikūn* **often rejected bodily resurrection, sometimes the afterlife altogether, and denied that judgement was imminent** — [Crone, *The Qurʾānic Pagans and Related Matters*](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/Library/Crone,%20P%20-%20The%20Qur%ca%be%c4%81nic%20Pagans%20and%20Related%20Matters.pdf)
- [EPI] Robin: the c. 140–150 Ḥimyarite Judaic inscriptions **never mention resurrection**, although five end with petitions about the authors' lives — which he reads as evidence the Ḥimyarite form of Judaism was not rabbinic — [Robin ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- [EPI] Funerary religion at **Qaryat al-Fāw**: frescoes in the *mrzḥ* reflect a funerary banqueting function, with symbols linked to regeneration and the afterlife — the clearest archaeological hint of an afterlife concept in central Arabia — [Wikipedia, Qaryat al-Faw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw)
- [EPI] Scale of South Arabian burial: the Awām cemetery held an estimated **20,000 burials in multi-storey tombs of up to four levels**, adjoining the holiest part of the sanctuary — [Wikipedia, Temple of Awwam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Awwam)

### Inferences
- The absence of resurrection talk is attested across three independent bodies of evidence — Safaitic, the Ḥimyarite Judaic inscriptions, and the Qurʾān's characterisation of its opponents — which makes "no developed afterlife" a reasonably well-founded generalisation rather than an argument from silence in one corpus.
- "Invoking the Names of the Dead" as a *section heading* in Al-Jallad, together with the *wgm ʿl-* grief formula and the thousands of graffiti that simply record a name, suggests the real Arabian technology of survival after death was **being named and remembered in writing**, not an afterlife. That is a strong, defensible, and dramatically rich reading.
- The hāma/balīya material should be used as *what people said others believed* — Sale is an 18th-century compiler, Lane a 19th-century lexicographer, and the main ḥadīth attestation is a denial. For fiction this is fine and the images are excellent; as history it is thin.

### Gaps
- **Al-Jallad's Chapter 4 (Fate) and Chapter 5 (Afterlife) could not be retrieved.** These are the only contemporary-evidence treatments of these questions and would be the most valuable additions. Retrieve pp. 73–83 of the OA PDF directly.
- The vocabulary *dahr* (دهر, time/fate) and *manāyā* (منايا, dooms) in jāhilī poetry: I found **no reliable source** in this pass. Searches surfaced only an Arabic-language journal article ([Jordan Journal of Islamic Studies](https://jjis.aabu.edu.jo/index.php/jjis/article/view/126)) that I did not verify, and Izutsu's *God and Man in the Qur'an* (the standard English treatment of the jāhilī fate vocabulary) was not found free. These terms should be sourced before the report asserts anything about them.

## Key sources and their reliability

### Takeaway
Ibn al-Kalbī's *Kitāb al-Aṣnām* is the single richest text on Arabian idols and also the least reliable: a composite work surviving in **one manuscript**, written ~150+ years after the events, structured as a decline-from-Abraham narrative, with genealogies that do not match the inscriptions. Faris's 1952 English translation is freely available on archive.org; the Arabic text is free on Shamela and Arabic Wikisource; Al-Jallad's 2022 book is fully open access; Hawting's and Crone's and Alpass's books are available as scanned PDFs on almuslih.org.

### Cited Findings

**Ibn al-Kalbī, *Kitāb al-Aṣnām* / كتاب الأصنام**
- Author: **Hishām ibn al-Kalbī (737–819 CE / d. 204 AH)**. **Now seen as a composite work**, not solely his: the core is thought to go back to him, transmitted through **Abū Bakr al-Jawharī (d. 944–45)**, and it grew over centuries through additions and an appendix — [Wikipedia, Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam)
- Form: an **itemised list of short entries** on idols and sanctuaries, usually giving location and tribe, often with a destruction notice from the Islamic era. The longest entry is al-ʿUzzā. It contains **repetitions and contradictory accounts**, cites pre-Islamic poetry and (rarely) the Qurʾān, and **presents Arabian polytheism as a decline from Abraham's monotheism, restored by Islam** — [Wikipedia, Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam)
- Manuscript: **the sole known manuscript** was bought at auction in Damascus by **Ahmad Zaki Pasha**, who announced the find at the XIVth International Congress of Orientalists; after his death in 1934 it was donated to the state. **Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī (d. 1229) quoted the text at length, and Wellhausen used those quotations before the manuscript was found** — [Wikipedia, Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam)
- Editions: **R. Klinke-Rosenberger, Leipzig 1941**; **Ahmad Zaki, Cairo, 3rd ed. 1995** (Shamela carries the 4th ed., 2000, Dār al-Kutub al-Miṣriyya, 64 pp.) — [Wikipedia, Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam); [Shamela, book 6513](https://shamela.ws/book/6513)
- Translation: **Nabih Amin Faris, English, Princeton 1952** — [Wikipedia, Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam)
- Reliability: **"Many historians do not consider the text reliable."** Hawting: accusations of idolatry were rhetorical weapons against rival monotheists, and the text is "a collection of characteristic monotheistic traditions and ideas adapted to reflect Muslim concerns." Its genealogies are broadly unreliable and do not match epigraphic evidence. Archaeology conflicts with it: **only the eight deities named in the Qurʾān are attested in pre-Islamic inscriptions**, and **Robin argues the text exaggerates the spread of ritual stones and statues** — [Wikipedia, Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam)
- Al-Jallad treats the Akhbār material as **largely folkloric, with only some names reliably preserving older knowledge** — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- [TRAD] The text's own *isnād* traces transmission through several scholars to ʿAlī ibn al-Ṣabbāḥ, who said he studied under Hishām ibn al-Kalbī in **201 AH (816–817 CE)**; Hishām says he checked his sources' reports personally — [Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)
- F.E. Peters describes it as the most substantial treatment of pre-Islamic religious practice; later scholarship doubts its reliability (e.g. on Manichaeism in Mecca) — [Wikipedia, Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)

**Modern scholarship: positions and disputes**
- **Wellhausen** (*Reste arabischen Heidentums*, 1897): the foundational collection, assembled from Yāqūt's quotations of Ibn al-Kalbī **before the Aṣnām manuscript was found** — i.e. built on an indirect transmission of a text we now know to be composite. He made Hubal a son of al-Lāt — [Wikipedia, Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam); [Wikipedia, Hubal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubal)
- **Hawting** (*The Idea of Idolatry and the Emergence of Islam: From Polemic to History*, CUP 1999): the Qurʾānic *mushrikūn* were **monotheists** whom the Qurʾān attacked polemically as idolaters; later Muslim tradition read that polemic literally and identified them as Meccan Arab polytheists. "**The identification of the mushrikūn as pre-Islamic idolatrous Arabs is dependent upon Muslim tradition and is not made by the Koran itself.**" He treats the traditional descriptions of pre-Islamic religion as **stereotypical and formulaic**, reflecting how early Muslims understood their origins rather than reliable information; Qurʾānic passages were fleshed out with stories (e.g. the idol ʿUmyānis) possibly generated to explain verses. He dates the *Kitāb al-Aṣnām* to 206/821, significantly later than the texts it purports to explain. He does **not** claim to know Mecca's real religious situation; his stated aim is to replace some accepted "facts" with questions and accept more uncertainty — [Hawting, *The Idea of Idolatry*](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/The-Idea-of-Idolatry-by-G.-R.-Hawting.pdf); [Wikipedia, Mushrikun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mushrikun)
- **Crone** ("The Religion of the Qurʾānic Pagans: God and the Lesser Deities," BSOAS; in *The Qurʾānic Pagans and Related Matters*): she rests her work **on the Qurʾān alone**, was sceptical of "almost everything the tradition told" her, and argues the Qurʾān itself should be read as evidence rather than only through exegesis and ḥadīth. She describes the *mushrikūn* as believing in God while ascribing "associates" to Him, which she identifies with lesser divine beings she treats as **demonic**; they often rejected bodily resurrection, sometimes the afterlife altogether, and denied imminent judgement. She calls **Hawting's view of the opponents as monotheists "utterly implausible"** and holds that Islam arose in a pagan environment; elsewhere she writes that it is hard to avoid the impression that Jews and Judaizing pagans are involved — [Crone](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/Library/Crone,%20P%20-%20The%20Qur%ca%be%c4%81nic%20Pagans%20and%20Related%20Matters.pdf); [Wikipedia, Mushrikun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mushrikun)
- **Nicolai Sinai** (as reported): the *mushrikūn* recognised Allāh as creator of the world and credited him with ultimate dominion over the cosmos; they made animal sacrifices, but he questions whether idol veneration was central, though the Qurʾān depicts it that way — [Wikipedia, Mushrikun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mushrikun)
- **Robin**: exaggeration of ritual stones and statues in Ibn al-Kalbī; the Sayīn reading displacing "Sin"; the Kaʿba's rise only in the late 6th c.; Ḥimyarite Judaism as priestly rather than rabbinic; the warning that Christian narratives of 523 are religious while the contemporary inscriptions are political — [Wikipedia, Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam); [Islamic Awareness](https://www.islamic-awareness.org/quran/sources/allah/moongod); [Wikipedia, Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba); [Robin ch. 7](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0219/ch7.xhtml)
- **Al-Azmeh** (*The Emergence of Islam in Late Antiquity: Allāh and His People*, CUP): addresses how the Muslim faith arose and the Arabs' role; directs historians toward Hellenism, Late Antiquity and the anthropology of religion. He suggests Isāf and Nāʾila were probably original Quraysh deities brought from their earlier territories — [Al-Usur al-Wusta review](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/alusur/article/view/7046); [Wikipedia, Isaf and Na'ila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaf_and_Na%27ila)
- **Al-Jallad's method** (the most important methodological statement for this topic): Safaitic inscriptions are treated as **eyewitness evidence** of nomadic religion, in contrast to Islamic-period narratives, which he views as polemical and filtered. Prayers are semantically tied to the narratives preceding them. The corpus shows a fairly homogeneous *writing* tradition but **not a single unified community**; he cautions that not every recorded practice was necessarily shared by all groups, and that the inscriptions may not capture all religious practices — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)

### Inferences
- The scholarly field divides on one axis: whether the Qurʾān's opponents were real polytheists (Crone, and in a different way Sinai) or rival monotheists misdescribed by polemic (Hawting). Both agree the *Islamic literary tradition* is not a safe guide. For world-building this means: the "360 idols" Mecca is a legitimate *fictional* setting with deep roots in the tradition, but it should not be presented as the scholarly consensus about the 6th century.
- The *Kitāb al-Aṣnām*'s one-manuscript, composite, Yāqūt-mediated transmission history means Wellhausen's foundational synthesis — which shaped a century of handbooks and encyclopedias — was built on an indirect text. Many "facts" about Arabian idols in general reference works trace back through this single chain.
- Al-Jallad's open-access book is the single most valuable free resource for this topic and the one to lean on hardest, precisely because it is contemporary-evidence-based, explicitly methodologically cautious, and freely downloadable.

## What the Safaitic and Ancient North Arabian inscriptions tell us directly

### Takeaway
The ANA corpora give us something no literary source does: tens of thousands of first-person religious statements by nomads, undated but pre-Islamic, with their own ritual vocabulary, their own pantheon, and their own prayer grammar — and that picture does not match the later literary tradition's picture of Arabian idolatry. Allāt and Ruḍaw dominate; Hubal, the Q 71:23 group, and most of Ibn al-Kalbī's catalogue are absent.

### Cited Findings
- **Scale and date**: about **30,000 Safaitic inscriptions** recorded, with possibly many hundreds of thousands undiscovered. The script is dated **3rd c. BCE – 4th c. CE**. Named after **al-Ṣafā**, the basalt desert southeast of Damascus, where they were first found in **1857**. The largest concentration is in the **Ḥarrat al-Shām**, extending through southern Syria, Jordan and northwestern Saudi Arabia; isolated examples at Palmyra, Wādī Ḥawrān in western Iraq, and Ḥāʾil — [Wikipedia, Safaitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safaitic)
- **Content**: mostly graffiti on the author's own concerns — grazing for camels, mourning a deceased person, recording genealogy; others mention raids and seek booty, or refer to religious practices. **A few are by female authors.** Some accompany rock art showing hunting or battle scenes, camels and horses with riders, camp scenes, or female figures — [Wikipedia, Safaitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safaitic)
- **Deities invoked** (see the deity section above for detail): **Allāt** (most widely invoked), **Ruḍaw** (second-most attested), **Baʿal-Samīn** (storm/rain), **Dushara**, **Gadd-Ḍayf** and **Gadd-ʿAwīdh** (tutelary "fortune" deities, Gadd-Ḍayf being the most frequent sacrifice recipient at Tell al-Rāḥib), **Shayʿ-ha-Qawm** (caravan protector), **Yayṯeʿ**, and "**the Goddess of Dṯn**"; one attested prayer to **Allāh** (zega 1) — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf); [Wikipedia, Ruda (deity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruda_(deity))
- **Ritual vocabulary attested directly**: *ḏbḥ* (sacrifice, ~91 texts, camels only, no priest), *ṣmd* (high place; Tell al-Rāḥib, with 23 of 53 inscriptions recording sacrifices), *nṣb* (cult stone, often *nṣb w ḏbḥ*), *nfs(t)/ʾfs* (funerary monument, built not erected), *rgm* (cairn), *wgm ʿl-* and *bny ʿl-* (grief formulas), the petition set *slm / qbll / rwḥ / ġnmt* (security, reunion, relief, booty), and the blinding-curse formula *ʿwr … ʿwr* — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- **Other ANA corpora and their distinct pantheons**: **Dumaitic** — Nuhay and ʿAttarsamē together (wti 23), and Ruḍaw titled "from Chaldea" (*mkśd*); **Thamudic B** — Malkom, Kemosh, Qaws (htham 1); **Taymanitic** — Ṣalm; **Dadanitic** — Dhū Ghaybat, and the verb *ḥggw* "to make pilgrimage", and the *ẓll* rite — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- **Dadanitic** generally: probably spoken in the second half of the first millennium BCE; the script and probably the language are tied to the oasis of **Dadān (modern al-ʿUlā)** and the kingdom of **Liḥyān** in northwest Arabia; most inscriptions come from Dadān, which is why **Michael Macdonald proposed the name "Dadanitic" in 2000** (earlier "Lihyanite," then "Dedanite," the latter used by Hubert Grimme in 1932). Grammar poorly understood; 28 phonemes as in Arabic; the only ancient South Semitic script known to use *matres lectionis* — [Wikipedia, Dadanitic (archived)](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20220428163108/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dadanitic)
- **Minaean Levites at Dedan**: Minaean inscriptions attest **Levites in Wadd's temple at Dedan** — [Wikipedia, Wadd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadd)
- **The methodological contrast stated explicitly**: Al-Jallad treats these inscriptions as eyewitness evidence, against Islamic-period narratives he views as polemical and filtered; and he treats the Akhbār material as largely folkloric, with only some names reliably preserving older knowledge — [Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)
- **The corpora are freely searchable**: OCIANA covers Aramaic scripts of North Arabia, Paleo-Arabic, ANA, Dadanitic, Dumaitic, Hasaitic, Hismaic, Safaitic, Taymanitic and Thamudic, with transliteration, English translation, earlier readings, commentary, bibliography and provenance for each record; photographs and facsimiles are downloadable free. Nabataean is not included. Relaunched September 2024 at Ohio State (DLATO), succeeding the 2013–17 Oxford Khalili Research Centre version — [OCIANA](https://ociana.osu.edu/)

### Inferences
- The chronological mismatch matters and should be stated plainly: the Safaitic script is dated 3rd c. BCE – 4th c. CE, which means Al-Jallad's "nomadic religion" is **earlier** than the 5th–7th c. window, and strictly speaking we have *no* comparable first-person corpus for the nomads of the two centuries before Islam. Any use of Safaitic material for a 6th-c. setting is an extrapolation — a reasonable one, given continuity of names (Allāt, Dushara, Ruḍaw all persist into the literary record), but an extrapolation.
- The ANA corpora show **regionally distinct pantheons**, not a single Arabian religion: Nuhay and ʿAttarsamē at Dūma, Ṣalm at Taymāʾ, Dhū Ghaybat at Dadān, Allāt and Ruḍaw in the Ḥarra, Almaqah at Maʾrib, Kahl at Qaryat al-Fāw. A story that moves across Arabia should move across religions, not just across shrines of one.
- The Safaitic record's silences are informative: no priest, no divination, no pilgrimage verb (that is Dadanitic), no theology. What there *is*: sacrifice, stones, cairns, grief, and petitions for safety, rain, reunion and loot. That is the religion of people who travel, raid and bury.
