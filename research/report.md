# Building Arabia's Missing Timeline from Scratch

**The map-plus-timeline you imagined does not exist, and no one has built the timeline half at all** — no machine-readable chronology of pre-Islamic Arabia was found in any repository, gazetteer or dataset, which is a genuine absence in the field rather than a search failure. The closest tool, al-Ṯurayyā, has the right region, Arabic-script search, routes and travel-time pathfinding, but covers the 9th–10th centuries and has no time axis whatsoever ([al-Ṯurayyā](https://althurayya.github.io/)); its data is, however, CC BY 4.0 GeoJSON on GitHub, so it can serve as the spatial backbone while you author the temporal layer yourself. The chronology below is that layer, roughly 300–632 CE, with every entry tagged so you can see at a glance what rests on a dated inscription and what rests on ʿAbbāsid storytelling. The short version: **South Arabia has a real epigraphic chronology, the Lakhmid and Ghassanid courts are externally dated from about 503 onward, and everything in the interior — Basūs, Dāḥis, the poets, the Fijār, the Year of the Elephant — is back-calculated, contested by decades, and in several cases contradicts itself.** Shiʿb Jabala is dated across a fifty-year spread; the Year of the Elephant has an inscription nearly twenty years before its traditional date; *Encyclopaedia Iranica* declines to print a Lakhmid king-list at all. For your purposes this is an asset: a traveller in 560 CE who hears three incompatible accounts of when Kulayb died is behaving exactly as a person in that world did, because the tradition was oral, performed and contested from the start.

## The tool you want does not exist, but three of its four pieces do

**No project found covers 5th–7th century Arabia cartographically *and* chronologically in one interface.** The verdict is worth stating flatly because it saves you weeks of searching. What exists is a set of parts.

**al-Ṯurayyā** (الثريا) is the single most promising item: a gazetteer and geospatial model of the early Islamic world by Masoumeh Seydi and Maxim Romanov, with **over 2,000 georeferenced toponyms and almost as many georeferenced route sections**, a Dijkstra pathfinder, and a Network tab that computes settlements reachable within N days at **~30 km/day** ([al-Ṯurayyā](https://althurayya.github.io/)). Its base source is Georgette Cornu's atlas of the 9th–10th century Islamic world, so **the chronological frame sits 200–400 years after your period**, and the project itself warns that its source-to-toponym matching is fuzzy, so a 100% match does not guarantee the right place. Two features make it unusually useful anyway. It exposes a **coordinate-certainty field** per place — which tells you exactly which locations are securely fixed and which are scholarly guesses you can move for narrative convenience without embarrassment. And **its search works only in Arabic script or a strict one-to-one transliteration, with no English index**, which means your Arabic is a genuine advantage; most English-only researchers find the tool hard to use. The repository is at [github.com/althurayya/althurayya.github.io](https://github.com/althurayya/althurayya.github.io), with individual places as GeoJSON in `places/`, primary sources as JSON in `sources/`, and precomputed shortest paths in the root. **Code is Apache-2.0; the project's own datasets — place and route records, coordinates, identifiers, transliterations — are CC BY 4.0.** There is no API and no CSV: you clone and parse.

For the classical and late-antique layer, al-Ṯurayyā already **cross-references Pleiades**, the *Encyclopaedia of Islam* and Wikipedia, so Pleiades IDs are the natural join key into the whole Pelagios ecosystem ([al-Ṯurayyā](https://althurayya.github.io/)). For the inscriptional layer, **DASI** ([dasi.cnr.it](https://dasi.cnr.it/)) holds the South Arabian corpus including the Maʾrib dam texts, and **OCIANA** — now at [ociana.osu.edu](https://ociana.osu.edu/), relaunched at Ohio State in September 2024, not the old Oxford URL — holds Ancient North Arabian, with transliteration, translation, commentary and **downloadable photographs and facsimiles at publishable resolution**. One practical find worth knowing: **Tübinger Atlas des Vorderen Orients sheets have been georeferenced and appear in Princeton's geospatial catalogue** (e.g. [princeton-2f75r987h](https://maps.princeton.edu/catalog/princeton-2f75r987h)), meaning scholarly historical map sheets may be obtainable as raster layers you could drop into MapLibre as a basemap — licence terms need checking per sheet.

So the realistic architecture is: **al-Ṯurayyā's GeoJSON as the spatial base, joined on Pleiades IDs to the classical layer, with DASI and OCIANA for dated site-level evidence, and a hand-authored chronology CSV as the temporal layer, filtered by a client-side time slider.** Everything except the chronology already exists under an open licence. The chronology is the irreducible manual work — and it is the research you need to do to write the story anyway, so it is not wasted. Two caveats before you build. First, because al-Ṯurayyā is Abbasid-era, you will need to audit its toponyms against the pre-Islamic record and flag which existed in the 6th century; the `source` and certainty fields make that tractable. Second, **Wikidata is the only plausible seed for a chronology dataset and its coverage of pre-Islamic Arabia is thin**; the research produced a method sketch (a SPARQL query on `P31` battle/city/human, filtered by `P17`/`P131` or `P361` membership of Ḥimyar, the Lakhmids, Ghassanids and Kinda, bounded by `P585`/`P580`/`P582` to c. 350–650, with `P625` for the map) but **that query was never run or verified** — treat it as untested method.

## A master chronology, 300–632 CE, with the evidence graded entry by entry

The tagging scheme: **[EPI]** fixed by a dated inscription; **[EXT]** from a Greek, Syriac, Latin or Persian external source; **[TRAD]** from the later Arabic-Islamic tradition, generally not independently datable; **[RECON]** a modern scholarly reconstruction, with the scholar named where known. The distinction is not decorative. Almost every date in the interior of Arabia is a back-calculation, not a dated record, and the one near-exception among the great *ayyām* is Yawm Ḥalīma, which can be tied to a Greek-sourced battle of June 554.

Three anchoring devices generate the traditional chronology, and it helps to see them working. **Counting back from the Hijra (622)** gives Buʿāth c. 617. **Muḥammad's reported age**, itself pegged to the Year of the Elephant, gives the Fijār the 580s–590s. **Synchronism with a datable Lakhmid or Ghassanid reign** gives Dhī Qār 604–611 and pushes Shiʿb Jabala to c. 580. Only the third method touches external records, and it is therefore the only one worth trusting.

### The relative chains are more trustworthy than the absolute dates

Where the Arabic sources are genuinely strong is sequence, not year. **Four relative chains are worth more to you than the CE column.** The Najd chain runs: *Khālid ibn Jaʿfar killed → Yawm Raḥraḥān* (where ʿĀmir capture **Maʿbad ibn Zurāra**, who dies in captivity) *→ one year later, Shiʿb Jabala* (Laqīṭ killed) *→ about a year later, Yawm Dhū Najab*, where the Yarbūʿ of Tamīm take their revenge ([Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala); [almerja](https://almerja.com/more.php?idm=66589)). Al-Khāqānī's list independently dates Shiʿb Jabala to **"57 years before Islam"** and Dhū Najab to **one year after** — the only firm dates in the whole minor-days corpus.

The Basūs chain, from Ibn al-Athīr's *al-Kāmil* as served verbatim at almerja, runs through seven named days: **ʿUnayza** (at Falj, in al-Yamāma, no clear victor) → **al-Nahy** (Taghlib) → **al-Dhanāʾib** (the greatest encounter; Taghlib) → **Wirdāt/Wāridāt** (Hammām ibn Murra killed; Taghlib) → **al-Quṣaybāt** (Taghlib) → **Quḍa, also called Taḥlāq al-Limam** (Bakr, under al-Ḥārith ibn ʿUbād) → **al-Fuṣayl** (Bakr) ([almerja](https://mail.almerja.com/more.php?idm=66635)). The structural hinge in both Ibn al-Athīr and al-Maʿrifa is the killing of **Bujayr**, whom al-Ḥārith sent to al-Muhalhil and whom al-Muhalhil killed: **Taghlib wins every day up to that point and loses after it** ([al-Maʿrifa](https://www.marefa.org/حرب_البسوس)).

The Dāḥis sequence comes from al-ʿIqd al-Farīd's volume-6 index, the only source giving an ordered day-list *with* winner and loser per day: **al-Muraqqab** (ʿAbs over Fazāra) → **Dhū Ḥisā** (Dhubyān over ʿAbs, the one clear ʿAbs defeat) → **al-Yaʿmariyya** (ʿAbs) → **al-Habāʾa** (ʿAbs) → **al-Farūq** → **Qaṭan** → **Ghadīr Qalhā** → **al-Raqam** (Ghaṭafān over Banū ʿĀmir, not an ʿAbs–Dhubyān day at all) ([shamela, al-ʿIqd al-Farīd vol. 6](https://shamela.ws/book/23789/1829)).

And the poets fall into two court generations. **Around ʿAmr ibn Hind's al-Ḥīra (554–568/9)**: Ṭarafa, al-Mutalammis, al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥilliza, ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm, with Imruʾ al-Qays slightly earlier and al-Samawʾal at Taymāʾ. **Around al-Nuʿmān III's al-Ḥīra (580–602) and the Ghassanids**: al-Nābigha, al-Aʿshā, Zuhayr, ʿAntara, ʿAdī ibn Zayd, with Labīd and al-Khansāʾ crossing into Islam. A traveller in the 560s and a traveller in the 590s meet two almost completely different casts.

## The uncertainty is not vagueness — it is specific, measurable and dramatically usable

**Shiʿb Jabala is the field's best demonstration of how bad the chronology is.** One famous battle is placed at **551, 553, 570, c. 580 (Caskel), and "under al-Nuʿmān III, 582–602" (Bosworth)** — a spread of roughly fifty years ([Battle of Shi'b Jabala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shi%27b_Jabala)). Worse, the same source's own data contradicts most of those options: it names **al-Mundhir IV (r. 575–580)** among the leaders, which fits Caskel's c. 580 but is incompatible with 551, 553, 570 and with Bosworth's placement. My recommendation is to adopt **c. 580 as your internal date, with 570 as the folk date characters cite**, and let the disagreement be audible.

**The Year of the Elephant is contested in at least five directions.** Traditional: c. 570–571, the year Islamic sources give for Muḥammad's birth and the start-point of Meccan chronology. **568 or 569**, from South Arabian archaeological finds. **552**, which **Iwona Gajda accepts as the expedition's date** on the strength of Ry 506, noting that Mecca is not named in the inscription. "One or two decades earlier than 570," per other scholars. **14 February 572**, proposed by Ibrahim Zein and Ahmad el-Wakil. And a tradition from **al-Zuhrī** placing it *before Muḥammad's father was born*, which contradicts the 570 birth-year synchronism from inside the tradition itself ([Year of the Elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_the_Elephant); [Abraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraha)). The honest position: there is **one dated Abraha campaign northward into Maʿadd, in 552, which does not mention Mecca**, Abraha's epigraphic record stops at 559/560, and the 570 synchronism was constructed to anchor a birth. A fragmentary inscription published in 2025, **Bāriqī-Robin-Nāʿiṭ 1** from Nāʿiṭ c. 50 km north of Ṣanʿāʾ, names Abraha and may read *ʾlfl* as "elephants" in line 5 — which would be the first epigraphic reference to elephants in South Arabia — but **the identification is unconfirmed, the word is damaged, and its etymology uncertain** ([Bāriqī-Robin-Nāʿiṭ 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C4%81riq%C4%AB-Robin-N%C4%81%CA%BFi%E1%B9%AD_1)).

**Imruʾ al-Qays carries a dating contradiction that cannot be resolved.** Two birth estimates circulate, **c. 496 and c. 526**, and the source states outright that historians are divided ([Imru' al-Qais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imru%27_al-Qais)). The early date makes a man in his late sixties undertake a diplomatic journey to Constantinople; the late date makes him roughly the right age for the journey but **an infant when his father was killed c. 525**, which is incompatible with an adult vengeance quest. Present this as an unresolved tension rather than picking a side. The version that is both dramatically usable and defensible: father killed c. 525, vengeance war in the 530s–540s with Bakr and Taghlib allies who desert him once they judge the revenge sufficient, refuge at **al-Ablaq near Taymāʾ** with al-Samawʾal, journey north through Ghassanid territory with a letter of introduction, Constantinople c. 561–564, death near Ankara c. 565 of illness — with the poisoned robe as **in-world rumour**, which is also what it is: the "poison dress" is an international folklore type, not a story specific to him ([Poison dress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poison_dress)).

**Encyclopaedia Iranica declines to print a Lakhmid king-list, and that refusal is a scholarly signal rather than an omission.** It says only that Lakhmid chiefs seem to have controlled al-Ḥīra from the late 4th century, and the only ruler it names with a date is al-Nuʿmān III, deposed in 602 ([Iranica, "Ḥira"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hira)). The tidy table that circulates everywhere — ʿAmr I 268–295, Imruʾ al-Qays I 295–328, and so on through fifteen reigns — derives ultimately from Ḥamza al-Iṣfahānī and similar late sources, is internally tidy and externally unverifiable before about 500, and contradicts its own text in places ([Lakhmid kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakhmid_kingdom)). Treat anything before al-Mundhir III as free narrative space, and anything from 503 onward as constrained year by year.

**Mecca is the weakest-evidenced place in the whole survey.** There is **no inscriptional, archaeological or external documentary evidence for Mecca or Quraysh before 632** in the sources consulted; everything is literary, and W. Montgomery Watt notes the pre-Islamic accounts contain a legendary element with most dating uncertain ([Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh)). The internal contradiction is a full century wide: **Hawting holds that Qusayy's children must have lived in the second half of the fifth century; Watt holds that Qusayy himself likely died in the second half of the sixth.** You therefore have essentially free rein on Meccan internal chronology before c. 600, provided you keep the sequence Qusayy → ʿAbd Manāf → Hāshim → ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib intact.

Several other spreads are worth having in hand. **Dhū Nuwās's accession is given as 517, 522, and "518 or 522" across two articles.** The **Aksumite conquest runs 525 to 543** depending on which accession you accept. The **Ghassanid al-Ḥārith's reign is 528–569 or 529–569**; his son's is 569–581 or 569–582. The **Basūs War ends in 534 or 540**. And the **Dāḥis war's conventional bracket, "c. 568–609," is Sir William Muir's Victorian reconstruction, not a source date** ([Dahis and al-Ghabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahis_and_al-Ghabra)) — label it as such in any timeline. It also carries a visible internal strain: the war's narrative involves Banū ʿAbs guarding **al-Nuʿmān III's caravans** (r. 582–602), which, if authentic, means the later phase cannot predate c. 582 and squeezes the forty years badly.

One correction that resolves a real confusion rather than creating one. **ʿAyn Ubāgh and Ḥalīma are two distinct battles a generation apart, not a conflation.** Ibn al-Athīr's table of contents lists them as consecutive separate entries, and the titles make the difference explicit: **ʿAyn Ubāgh is the killing of al-Mundhir ibn Māʾ al-Samāʾ; Marj Ḥalīma is the killing of al-Mundhir ibn al-Mundhir ibn Māʾ al-Samāʾ — the son** ([shamela, al-Kāmil contents](https://shamela.ws/book/21712/479)). Two battles, two generations, the same dynasty, the same Ghassanid adversary al-Ḥārith al-Aʿraj. The confusion belongs to the modern secondary literature, not to the Arabic primary sources. ʿAyn Ubāgh is also the better scene: al-Ḥārith proposed that each side send out **a son to fight in single combat, and when the sons were exhausted the two old men would come out themselves**; al-Mundhir sent out one of his champions pretending to be his son; a defector told al-Ḥārith of the deception; al-Mundhir was killed, and al-Ḥārith **loaded his two dead sons on a camel with al-Mundhir's corpse on top**, which is the origin of a proverb about the top-load above the two panniers ([almerja](https://almerja.com/aklam/indexv.php?id=7949)).

### Four smaller premises the research corrected

The book you are reading, **شخصيات حية من الأغاني, is by محمد المنسي قنديل** (Muḥammad al-Mansī Qandīl), one of Egypt's best-known novelists ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/23914485)). **Al-Nuʿmān III was not killed before Dhī Qār**: he was deposed in 602, seized at Ctesiphon, imprisoned at Khānaqīn, and died of plague shortly before the rise of Islam — the battle chains to the *aftermath* of his fall, which is why 602 is the hard lower bound and why the tradition's upper figure of 624 is untenable ([Battle of Dhi Qar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dhi_Qar)). **Zarqāʾ al-Yamāma's sight-range is given as a week, not three days**, and her story's weak sourcing should be noted: the invader is identified as Ḥassān ibn Tubbaʿ Asʿad Abī Karib, the enemy advanced **hidden behind trees they carried**, she warned that the trees were moving, her people thought she was going mad, and after the camp's men were killed the invaders **tore out her eyes and crucified her** ([Zarqa al Yamama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarqa_al_Yamama)). And **the she-camel of al-Basūs is a conflation of two source versions**: Ibn al-Athīr has Kulayb kill the camel **Sarāb**, belonging to **Saʿd ibn Shumays al-Jarmī** and sheltered among Jassās's herd by al-Basūs, Jassās's maternal aunt; al-Maʿrifa gives the camel no name, makes it **al-Basūs's own**, and has Kulayb shoot its **calf (fuṣayl)** first and then the camel's udder ([almerja](https://mail.almerja.com/more.php?idm=66635); [al-Maʿrifa](https://www.marefa.org/حرب_البسوس)). The war's last day is named **al-Fuṣayl**, which is either a beautiful structural irony or evidence that the trigger-story grew backward out of the day-name — the note flags this link as inference, not a sourced claim.

Two things the researchers could *not* verify and which you should not build a scene on without checking: **the link from Harim ibn Sinān to Zuhayr's Muʿallaqa and the Dāḥis peace is unconfirmed** — every peace account found credits **al-Ḥārith ibn ʿAwf al-Murrī** alone, and Harim's father **Sinān** appears only as a failed *war* coalition-builder, on the opposite side of the matter ([almerja](https://almerja.com/more.php?idm=66592)). And **al-Maydānī's chapter of 132 pre-Islamic battle-days could not be confirmed**; the figure is unverified and no ayyām chapter of *Majmaʿ al-Amthāl* was reached.

## What is free, what is paywalled, and the specific IDs that were verified

There is a sharp and useful asymmetry here: **the Arabic reference literature and the deep Byzantine-frontier scholarship are free; the modern English syntheses are paywalled.** As a native Arabic reader you are unusually well placed to research this period cheaply, because the expensive books are largely English summaries of material you can read in the original.

**Irfan Shahîd's entire *Byzantium and the Arabs* series — seven volumes, the most detailed narrative account of the Arabs of the Roman/Byzantine frontier in the 4th–6th centuries — is genuinely free**, uploaded to archive.org by Dumbarton Oaks itself under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. Confirmed for Volume 2 Part 2 (*Economic, Social, and Cultural History*): the [item page](https://archive.org/details/byzantium-and-the-arabs-in-the-sixth-century-v-2-p-2) offers a 4.0 MB PDF plus EPUB, uploaded 19 February 2021, with the publisher-prepared [direct PDF](https://ia801805.us.archive.org/8/items/byzantium-and-the-arabs-in-the-sixth-century-v-2-p-2/Shahid_Byzantium-and-the-Arabs-in-the-Sixth-Century_V2P2_WEB.pdf). The other six were *not* individually verified as free — the inference that they are in the same batch is reasonable but unchecked, so verify per volume. The full list is on the [Dumbarton Oaks bookstore](https://books.doaks.org/taxonomy/term/53). For your purposes **Vol. 2 Part 1 (*Toponymy, Monuments, Historical Geography, and Frontier Studies*) is the volume for building a map, and Vol. 2 Part 2 is the volume for art direction and daily-life detail.** Mirrors exist at the National Library of Armenia's DSpace, including [the fourth-century volume](https://dspace.nla.am/handle/123456789/11769).

**Jawād ʿAlī, *al-Mufaṣṣal fī tārīkh al-ʿArab qabl al-Islām* (المفصل في تاريخ العرب قبل الإسلام), is confirmed free and the edition question is settled: 20 parts, c. 7,571 pages, Dār al-Sāqī, 4th edition, 1422 AH / 2001 CE.** It is fully readable with print-matching pagination at **[shamela.ws/book/7299](https://shamela.ws/book/7299)** and at [ketabonline.com/ar/books/5566](https://ketabonline.com/ar/books/5566). A 10-volume PDF scan (files Mofassal01–Mofassal10, 300 PPI with OCR) is at [archive.org/details/mofassal-06](https://archive.org/details/mofassal-06/Mofassal01/). Be warned that **Shamela's table of contents lists only volume labels with no subject descriptions**, so there is no quick topical index — you will search within volumes. Earlier Baghdad editions exist in different volume counts, which is why the "how many volumes" question gets inconsistent answers; the 20-part Sāqī edition is what the digital copies reproduce.

**Ahmad Al-Jallad's 2022 book on the religion and rituals of the pre-Islamic nomads is fully open access** — the single most valuable free resource for authentic religion, because it works from Safaitic inscriptions as eyewitness evidence rather than from later literary narrative. CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, 164 pages: **[OAPEN PDF](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)**. His academia.edu profile is explicitly titled for pre-print and open-access books, making him the best free source of current scholarship in the field: [osu.academia.edu/AhmadAlJallad](https://osu.academia.edu/AhmadAlJallad).

For the idols, **Nabih Amin Faris's 1952 translation of Ibn al-Kalbī's *Kitāb al-Aṣnām* (The Book of Idols) is free and public domain** — [full text](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt), [PDF](https://archive.org/download/KitabAlAsnam/Allat.pdf). The Arabic is at [shamela.ws/book/6513](https://shamela.ws/book/6513) (Zaki ed., 4th ed. 2000, 64 pp.) and on [Arabic Wikisource](https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/كتاب_الأصنام). Read it with the caveat attached: it is a **composite work surviving in a single manuscript**, bought at auction in Damascus, transmitted through Abū Bakr al-Jawharī (d. 944–45) and grown over centuries. **Many historians do not consider it reliable**; Robin argues it exaggerates the spread of ritual stones and statues, its genealogies do not match the epigraphic evidence, and **only the eight deities named in the Qurʾān are attested in pre-Islamic inscriptions** ([Kitab al-Asnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Asnam)). Wellhausen's foundational 1897 synthesis, which shaped a century of handbooks, was built on **Yāqūt's quotations of this text before the manuscript was even found**.

For the Muʿallaqāt, **the single most useful free resource is Ithra's modern English translation with parallel Arabic text**: [ithra.com/files/6516/0984/6883/Al_Muallaqat.pdf](https://www.ithra.com/files/6516/0984/6883/Al_Muallaqat.pdf). Behind it sit the public-domain translations: Sir William Jones (1782), [F. E. Johnson (1893)](https://archive.org/details/alsabalmuallaqat00johnrich/), the [Blunts (1903)](https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/001229726), [Clouston (1881)](https://archive.org/details/arabianpoetryfo00clougoog/), and [Lyall's *Translations of Ancient Arabian Poetry* (1930)](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.50391/); Lyall's edition of [ʿAbīd ibn al-Abraṣ's dīwān (1913)](https://archive.org/details/diwansofabidibna21abiduoft/) is directly relevant, since ʿAbīd was an Asad contemporary of Imruʾ al-Qays — a poet of the tribe that killed Ḥujr.

The hard-won Arabic-source IDs are these, and they are worth keeping because they were difficult to find. **shamela.ws page URLs are pure ASCII and always fetch.** Book **23789 = al-ʿIqd al-Farīd**, with volume 6 as the *ayyām* volume: the index is at [page 1829](https://shamela.ws/book/23789/1829), and narrative pages run at least 1819 (Raḥraḥān), 1820 (Shiʿb Jabala), 1833–1834 (al-Habāʾa), 1891 (al-Ṣafqa / al-Kilāb II), 1908 (Khazāz) — **a chapter-head page number returns only the contents sidebar, so increment by one or two to reach the body.** Book **21712 = al-Kāmil ed. Tadmurī**, *ayyām* chapter from [page 449](https://shamela.ws/book/21712/449), with ʿAyn Ubāgh at book-page 483 and Marj Ḥalīma at 484. Book **10283 = Nihāyat al-Arab** (the Dāḥis chapter at [vol. 15 p. 357](https://shamela.ws/book/10283/5203)); book **12929 = Majmaʿ al-Amthāl**; book **11084 = Tārīkh al-ʿArab al-Qadīm** ([page 329](https://shamela.ws/book/11084/329) looks like the most promising lead for the four obscure days nobody could locate). **almerja.com serves long verbatim extracts of Ibn al-Athīr's *ayyām* chapter** at `more.php?idm=N` and `reading.php?idm=N`; confirmed ids are **66589** (a 111-entry composite index of al-Qalqashandī's 64 days plus al-Khāqānī's 47, each tagged with tribes and usually a victor), **66592** (Dāḥis), **66635** (Basūs), **66646** (Raḥraḥān), **66664** (al-Kilāb I), plus `aklam/indexv.php?id=7949` for ʿAyn Ubāgh. **The ids cluster in the 665xx–666xx range and walking them would almost certainly surface the remaining days.** Note that `mail.almerja.com` and `mail.almerja.net` mirror the same ids and sometimes return *different text*, so fetch both.

The modern Arabic compilation **أيام العرب في الجاهلية by جاد المولى، البجاوي and أبو الفضل إبراهيم** is free in at least five digitisations; **the one to cite is the NYU Arabic Collections Online copy**, [nyu_aco001377](https://sites.dlib.nyu.edu/viewer/books/nyu_aco001377/1?embed=1&lang=ar), since the Archive copies carry no edition metadata. Two Internet Archive copies: [one](https://archive.org/details/20200908_20200908_0556), [two](https://archive.org/details/20200829_20200829_1936). Publishers attested: **دار الجيل** and **المكتبة العصرية**; the publication year and volume count were not established.

One resource deserves a specific warning for a commercial project. **OpenITI** is the field's machine-readable corpus — version 2025.1.9, published 30 December 2025, **5.9 GB**, with a separate **12.1 MB metadata TSV** so you can query the catalogue without unzipping the archive, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.17767721 ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/17767721)). **Its licence is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, and the non-commercial clause means the corpus cannot be redistributed inside a commercially sold game.** Using it to research one is fine; shipping extracted text is not, without checking per-text provenance. Since Maxim Romanov co-authors both OpenITI and al-Ṯurayyā, the corpus and the gazetteer are designed to interoperate — the natural text-plus-place toolchain for exactly your project. The practical workflow: pull the 12 MB TSV first, confirm which classical texts are in the corpus, and only then decide whether the 5.9 GB download is worth it. One more caveat on Arabic digital libraries: **Shamela's search engine is documented as unreliable** — diacritics, orthographic variants and indexing produce false negatives, so **a failed search there does not mean the word is absent from the text** ([The Digital Orientalist](https://digitalorientalist.com/2018/12/01/never-trust-al-shamila-search-results%e2%98%9d%ef%b8%8f)). Treat Shamela as a reading library and do serious searching against a local corpus with your own regex.

What is paywalled: **Greg Fisher's *Arabs and Empires before Islam* (OUP 2015)**, the sourcebook that gathers translated Greek, Latin, Syriac and South Arabian texts in one place with commentary — not open access, institutional login or interlibrary loan ([OUP](https://academic.oup.com/book/12636)). **Peter Webb's *Imagining the Arabs* (EUP 2016)**, whose revisionist argument — that "Arab" as a shared identity was substantially a product of the Islamic period — is worth knowing before you commit to characters who think of themselves as Arabs ([publisher](https://edinburghuniversitypress.com/book-imagining-the-arabs.html)); a free review is at [AJIS](https://www.ajis.org/index.php/ajiss/article/view/865). **The History of al-Ṭabarī, Vol. 5: The Sāsānids, the Byzantines, the Lakhmids, and Yemen**, translated and annotated by C. E. Bosworth, 458 pp., SUNY Press 1999, ISBN 0791443566 — **the right volume and the most directly useful single English primary-source translation for your setting**, purchase or library ([publisher](https://www.sunypress.edu/isbn/9780791443552)). And **Brill's *Encyclopaedia of Islam* 2nd/3rd editions remain the single most valuable unconsulted resource across every one of the eight research files** — the entries on Ayyām al-ʿArab, Basūs, Dāḥis, Dhū Ḳār, Fidjār, Ghūl, Djinn, Kāhin and Anwāʾ would settle a dozen open questions.

Finally, on al-Aghānī, which you are already reading: **there is no complete European-language translation**, which the research confirms as a negative finding from *Encyclopaedia Iranica*'s silence rather than an explicit denial ([Iranica, "Ketāb al-aḡānī"](https://mail.iranicaonline.org/articles/ketab-al-agani)). The standard edition is **24 volumes, over 9,000 pages, Cairo 1927–1974** — begun by Dār al-Kutub for volumes I–XVI and completed by al-Hayʾa al-ʿĀmma for XVII–XXIV, 1970–74; the two are halves of one project, not rival editions. **Because al-Aghānī has no translation and is 24 volumes of exactly the anecdotal, personal, poetic material a character-driven visual novel needs, your Arabic is not merely convenient — it gives you access to the richest source for the period that English-language writers essentially cannot use.** Almost no existing fiction in English draws on it.

## The authentic supernatural layer: territory, not taxonomy

**The single most useful corrective to the "Arabian Nights" register is that in the 5th–7th century the supernatural was primarily territorial, not taxonomic.** North of the Hijaz, including Palmyra and Baalbek, **"jinnī" and "ilāh" (god) were often used interchangeably** ([Jinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)). Muqātil ibn Sulaymān records that **Banū Ḥanīfa travellers sought refuge with a valley's "master" (sayyid) of jinn** — and a tradition attributed to ʿIkrima describes jinn **afflicting** people who did so, meaning the practice carried risk. Qurʾān 72:6 condemns exactly this, travellers seeking refuge with jinn in fear — **which makes the Qurʾān a contemporary witness to the belief it attacks, and therefore the most securely in-period source you have.** A traveller's question in this world was not "what species is this?" but *whose ground am I on, and have I paid?*

The deities genuinely in the inscriptions reinforce this. In the Safaitic corpus **Allāt is the most widely invoked deity and Ruḍaw the second**; also attested are **Baʿal-Samīn** the storm god, **Dushara**, the tutelary fortune-gods **Gadd-Ḍayf** and **Gadd-ʿAwīdh**, and **Shayʿ-ha-Qawm**, protector of caravans, described in inscriptions as one who drinks no wine and builds no home ([Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf); [List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pre-Islamic_Arabian_deities)). **Hubal is essentially absent, appearing in exactly one Nabataean tomb inscription**, and **none of Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq or Nasr is epigraphically attested at all.** And the pantheons are **regionally distinct, not one Arabian religion**: Nuhay and ʿAttarsamē at Dūma, Ṣalm at Taymāʾ, Dhū Ghaybat at Dadān, Allāt and Ruḍaw in the Ḥarra, Almaqah at Maʾrib, Kahl at Qaryat al-Fāw. **A story that moves across Arabia should move across religions, not just across shrines of one.** One chronological caveat to carry: the Safaitic script is dated 3rd c. BCE – 4th c. CE, so this nomadic religion is *earlier* than your window, and using it for a 6th-century setting is an extrapolation — a reasonable one, given that Allāt, Dushara and Ruḍaw all persist into the literary record, but an extrapolation.

**The most valuable single text for your authenticity question is Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2220**, whose chapter heading names five or six pre-Islamic supernatural beliefs in one breath in order to abolish them: **لا عدوى ولا طِيَرَةَ ولا هَامَةَ ولا صَفَرَ** — no *ʿadwā* (contagion by contact), no *ṭiyara* (bird-omens), no *hāma*, no *ṣafar*, no *nawāʾ*, and no **ghūl** ([Sahih Muslim 2220a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2220a)). Anything on that list is **certified live belief in your window**, because the denial is the evidence. The *hāma* (هامة) is the most striking item: the soul or blood of a murdered man rising from his skull as a bird — or in another version a worm leaving the skull — circling the tomb and crying to be given drink, and not resting until the murderer's blood slakes it. The sources genuinely conflict on whether it was an owl, a bird of prey, or a worm, and this should not be smoothed over; the pre-Islamic status rests on the hadith denial rather than on a dated poem, since Lane (1843) and Hulme (1886) are Victorian compilers ([Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)). **Why it matters for your story: the hāma is the mythic engine of the blood feud.** If an unavenged killing literally produces a thirsty thing that will not stop crying, then vengeance is not merely honour — it is laying a ghost. That converts the entire *ayyām* corpus from social obligation into supernatural obligation, and it is period-authentic. Note too that the Islamic replacement is **substitution rather than abolition**: a hadith has martyrs' souls living in green birds in Paradise. The soul-bird survives, reassigned from the murdered to the martyr, and at peace instead of thirsty.

**The ghūl is the best-attested genuinely pre-Islamic Arabian monster, and it is a kind of jinn, not a separate species.** Its defining trait is **not corpse-eating — that is Antoine Galland's 18th-century Europeanisation — but predatory impersonation: it takes the form of the person it most recently ate, and it lures travellers with fires in the night** ([Ghoul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghoul); [Al-Rawi 2009](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~culturalanalysis/volume8/pdf/ghouls.pdf)). Early Arabic treated *ghūl* as feminine. Al-Jāḥiẓ supplies a concrete folk combat-rule of the kind that makes an invented world feel researched: **a single sword blow kills the siʿlwa, while two strikes prolong its life** — and he also offers a rationalist account, that fear and solitude in the desert cause hallucinations. Al-Masʿūdī preserves a creation myth distributing the beings by terrain: **the ghūl in the wilderness, the cat-like *quṭrub*, the devils in the seas, the *siʿlwa* in waste places.** In the folk tales a "Mother Ghoul" appears to men as a long-lost female relative or an unassuming old woman, and **female relatives often see through the illusion while men survive only if they believe them** — undated, so folk-tale rather than 6th-century, but a ready-made scene structure.

**Drop these as creature-categories: ʿifrīt and mārid.** *ʿIfrīt* appears **once in the Qurʾān (27:38–40) and as an epithet, not a species-name**, always in the phrase "among the jinn," and **does not appear in pre-Islamic poetry** ([Ifrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifrit)). Its systematised, ranked use is medieval. If you want it at all, use it in its folk sense as **the ghost of the murdered** — in Egyptian belief a person who dies naturally produces no malevolent ifrit, but a murdered person may produce a vengeful one drawn to the victim's blood, and driving an unused nail into the blood prevents it. That rhymes exactly with the *hāma*, which suggests a single underlying Arabian intuition: **violent death leaves something behind that is owed something.** Also drop "genie," which entered English through French via 18th-century translations of the *Nights*. Neither of the two most "Arabian Nights" images in the popular imagination is Arabian; dropping them is a gain, not a loss.

**The seer and the half-being are the same image, and this is one of the best-sourced motifs in the whole corpus.** **Saṭīḥ (سطيح) had almost no bones and was compared to a doormat, which his companions folded up when travelling with him. Shiqq (شقّ) was a Cyclops with half a face, half a head and half a body. Their names allude to these deformities** ([Savran](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition)). El-Zein records further seer-legends: one a half-person with one leg, one arm and one eye; another whose **body rolled up like a mat when he received communication from his jinnī, and whose only bone was his skull, with his face on his chest** ([Mohamed](https://philarchive.org/archive/MOATJA)). And the *shiqq*/*nasnās* **monster** is described identically — one eye, one hand, one leg, the word meaning "half." The one who sees the unseen is himself not quite a whole man. But you need Savran's verdict before you build on Saṭīḥ and Shiqq as characters: **he reads them as early-ʿAbbāsid literary constructions, "connectors" inserted to link ancient Arabia, the Sasanians and the rise of Islam into one master narrative, and legitimising agents presenting Jāhiliyya Arabs as carrying a hidden monotheist current.** Using them as 5th–6th century figures reproduces an ʿAbbāsid ideology rather than a 6th-century reality — which need not stop you, but means you are writing a tradition rather than a fact, and the game could make that its subject.

**The jinn/kāhin/meteor complex gives you a dateable supernatural event.** Qurʾān 72:8–9 says jinn and devils **once spied at heaven's gates to get news for soothsayers and are now driven off by meteors**. That simultaneously supplies the *mechanism* of soothsaying and declares it defunct. For a story set across the religious change, this is enormous: a world in which **divination used to work**, and a generation whose oracles have gone silent because the sky is now guarded. The poetic side of the same belief is equally usable. **Early *shuʿarāʾ* and *kuhhān* were not clearly distinguished**, and the late pre-Islamic poet was seen as an artist whose imagination was linked to unseen powers rather than as someone fully possessed; **Yosefi's terminological point is that *shayṭān* in the poetic sense means "daemon," not "devil"** ([Yosefi 2018](https://www.academia.edu/44281190/The_jinn_of_poetry_in_contemporary_Yemen_and_ancient_Arabia)). **Al-Aʿshā credited a jinnī named Misḥal (مسحل)** with inspiring his poetry. The full roster of named jinn-familiars paired with named poets — Lāfiẓ ibn Lāḥiẓ for Imruʾ al-Qays, Jālid ibn Ẓill for ʿAntara, Hādhir ibn Māhir for al-Nābigha, and the rest, all from **Wādī ʿAbqar (وادي عبقر)**, a deep valley in Najd where anyone who spends a night may be visited by a jinn poet who teaches him verse — survives only through al-Maʿrifa citing Abū Zayd al-Qurashī, **the names vary between sources, and the list includes the Umayyad-era poet al-Kumayt, so it was still growing in the Islamic period** ([Marefa](https://www.marefa.org/%D9%88%D8%A7%D8%AF%D9%8A_%D8%B9%D8%A8%D9%82%D8%B1)). Use it in-fiction as something a character *cites* — a poet boasting of his line of inspiration — which is exactly how it reaches us. One living parallel is worth stealing: in contemporary Yemen the **hājis (هاجس)** is a *male* muse who supplies wording when the poet asks, while the **ḥalīla (حليلة)** is a *female* muse of imagination who **may drive a poet mad by overstimulating his mind**. The split gives a poet-character two possible relationships to inspiration instead of one; Yosefi does not claim direct continuity, so treat it as borrowed rather than reconstructed.

**The ṣaʿālīk are your best answer to "how do I do Arabian supernatural without Arabian Nights."** The uncanny in their poetry is not a creature-roster but a *condition*: the outlaw who has become slightly inhuman, kin to wolves and hyenas, at home where humans are not. **Al-Shanfarā died c. 525 and ʿAntara c. 608, both inside your window**, so this is in-period, and it is literary rather than folkloric, carrying the prestige of poetry instead of the taint of superstition. Al-Shanfarā's death is a complete revenge-myth in itself: **he had vowed to kill 100 men of the Azd and died having killed 99; later an Azdī man kicked his skull, took a splinter, and died of the infection — which the tradition treats as completing the vow** ([al-Shanfara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shanfara)). That is structurally the same idea as the hāma: the unfinished claim of the dead acts in the world. **Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's ghūl-fight is attested, in al-Aghānī, with the ghūl in the shape of a ram** — but note that **al-Jāḥiẓ denounced him for boasting about the victory and al-Maʿarrī treated the story as contrary to reason**, which lets you stage period-appropriate scepticism as a position a character can hold. Two cautions the research insisted on: the famous **wolf-kinship passage of the *Lāmiyyat al-ʿArab* was not verified in any accessible source** (only the hyena in his burial verses was), and the *Lāmiyya*'s attribution **has been disputed since medieval times** — al-Tibrīzī credited Khalaf al-Aḥmar, a known 8th-century forger of "ancient" verse, and Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih credited a nephew. The two poems that made these figures famous are precisely the ones with the strongest forgery attributions. **Their personae are more securely medieval-literary than their biographies are historical.**

## The material that is unusually vivid, and why each piece earns its place

**Maysir (ميسر) is the best single scene-engine in the entire research set.** Ten unfeathered, headless arrows; ten players; **a camel bought on credit** and slaughtered, the carcass divided into ten portions. Each arrow has a name and notches matching its share: **al-Fadhdh (1), al-Tawʾam (2), al-Raqīb (3), al-Ḥils (4), al-Nāfis (5), al-Musbil (6), al-Muʿallā (7, the highest)**; the eighth, ninth and tenth — **al-Safīḥ, al-Manīḥ, al-Waghd** — win nothing. The arrows go into a quiver-like *ribāba* (ربابة), are shuffled, and a **ḥakam (حكم, judge)** draws them one at a time in each player's name. **Those who drew blanks paid the price of the camel, and the meat was given to the poor. A man who refused to play was reproached and called *baram* (بَرَم)** ([Lane's Lexicon, ميسر](https://www.laneslexicon.com/word/ميسر/n42474)). It is a gambling game, a butchery, a public performance with a named officiant, a charity mechanism and a test of masculine honour at once, every prop has a name, buying the camel on credit means the game settles afterwards in obligation rather than cash, and the Qurʾānic prohibition gives it a built-in historical clock.

**Camel watering intervals are a plot clock built into the landscape.** *Ẓimʾ* (ظمء) is the interval between two waterings, and the named intervals are keyed to the stars: **ghibb (غِبّ)**, the shortest, used in the hottest season — water every second day; **ribʿ (رِبْع)** — *after the rising of Suhayl (Canopus)* the interval lengthens to watering on the fourth day; then **khims, sids,** and **ʿishr (عِشْر)**, the longest ([Lane's Lexicon, ظمء](https://www.laneslexicon.com/word/ظمء)). Combined with the **marḥala (مرحلة)**, a day's march generally reckoned at **40–45 km** ([Marhala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marhala_(unit))), you can price any journey in days without inventing numbers — Mecca to Medina is ten to twelve stages — and **the same route is a different problem in high summer than after Canopus rises.** The Safaitic nomads' own four-season vocabulary is the right seasonal frame: *s2ty* (شتي, winter), *dṯʔ* (the later rains), *ṣyf* (صيف, early summer), *qyẓ* (قيظ, the dry season), and **Safaitic texts time rituals to the sun's position in particular *anwāʾ***, which is contemporary epigraphic confirmation rather than later report ([Calendars in pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendars_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia)).

**The Maʾrib dam is visibly failing across exactly your century, and the real story is better than the legend.** The epigraphic record gives **breaches in 449, 450, 542 and 548, repairs in 450 and 557, and then an unrepaired overtopping c. 570–575**. What the inscriptions show is not a single catastrophic *Sayl al-ʿArim* but a **600-year maintenance cycle terminating in the collapse of the political capacity to repair** — which coincides precisely with the Aksumite-to-Sasanian transition. The legend supplies the explanation a 6th-century person would reach for: **a soothsayer king, ʿImrān, predicted the final breach, and later the king's wife did too; and rats gnawed at the dam with their teeth and scratched it with their nails** ([Marib Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam)). You can stage both at once with complete honesty. Note that the "up to 50,000 migrants" figure is marked citation-needed and should not be reported as fact. **Ṭarīfa/Turayfa the kāhina** is independently attested as the dam-prophetess and as the source of Saṭīḥ and Shiqq's gift of soothsaying ([Savran](https://www.academia.edu/63286164/The_Two_Old_Soothsayers_in_the_Islamic_Historiographical_Tradition)) — but her relation to ʿAmr Muzayqiyāʾ could not be confirmed, and that is a high-value loose end.

**The recurring ignored-woman-warner is the single most persistent narrative shape in the whole corpus** — Zarqāʾ al-Yamāma whose people thought her mad; **the unnamed woman of Jadīs who warned of invasion and was ignored**; the woman at Raḥraḥān who **saw the armies and told Zurāra who the commanders were**, which is the one warning that *was* heeded; Ṭarīfa predicting the dam; the Maʾrib king's wife. It is worth treating as a structural pattern rather than four separate anecdotes.

**Al-Ḥīra killed three of its own poets in forty-five years.** Ṭarafa executed c. 569 after carrying his own sealed death-warrant to Baḥrayn, having **refused to break the royal seal while his uncle al-Mutalammis had his letter read by a boy and threw it in a stream**; the king **ʿAmr ibn Hind murdered at dinner by the poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm** in 568/9; and **ʿAdī ibn Zayd imprisoned and strangled c. 600** by al-Nuʿmān III. That cluster of violence around a single court is the strongest dramatic material in the entire dossier and it is better attested than anything about the ṣaʿālīk. Al-Ḥīra is also the one court with attested salaried positions, Persian-language administration, Christian clergy, children from nearby villages coming to learn reading and writing, a local **Hīrī script considered a precursor to the Arabic script**, and a documented route to Constantinople ([al-Hirah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hirah)). **ʿAdī ibn Zayd is the single most useful insider character available**: born c. 550 at al-Ḥīra into an ʿIbādī Christian family, educated at the Sasanian court at al-Madāʾin, speaking Arabic and Persian and probably Syriac, serving as *dabīr* for Arab affairs, with a documented 579 journey to Constantinople that parallels Imruʾ al-Qays's legendary one, and a death datable to within a few years.

**Qaryat al-Fāw gives you the only painted human figures from the Arabian interior, and the palette is usable as a colour script.** The *mrzḥ* (مرزح, ritual banqueting hall) in the Temple of Shams has painted walls showing **reclining banqueters, vine scrolls, cupids, amphorae, chariots and figures in Roman dress**, in a style paralleling Fayyūm mummy portraits, with pink rose garlands suggesting a funerary register; the best-known fragment shows **camel drivers**, and another a tower house with its inhabitants. **The palette is mainly white, ochre, red and black, with rare blue** ([Qaryat al-Faw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-Faw)) — so blue reads as expensive. The site also contains a documented oasis collapse inside your period: planned canals, cisterns, a vast irrigation network and **more than seventeen wells**, a city that lasted nearly a millennium and was **abruptly abandoned around the 5th century CE after water resources were irreversibly depleted** ([UNESCO 1712](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1712)). For a story opening around 400–500, that is a great city dying within living memory. Two cautions: the **Temple of Shams at Fāw holds the easternmost epigraphically attested *mrzḥ***, showing Hellenised cult practice reaching central Arabia — and the identification of Fāw as the capital of a 450–550 Kindite kingdom is almost certainly a conflation, since the site article has **the Kinda leaving around 300 AD** and the place abandoned shortly after.

**Hinat of Ḥegra is a real pre-Islamic woman you can look at.** *Hinat daughter of Wahbu* (هنات بنت وهبو) is named in a tomb inscription carved c. 60–61 CE; skeletal analysis gives her **age 40–50, height about 1.6 m, medium social status**, with chronic joint disease and dental infection. Her tomb in Jabal Aḥmar preserved **bones, skin, hair, textiles, leather and plant matter**, and may have held up to 80 burials. The facial reconstruction dressed her in **artisan-woven linen matched to textile fragments from the Ḥegra tombs and replica earrings copied from excavated Ḥegra jewellery** — while the article is candid that eye colour, skin tone and wrinkles were interpretive ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/paid-content-meeting-hinat)). She is six centuries before your setting, but she is the strongest basis available for a woman's physical register and clothing, and a far better anchor for characterisation than the Islamic-era marriage typology.

Two further items worth having. The **dawār** is the most evocative ritual detail in the corpus and it is corroborated by independent evidence: Arabs circumambulated the Kaʿba, and **those without a temple set up a stone and circled it the same way, and this was called *dawār*; a traveller might choose the finest of four stones as a god and use the other three to prop his cooking pot** ([Faris trans.](https://archive.org/stream/KitabAlAsnam/Allat_djvu.txt)). The Safaitic *nṣb* practice corroborates the substance — a worshipper erects a stone and sacrifices, **with no priest attested anywhere in the roughly 91 sacrifice texts** ([Al-Jallad 2022](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/54655/1/9789004504271.pdf)). The nomadic religion really was portable and un-institutional. And the **Safaitic prayer grammar is effectively a template for writing authentic invocations**: the petition is semantically bound to the narrative that precedes it — longing yields a request for reunion (*qbll*), awaiting the rains yields rain or relief (*rwḥ*) addressed to Baʿal-Samīn, raiding yields booty (*ġnmt*) and security (*slm*) — with the standard blinding-curse against anyone who effaces the writing. The Safaitic record's silences are as informative as its contents: no priest, no divination, no theology, no resurrection. What there is: sacrifice, stones, cairns, grief, and petitions for safety, rain, reunion and loot. **Al-Jallad has a section titled "Invoking the Names of the Dead," and the thousands of graffiti that simply record a name suggest the real Arabian technology of survival after death was being named and remembered in writing, not an afterlife.**

Two tone corrections before you write. **Routine female infanticide would be dramatising a 9th-century polemic rather than a 6th-century practice**: Lindstedt argues **no pre-Islamic source in any language evidences *waʾd al-banāt* as a custom**, reads *yadussuhu fī al-turāb* (Q 16:59) as *abandoning* a female infant rather than burying her alive and *al-mawʾūda* (Q 81:8) as "the one trodden," and traces the accusation to **anti-Tamīm polemic after the second fitna (680–692)** ([Lindstedt](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Lindstedt-I-–-The-portrayal-of-the-pre-Islamic-.pdf)). The safe framing is an individual act in famine, treated as horrifying. And **the caravan should look like bulk leather, hides, raisins and cloth, not chests of jewels**: Crone's argument is narrower than it is usually reported — she does not deny Meccan trade, she denies that it explains the rise of Islam, on the grounds that the goods were **modest rather than luxury**, the boom too short to dissolve tribal order, and that the message succeeded in Medina while **the Meccans had to be conquered before they converted** ([Fordham/Crone](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/med/crone.asp)). One prop to delete from any tent scene: **coffee is a 16th-century import.** Keep the hearth at the centre of the guest area, the incense burner, the milking vessel, and **the host's camel saddle as a guest's backrest**; delete the coffee pots, roaster and grinder that the 20th-century ethnography supplies ([Aramco World, 1966](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/196603/the.black.tent.htm)).

## Conclusion

The deepest finding here is methodological, and it changes what the project should try to be. **The *ayyām* are oral tribal history, flexible and reshaped by performers and audiences, lacking absolute dating and offering only limited relative chronology — and poetry is the main device of contextualisation and authentication** ([Days of the Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Days_of_the_Arabs)). In this culture the proof that a battle happened *is* the verse. That means a visual novel can legitimately stage the transmission of a poem as the stakes of an episode, and it means the contradictions in the record are not noise to be cleaned up but the texture of the thing itself. The loss of **Abū ʿUbayda's two *ayyām* books — a shorter work of "75 or 150" days and a *Maqātil al-fursān* of "1200 or 1600"** — is the central source-critical fact of the field, and the instability is visible even in those numbers: the figures themselves are transmitted in two versions. Everything surviving is excerpt and quotation at several removes.

So the defensible move is to adopt one scheme as the game's internal calendar — Caskel's where available, the epigraphic dates where they exist, the relative chains where they do not — and then let characters disagree about when things happened, because that disagreement is period-accurate rather than a failure of research. The three-tier rubric the research arrived at is worth keeping on the wall: **attested** (a poem's presence in the early anthologies, a poet's tribe, a court's reign dates, externally datable events), **akhbār** (nearly every named episode — the sealed letters, the three deposits at al-Ablaq, the camel of al-Basūs, the meeting with Justinian — usable as in-world *story*, which is exactly what it was), and **later accretion** (*Sīrat ʿAntara*, the poisoned robe, the Kaʿba suspension, Quss's seven-hundred-year lifespan, the *Nights*). The sharpest single illustration is **Thamūd**: a people the Qurʾān and the pre-Islamic poets both treat as annihilated in the deep past were in fact **serving in Roman auxiliary units and dedicating a temple at Ruwāfa in 165–169 CE**, while "Thamudic" as an epigraphic label is a misnomer with no demonstrated link to the tribe ([Thamud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamud)). Your characters can walk past the Nabataean tombs at Hegra and call them the houses of a vanished race destroyed by God — and that is not your error. It is period-accurate. **The "ancient ruined civilisation" was itself a live belief of the period, held about people who had only recently stopped existing.** That is the whole project in one image, and it is yours to use.

**4. Walk the almerja id range 665xx–666xx.** The confirmed ids (66589, 66592, 66635, 66646, 66664) are clustered, and incrementing through the range would almost certainly surface the remaining days of Ibn al-Athīr's *ayyām* chapter verbatim — the cheapest large gain available. Fetch both `almerja.com` and the `mail.almerja.com`/`.net` mirrors, since they sometimes return different text. In parallel, **al-eman.com carries the complete *al-Kāmil* ayyām chapter split into per-day فصل pages** but its URLs were too long for the research proxy; in a normal browser it should open fine, and it is the richest single target.

**5. Fill in the day-narratives from al-ʿIqd al-Farīd by incrementing shamela page numbers.** **Yawm al-Muraqqab, Dhū Ḥisā, al-Yaʿmariyya, Qaṭan, Ghadīr Qalhā, al-Raqam, al-Nitʾa and al-Nisār survive only as index entries with no narrative recovered.** Their pages are reachable by walking forward from 1829 in [book 23789](https://shamela.ws/book/23789/1829). Four days were not found anywhere at all — **يوم الفراض، يوم البشر، يوم المشقر** (one line only) **and يوم أحد الجو** (probably **أوارة**) — and the most promising lead for them is [Tārīkh al-ʿArab al-Qadīm, shamela book 11084 p. 329](https://shamela.ws/book/11084/329).

**6. Use the mdmf1439 *Muʿjam al-Asāṭīr* blog (معجم الأساطير, by محمد مفرج السيد الرديني) as your own search starting point for obscure days.** Despite being a blog, it is the most *useful* modern Arabic source encountered, because it is the only one that cites Yāqūt, Ibn al-Kalbī, al-Maydānī and Jawād ʿAlī and then **adjudicates between them** — it caught Jawād ʿAlī confusing al-Sullān I with al-Sullān II, and al-Maydānī making the same error. Confirmed entries: [خزاز](http://mdmf1439.blogspot.com/2018/09/blog-post_7.html), [السلان](http://mdmf1439.blogspot.com/2018/09/blog-post_8.html), [رحرحان](http://mdmf1439.blogspot.com/2018/08/blog-post_55.html).

**7. Check al-Aghānī for three specific passages, since you have it.** **Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's ghūl poem** (al-Rawi cites the 1983 edition, p. 144, for the ram-shaped ghūl but explicitly does not describe the poem's content). **Any dealings between Imruʾ al-Qays and the Banū Ṭayyʾ**, which appear in no accessible source and, if you have seen them reported, most likely come from al-Aghānī. And the **named days of the Basūs and Dāḥis wars**, which no English-language source names at all. One more Arabic target worth the trip: **Yāqūt's *Muʿjam al-Buldān* entry on ʿAbqar**, which would be the authoritative geographical attestation for Wādī ʿAbqar — currently nothing confirms it was ever a real identifiable place beyond "a deep valley in Najd."

**8. Get one paywalled thing, and make it Brill's *Encyclopaedia of Islam*.** Across all eight research files, EI2/EI3 is the single most valuable unconsulted resource, and the entries would close the largest remaining holes in one visit: **Ayyām al-ʿArab, Basūs, Dāḥis, Dhū Ḳār, Fidjār, Ghūl, Djinn, Kāhin, Anwāʾ, Istiksām, Silāḥ.** Two runners-up worth a library card: **Fisher's *Arabs and Empires before Islam***, which collects in translation precisely the Greek, Syriac and South Arabian sources behind the **[EXT]** and **[EPI]** rows of the chronology above, and **Bosworth's al-Ṭabarī Volume 5**. One genuinely free gap-filler: the **anwāʾ and the lunar mansions remain entirely unresearched** — the 2018 dissertation *Rain Stars Set, Lunar Stations Rise* was robots-blocked, and since the Suhayl-keyed watering schedule depends on this system, it is worth chasing. Finally, two scholars whose pages were not located and who matter most for your exact period and region: **Christian Julien Robin** (CNRS) for Ḥimyar and Najrān, and **Isabel Toral-Niehoff** for al-Ḥīra, whose monograph *Al-Ḥīra: Eine arabische Kulturmetropole im spätantiken Kontext* is the standard study. **Alessia Prioletta's** [CNRS academia page](https://cnrs.academia.edu/AlessiaPrioletta) is the verified entry point into Robin's circle.
